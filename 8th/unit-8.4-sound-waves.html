Sound Waves

Pluck a guitar string and you can feel the vibration; shout across a canyon and the sound comes back. Sound needs matter to travel through, unlike the light waves of Unit 7.8. In this unit you measure, graph, and model sound as a wave, using the data habits of Unit 0: How Science Works.

Concept 1 · What is sound?

Sound is vibration travelling through matter

A vibrating object pushes and pulls on the particles around it, making areas of compression (crowded) and rarefaction (spread out) that travel outward as a longitudinal wave. The particles only jiggle back and forth; the wave moves on.

A wave is a disturbance that carries energy from one place to another without carrying the material along with it. Sound is a mechanical wave, which means it needs a medium (matter such as air, water or a solid) to travel through. When something vibrates, it pushes the nearby particles together and then lets them spread out again. The squeezed regions are compressions and the spread-out regions are rarefactions. Because the particles move back and forth along the same line the wave travels, it is called a longitudinal wave. In empty space there are no particles, so there is no sound.

Analogy

A slinky and a stadium wave

Push and pull one end of a slinky and bunches travel along it. In a stadium wave, people just stand up and sit down; the wave travels around, but nobody changes seats.

Concept 2 · Wave properties

Frequency sets pitch; amplitude sets loudness

Frequency (vibrations per second, in hertz) is heard as pitch: higher frequency, higher note. Amplitude is the wave height, heard as loudness (measured in decibels).

Two properties describe how a sound wave looks. Frequency is the number of vibrations (cycles) each second, measured in hertz (Hz); 440 Hz means 440 vibrations per second. Our ears hear frequency as pitch, how high or low a sound is. Amplitude is how big the vibration is, meaning how far particles are pushed from rest. A bigger amplitude carries more energy and is heard as a louder sound. Loudness is measured in decibels (dB), a scale where every 10 dB step is about ten times the sound energy.

Analogy

A guitar string

A short, tight string vibrates fast (high note). Pluck harder and the swing is larger (louder), but the note stays the same.

Data

How loud is that?

Decibels (dB). The scale is logarithmic, not linear: every +10 dB is ten times more sound energy, but we hear it as only roughly twice as loud. So 80 dB is not "twice" 40 dB; it is 10,000 times the energy. Over 85 dB for hours can damage hearing.

Concept 3 · How fast sound travels

Faster in solids and liquids than gases

Sound moves fastest through solids (particles close together pass the push quickly), slower in liquids, slowest in gases. It also speeds up as temperature rises.

The speed of sound is how fast the wave moves through a medium. It depends on the medium: sound passes the push from particle to particle, so it moves faster when the particles are closer together and bounce back strongly, as in solids and liquids. In air at room temperature the speed is about 343 m/s (about 770 mph), in water about 1,480 m/s, and in steel about 5,000 m/s. In warmer air the particles move faster, so sound also travels a little faster. Light is much faster still, around 300,000 km/s, which is why you see lightning before you hear thunder.

Analogy

Dominos close vs. far

Tightly packed dominos pass a tip quickly. Spread them out and the signal takes longer to reach the end.

Concept 4 · Waves meet obstacles

Echoes, absorption, and resonance

Sound waves reflect (echo), are absorbed (soft materials), or pass through. When a wave matches an object's natural frequency, resonance makes it vibrate strongly.

When a sound wave meets a surface, three things can happen. It can reflect (bounce back), which we hear as an echo if the sound comes back after a delay long enough to notice. It can be absorbed, with its energy turned into a tiny amount of heat; soft, porous materials like foam and curtains absorb well. Or it can transmit through. Every object also has natural frequencies at which it likes to vibrate. When a sound wave matches one, even a small push repeated at the right rhythm makes the object vibrate strongly. This effect is resonance, and it is how a guitar body amplifies a string.

Analogy

A ball and a pillow

Three different things happen when sound meets an object. A hard wall bounces it back (echo). A soft curtain absorbs it (no echo). A swing pushed in rhythm builds up (resonance). Where it breaks: a hard wall reflects most sound but not all; some always passes through or is absorbed.

Concept 5 · Using sound

Hearing ranges and acoustic engineering

Humans hear about 20 to 20,000 Hz. Other animals hear different ranges. Engineers use sound for ultrasound imaging, sonar, concert halls, and noise control.

Human hearing covers roughly 20 to 20,000 Hz, and the range shrinks with age. Sound below that range is infrasound and above it is ultrasound. Many animals hear differently: dogs hear higher pitches than people, and bats and dolphins use ultrasound to find objects by listening for the echo (echolocation). Engineers use the same ideas: sonar times echoes to map the sea floor, medical ultrasound builds pictures of the body, and acoustic design chooses reflecting and absorbing surfaces so rooms sound clear and noise is controlled.

Analogy

Radio stations on a dial

Each animal listens to a different set of "stations". Dogs hear high stations we miss; elephants talk on low ones we feel as rumbles.

Tutor notes: hands-on kit, misconceptions, pacing

Kit (about $8): rulers, rubber bands, a tuning fork, a slinky, tin cans and string, a bell jar or alarm in a sealed bag, a phone with a spectrum/tone app, a decibel app, cups of different water levels.

  • Concept 1: Twang a ruler off a desk at different lengths: pitch vs. length.
  • Concept 2: Hum with fingers on your throat; feel vibration.
  • Concept 3: Tin-can telephone with taut vs. loose string.
  • Concept 4: Fill glasses with different water amounts and tap/hum; chart pitch vs. volume of water.
  • Concept 5: Measure echo time in a gym or hallway and compute the speed of sound.

Common misconceptions:

  • "Sound travels in space." It needs a medium.
  • "Louder sound is faster." Speed depends on the medium, not loudness.
  • "Higher pitch means bigger amplitude." Pitch is frequency.
  • "Sound is air moving from the speaker to your ear." Air jiggles; the wave moves.

Pacing: about five 30-minute sessions. Method link: for each lab, write a prediction (hypothesis) and make a graph (Unit 0). Pitch vs. ruler length makes a clean pattern.
