Concept 1 · What is an orbit?

An orbit is falling and missing the ground

Gravity pulls a moving object toward the planet, but if it moves sideways fast enough, the ground curves away as fast as it falls. That endless fall is an orbit.

Analogy

Newton's cannonball

Fire a cannon sideways from a tall mountain: the ball lands farther with more speed. Fast enough, and it never lands.

Concept 2 · Kepler's patterns

Farther planets move slower and take longer

Orbits are ellipses. The farther from the Sun, the weaker the gravity, so the slower the speed and the longer the year. Kepler found that (period)² grows as (distance)³.

Analogy

Runners on a track

Outer lanes are longer, so a runner at the same speed needs more time per lap. Planets farther out also move slower, so their years are longer still. Where it breaks: real race lanes are staggered so every runner covers the same distance; planet orbits are not.

Concept 3 · How the Solar System formed

A collapsing cloud made a Sun and disk

About 4.6 billion years ago a cloud of gas and dust collapsed under gravity, spun faster, and flattened into a disk. The Sun formed in the middle; dust clumped into planets. Heat sorted the materials: rock and metal near the Sun, gas and ice farther out.

Analogy

Pizza dough

A spinning pizza flattens into a disk. A spinning, collapsing gas cloud flattens too, which is why planets orbit in nearly the same plane.

Concept 4 · Eight worlds

Compare the planets by the numbers

Planets differ in size, gravity, temperature, atmosphere, and year length. Only Earth has liquid surface water and life that we know of.

Analogy

Goldilocks planets

Venus is too hot, Mars too cold, Earth is "just right" for liquid water, at least partly because of its distance from the Sun. Where it breaks: distance alone does not decide habitability. Venus is hotter than its distance predicts because of its runaway greenhouse atmosphere, and Mars is cold partly because its air is thin.

Concept 5 · Space technology

Using gravity to explore

Spacecraft use rockets to reach orbit and gravity assists (swinging by planets) to gain speed without fuel. Telescopes, rovers, and probes collect data we cannot get from Earth.

Analogy

Slingshot on a merry-go-round

Jump onto a spinning merry-go-round and get flung off faster. A probe passing a moving planet steals a bit of its orbital energy.

Check yourself

Can you explain it?

A classmate says astronauts float because there is no gravity in space. Use the idea of orbits to correct them and give the evidence from the ISS data.

Show a model answer

Gravity at the ISS altitude is about 90% of what it is on the ground. The station and astronauts are in free fall, moving sideways at 7.7 km/s, so they keep falling around Earth instead of toward it. Since they all fall together, they feel weightless.

Words to know

orbit
The curved path of an object around another because of gravity.
ellipse
A stretched circle.
period
Time for one orbit.
AU
Astronomical unit: Earth–Sun distance (150 million km).
nebula
A cloud of gas and dust.
terrestrial planet
A rocky inner planet.
gas giant
A large planet of mostly hydrogen and helium.
escape velocity
Speed needed to leave a planet's gravity.
gravity assist
Using a planet's gravity to change a probe's speed.
free fall
Falling with only gravity acting.
Tutor notes: hands-on kit, misconceptions, pacing

Kit (about $10): string and a ball (orbit demo), a stretched lycra sheet or trampoline with marbles (gravity well), a 3 m roll of paper (scale model), clay for planets, a bike wheel or lazy Susan, a flashlight.

  • Concept 1: Swing a ball on a string and let go to see the straight-line path.
  • Concept 2: Roll marbles around a stretched sheet with a heavy ball in the middle.
  • Concept 3: Build a scale model: 1 AU = 10 cm, place the planets.
  • Concept 4: Calculate each planet's year from distance using T² = a³.
  • Concept 5: Make a poster comparing two planets and arguing which is more habitable.

Common misconceptions:

  • "There is no gravity in space." Gravity reaches everywhere.
  • "Orbits are circles." They are ellipses, mostly almost circular.
  • "The Sun is a planet." It is a star.
  • "Planets are in a straight line." They are scattered along their orbits.

Pacing: about five 30-minute sessions. Method link: compare model predictions with data: if T² = a³ predicts Mars's year, check it against the table (Unit 0).
