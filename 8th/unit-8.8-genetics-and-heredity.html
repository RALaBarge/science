Concept 1 · The instruction manual

DNA stores instructions in a 4-letter code

DNA is a long double-helix molecule made of four bases: A, T, C, G. A pairs with T and C pairs with G. A gene is a stretch of DNA that holds the instructions for one protein. Genes are packed into chromosomes in the nucleus.

Analogy

A library

The nucleus is a library. Each chromosome is a volume, each gene a recipe, and the four letters A, T, C, G are the alphabet.

Concept 2 · Dominant and recessive

Two copies of each gene, in different versions

You inherit one copy of each gene from each parent. Different versions are alleles. A dominant allele shows when present; a recessive allele shows only with two copies. Genotype is the alleles; phenotype is the trait.

Analogy

Two light switches

Think of two switches wired to one bulb. A dominant allele turns the bulb on by itself. A recessive allele only shows when both switches are recessive; one dominant switch overrides it. Where it breaks: real alleles rarely act like simple on/off switches. Many traits are built by several genes plus the environment.

Concept 3 · Predicting offspring

A grid of probabilities

A Punnett square shows every way parental alleles can combine. It gives probabilities, not guarantees, like flipping coins.

Analogy

Flipping two coins

Each parent hands over one allele like a coin flip. The grid lists all four coin combinations.

Concept 4 · From code to protein

Genes tell cells which proteins to build

A cell copies a gene into messenger RNA and reads it three letters at a time (codons). Each codon calls for an amino acid; chains of amino acids fold into proteins, which build and run your body.

Analogy

Reading a recipe

Three-letter words like "CAT", "HAT" are codons: change a letter and you may change the whole meaning.

Concept 5 · Copies and sex cells

Mitosis copies; meiosis shuffles

Mitosis makes two identical body cells for growth and repair. Meiosis makes four different sex cells with half the chromosomes, so offspring get a unique mix from both parents. Today we also edit and copy genes (biotechnology).

Analogy

Photocopy vs. shuffled deck

Mitosis is a photocopier. Meiosis is shuffling a deck and dealing half to each player: every hand is different.

Can you explain it?

Two brown-eyed parents (both Bb) have a blue-eyed child (bb). Use a Punnett square to explain how this is possible and give the probability for each new child.

Show a model answer

Each parent can pass B or b, so the Punnett square is BB, Bb, Bb, bb. Blue eyes need bb, which is 1 of 4 outcomes, a 25% chance for each child. The parents carry the recessive allele without showing it.

Tutor notes: hands-on kit, misconceptions, pacing

Kit (about $8): two colors of beads or coins, paper bags, colored pencils, strawberry DNA extraction kit (dish soap, salt, rubbing alcohol, coffee filter), pipe cleaners, a deck of cards, pictures of family traits (tongue rolling, attached earlobes).

  • Concept 1: Extract DNA from strawberries and look at the stringy clump.
  • Concept 2: Flip two coins 100 times and compare to a 1:2:1 pattern.
  • Concept 3: Build paper "chromosomes" and do meiosis by dealing half to each gamete.
  • Concept 4: Survey the class on trait frequencies and graph them (many are not simple dominant/recessive!).
  • Concept 5: Decode a message written in codons.

Common misconceptions:

  • "Dominant means more common." Not necessarily (polydactyly is dominant and rare).
  • "Traits are controlled by only one gene." Most are polygenic.
  • "Mutations are always bad." Most are neutral; some help.
  • "You get the same DNA as your siblings." Meiosis mixes uniquely (unless identical twins).

Pacing: about five 30-minute sessions. Method link: Mendel's peas model the full cycle of Unit 0: observe, hypothesize, design a controlled cross, count, and look for a pattern. Have students count a class sample too.
