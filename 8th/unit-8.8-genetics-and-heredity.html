Concept 1 · The instruction manual

DNA stores instructions in a 4-letter code

DNA is a long double-helix molecule made of four bases: A, T, C, G. A pairs with T and C pairs with G. A gene is a stretch of DNA that holds the instructions for one protein. Genes are packed into chromosomes in the nucleus.

Every cell in your body carries the same set of instructions, stored in a long molecule called DNA. DNA is twisted like a spiral ladder, and its rungs are made of four chemical letters (bases): A, T, C and G. The order of those letters is the code. The nucleus is the part of a cell that holds DNA, and there the DNA is wound up tightly into structures called chromosomes (humans have 46 in most cells). Zoom in below to see how a person, a cell, a nucleus, a chromosome and the double helix fit inside one another.

Concept 2 · Dominant and recessive

Two copies of each gene, in different versions

You inherit one copy of each gene from each parent. Different versions are alleles. A dominant allele shows when present; a recessive allele shows only with two copies. Genotype is the alleles; phenotype is the trait.

Genes come in pairs, one copy from each parent, and a gene can come in different versions called alleles. For a pea plant's flower colour, one allele might say purple and another might say white. The alleles you hold are your genotype; the trait you can see is your phenotype. When the two copies differ, the dominant allele is the one that shows, which is why a plant with one purple and one white allele can still look purple.

Analogy

Two light switches

Think of two switches wired to one bulb. A dominant allele turns the bulb on by itself. A recessive allele only shows when both switches are recessive; one dominant switch overrides it. Where it breaks: real alleles rarely act like simple on/off switches. Many traits involve multiple genes and the environment, so one dominant allele rarely tells the whole story (see "Not every trait is Mendelian" in Concept 3).

Concept 3 · Predicting offspring

A grid of probabilities

A Punnett square shows every way parental alleles can combine. It gives probabilities, not guarantees, like flipping coins.

When two parents have children, each parent passes on just one allele of each gene, and which one is a matter of chance. A Punnett square is a small grid that lists every way those alleles could pair up. Because each box is equally likely, the square gives probabilities, such as 1 in 4 or 3 in 4, for the traits of offspring. A real family of four children does not have to match the ratio, just as four coin flips do not have to give exactly two heads.

Analogy

Flipping two coins

Each parent hands over one allele like a coin flip. The grid lists all four coin combinations.

Diagram

Mendel's cross, in pictures

Mendel crossed a purple-flowered plant (PP) with a white-flowered one (pp). Every child (the F1 generation) was purple, genotype Pp, because P hides p. When those purple children were crossed with each other, the white color came back in about one grandchild in four. The Punnett square on the right shows why.

Real world

Not every trait is Mendelian

Incomplete dominance: red x white parents make pink children. Cross two pink plants and you get about 1 red : 2 pink : 1 white. The same Punnett square, but now the middle box has its own look.

Punnett squares work cleanly for traits controlled by one gene with two alleles, like pea flower colour or (roughly) earwax type. Many traits do not follow that pattern:

  • Incomplete dominance: neither allele fully hides the other. Red and white snapdragon parents can make pink flowers.
  • Polygenic traits: many genes add up, so height and skin colour vary smoothly instead of falling into two groups.
  • Environment: genes set a range, and nutrition, sunlight, and health shape where you land within it.

Eye colour is a good example. It is often taught as brown dominant over blue, but it really depends on several genes, so two blue-eyed parents can occasionally have a brown-eyed child. That is why this lesson uses pea plants.

Concept 4 · From code to protein

Genes tell cells which proteins to build

A cell copies a gene into messenger RNA and reads it three letters at a time (codons). Each codon calls for an amino acid; chains of amino acids fold into proteins, which build and run your body.

A gene is useful because it tells the cell how to build a protein, and proteins do most of the work in a body: they build tissues, carry oxygen and speed up chemical reactions. The cell first makes a working copy of the gene called messenger RNA (mRNA). Machinery then reads the mRNA three letters at a time; each three-letter codon names one amino acid, and the chain of amino acids folds into the finished protein. A mutation is a change in the DNA letters, and it can change one amino acid or none at all.

Analogy

Reading a recipe

Three-letter words like "CAT", "HAT" are codons: change a letter and you may change the whole meaning.

Concept 5 · Copies and sex cells

Mitosis copies; meiosis shuffles

Mitosis makes two identical body cells for growth and repair. Meiosis makes four different sex cells with half the chromosomes, so offspring get a unique mix from both parents. Today we also edit and copy genes (biotechnology).

Your body makes new cells in two different ways. In mitosis, a cell copies its chromosomes and splits into two identical cells, which is how you grow and heal a cut. In meiosis, a cell divides twice to make four sex cells (sperm or egg), each with half the usual number of chromosomes and each slightly different. When a sperm and an egg join, the chromosome count is restored, and the mix of genes from both parents makes every child unique, except identical twins.

Analogy

Photocopy vs. shuffled deck

Mitosis is a photocopier. Meiosis is shuffling a deck and dealing half to each player: every hand is different.

Diagram

The cell cycle and mitosis, in plain words

Before a body cell divides, it goes through a cycle with these steps:

Orange-red or pink chromosomes came from the mother, blue from the father (4 chromosomes drawn; humans have 46). Stages 2 to 5 are mitosis; interphase happens before it.

  1. Growth and copying (interphase): the cell grows and copies all its DNA, so every chromosome now has an identical twin.
  2. Prophase: the copied chromosomes coil up and become visible, and the nucleus wall breaks down.
  3. Metaphase: the chromosomes line up across the middle of the cell.
  4. Anaphase: each chromosome and its twin are pulled apart to opposite ends.
  5. Telophase and cytokinesis: two new nuclei form and the cell pinches into two daughter cells.

Result: two cells with the same DNA as the parent cell. Meiosis (below) uses a similar set of moves, but divides twice and shuffles the chromosomes along the way.

Real world

Editing genes: what people hope for and what they ask

A GMO (genetically modified organism) is a plant, animal or microbe whose DNA was changed with lab tools, often by adding a gene. CRISPR is a gene-editing tool adapted from a defense that bacteria use against viruses: a guide molecule leads a DNA-cutting protein to one matching spot in the DNA, and the cell's repair of the cut lets scientists switch off a gene or change it. Gene therapy treats a disease caused by a faulty gene by giving body cells a working copy or an edit. Changes in body cells are not passed to children. Changes to eggs, sperm or embryos would be.

Tutor notes: hands-on kit, misconceptions, pacing

Kit (about $8): two colors of beads or coins, paper bags, colored pencils, strawberry DNA extraction kit (dish soap, salt, rubbing alcohol, coffee filter), pipe cleaners, a deck of cards, pictures of family traits (tongue rolling, attached earlobes).

  • Concept 1: Extract DNA from strawberries and look at the stringy clump.
  • Concept 2: Flip two coins 100 times and compare to a 1:2:1 pattern.
  • Concept 3: Build paper "chromosomes" and do meiosis by dealing half to each gamete.
  • Concept 4: Survey the class on trait frequencies and graph them (many are not simple dominant/recessive!).
  • Concept 5: Decode a message written in codons.

Common misconceptions:

  • "Dominant means more common." Not necessarily (polydactyly is dominant and rare).
  • "Traits are controlled by only one gene." Most are polygenic.
  • "Mutations are always bad." Most are neutral; some help.
  • "You get the same DNA as your siblings." Meiosis mixes uniquely (unless identical twins).

Pacing: about five 30-minute sessions. Method link: Mendel's peas model the full cycle of Unit 0: observe, hypothesize, design a controlled cross, count, and look for a pattern. Have students count a class sample too.
