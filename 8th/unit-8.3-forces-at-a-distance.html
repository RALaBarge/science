Concept 1 · What is a field?

A field is a map of force in space

A field is a region where an object feels a force without touching: magnetic (on magnets and some metals), electric (on charges), and gravitational (on masses). We draw them with field lines: closer together means stronger.

Analogy

The smell of baking cookies

The smell is strongest at the oven and fainter far away. A field is like that, but for force: stronger near the source, weaker farther out.

Concept 2 · Weaker with distance

Move twice as far, force falls to one-fourth

For gravity and electric forces, force falls with the square of distance (the inverse-square law). Magnets weaken even faster. Force is always strongest up close.

Analogy

Spray paint

Spray a wall from 10 cm: a small, dark circle. From 20 cm: a circle four times as big and four times fainter. The paint spreads out over the square of the distance.

Concept 3 · Charge

Like charges repel, opposites attract

Matter has positive and negative charge. Rubbing can move electrons, so one object becomes negative and the other positive. Like charges repel, unlike charges attract.

Analogy

Socks from the dryer

Static cling is not magnetism. Tumbling rubs electrons from one fabric onto another, so the sock and shirt attract. Where it breaks: the attraction is electric, and it fades as the extra charge leaks away.

Concept 4 · Mass attracts mass

Gravity is always attractive

Every mass pulls on every other mass. The force depends on both masses and the distance. It is weak unless one object is huge (a planet or star).

Analogy

Everyone has a pull

You pull on your friend and your friend pulls on you, but both pulls are far too small to notice. Earth is so massive that its pull dominates.

Concept 5 · Electricity makes magnetism

Current in a coil makes a magnet you can switch off

Moving charges make a magnetic field. Wrap wire into a coil around iron and send current through it: an electromagnet. More coils or more current means a stronger magnet.

Analogy

A dimmer switch for magnetism

A normal magnet is always on. An electromagnet has a dimmer: crank up the current and the pull increases; flip the switch and it drops its load. Where it breaks: current is not the only control. More coils or an iron core make the magnet stronger at the same current.

Concept 6 · Simple circuits

Current needs a complete loop

A circuit is a closed loop that lets charge flow. A battery supplies voltage (the push on the charges), the flow of charge is current (in amps), and everything in the loop that opposes the flow adds resistance (in ohms). They connect through Ohm’s law: current = voltage ÷ resistance. A switch opens or closes the loop. Bulbs can be wired in series (one path) or parallel (separate paths).

Analogy

Water in pipes

The battery is a pump, voltage is the water pressure, current is how much water flows past a point each second, and resistance is a narrow pipe. A switch is a tap. Where it breaks: a pipe can leak water into the ground, but charge in a circuit only flows around the loop, and the wire is already full of charge before you turn it on.

Real world

Circuit safety

  • Never experiment with wall outlets or anything plugged into them. Mains electricity can seriously hurt or kill. Use only batteries (such as AA cells or a 9 V battery) for your own circuits.
  • Never connect a wire straight from one battery terminal to the other. That short circuit makes the wire and battery hot very quickly.
  • Do not take apart chargers, devices or batteries that are plugged in, damaged or swollen.
  • Household wiring uses parallel branches, so one lamp going out does not turn off the house.

A student says magnets work because "something invisible is connecting them." Explain what a field is and how you could show a magnet's field with compasses or iron filings.

Show a model answer

The field is the region around the magnet where it exerts force. It is not a thread. Small compasses placed around a magnet each line up with the local field, showing its direction; iron filings sprinkled on paper line up in the same pattern. The field is strongest near the poles and weakens with distance.

Tutor notes: hands-on kit, misconceptions, pacing

Kit (about $12): bar magnets (2), compass, iron filings or a bag of staples, a 6 V battery or 2 D-cells, insulated wire (3 m), a big nail, paperclips, balloons, wool cloth, tape, small foil pieces.

  • Concept 1: Sprinkle iron filings over paper on a magnet and sketch the pattern.
  • Concept 2: Map a magnet's field with compasses and compare to the simulation.
  • Concept 3: Build an electromagnet and count paperclips with 10, 20, 30 coils (do one variable at a time).
  • Concept 4: Charge a balloon and test it on foil, hair, and water stream.
  • Concept 5: Measure magnet pull through different thicknesses of cardboard.

Common misconceptions:

  • "All metals are magnetic." Only iron, nickel, cobalt (and alloys).
  • "Gravity only exists where there is air." It works in space.
  • "Heavier things have stronger fields." Depends on the kind of field.
  • "Opposites always attract." True for charge and poles, but not for gravity.

Pacing: about five 30-minute sessions. Method link: use the electromagnet as a model experiment: hypothesis, one variable (coils or current), and a replicate table (Unit 0). Warn that wires and batteries get hot.
