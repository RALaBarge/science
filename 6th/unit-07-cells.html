Cells: The Units of Life

You are about 30 trillion cells working together. Each one is a tiny city with a power plant, a library, a gate, and a recycling center. Learn how one cell works and you understand how every living thing works, from a bacterium to a blue whale.

Where we left off: Units 1 to 6 were about matter and energy in the Earth system. Now we zoom in on living things. You may already have learned that sunlight is captured as energy (Unit 1), that energy changes form (Units 2 and 3), and that water moves through the world (Unit 4). Cells use all of these ideas to stay alive.

Concept 1 · What is life?

Everything alive is made of cells

Living things grow, use energy, respond to their surroundings, and reproduce. All living things are made of cells, and all cells come from other cells.

All living things share certain features: they grow and develop, use energy, respond to their surroundings, reproduce, and are made of cells. The cell theory summarizes this. All living things are made of one or more cells. The cell is the basic unit of structure and function in living things. And all cells come from other cells that already existed. Most cells are far too small to see without a microscope. Robert Hooke first used the word cell in the 1600s when he looked at thin slices of cork, and Anton van Leeuwenhoek soon saw living single-celled organisms in pond water. Some organisms, like bacteria, are a single cell. Others, such as people and trees, are made of trillions of them.

Analogy

LEGO bricks

You can't build a castle without bricks, and you can't build a body without cells. Every cell in your body came from an earlier cell, which is how one cell becomes a whole person.

Where it breaks: a brick cannot make another brick. Cells can divide and make more cells, which is why living things grow and heal; bricks have to come from a factory.

Concept 2 · Parts of a cell

A tiny city

Cells contain little structures called organelles, each with a job. Plant cells have three extras: a cell wall, chloroplasts, and a big vacuole.

Cells contain small structures called organelles, each with a job. The nucleus holds the cell's DNA, the instructions for building and running the cell. Mitochondria release usable energy from sugar. The cell membrane surrounds the cell and controls what goes in and out. Ribosomes build proteins, and the cytoplasm is the jelly-like fluid that fills the cell. Plant cells have all of these plus a stiff cell wall for support, chloroplasts that capture light energy for making food, and a large vacuole that stores water. Animal cells lack those three. Cells that do a lot of work, like muscle cells, have many more mitochondria than cells that do less. Bacteria are simpler: they have no nucleus or other membrane-bound organelles.

Concept 3 · Cell energy

Making and using food energy

Photosynthesis turns light, water, and carbon dioxide into sugar and oxygen. Cellular respiration turns sugar and oxygen back into usable energy. Plants do both; animals only do respiration.

Cells need a constant supply of energy. Photosynthesis happens in the chloroplasts of plants and algae. It uses light energy to turn carbon dioxide and water into glucose, a sugar, and oxygen. The light energy is stored as chemical energy in the sugar. Cellular respiration happens in the mitochondria of nearly all living cells. It uses oxygen to break down glucose and releases the energy in a form the cell can use, with carbon dioxide and water as products. The two processes are close to opposites, and each one makes what the other needs. Plants do both: they make sugar by photosynthesis and use it for respiration. Animals cannot photosynthesize, so they get glucose by eating plants or other animals.

Analogy

Solar farm and power plant

sunlight → stored in sugar → released as usable energy

One organelle stores sunlight in sugar. Another organelle releases energy from the sugar in controlled steps to run the cell.

The outputs of one recipe are the inputs of the other. That is why plants and animals depend on each other.

Try it

Light a water plant

Predict. This pond plant (Elodea) makes oxygen bubbles when it photosynthesizes. If you double the light, will you get double the bubbles? What happens in the dark?

Do. Set the light to 0, then 25, 50, 75 and 100, and count the bubbles rising for a few seconds at each setting. Read the numbers under the picture.

Notice. No light means no bubbles. More light means more bubbles at first, but the gain gets smaller and smaller until adding light barely helps. Going from 0 to 25 adds far more than going from 75 to 100.

Why it matters. Bubble count is a way to measure how fast photosynthesis is making food (and oxygen). It shows light is an ingredient, but the plant is limited by other things too, such as carbon dioxide and how many chloroplasts it has.

Concept 4 · Moving in and out

The membrane is a picky gate

Particles spread out from crowded to less crowded places (diffusion). Water does too (osmosis). The cell membrane lets some things through and keeps others out.

Cells must take in materials like oxygen and nutrients and get rid of wastes, and the cell membrane controls this traffic. It is selectively permeable, meaning it lets some substances through and blocks others. Diffusion is the spreading of particles from an area where they are crowded to an area where they are less crowded. It happens on its own, with no energy needed, and continues until the particles are evenly spread. Osmosis is the diffusion of water across a membrane. If a cell is placed in very salty water, water leaves the cell and it shrinks, and in fresh water, water enters and the cell swells. This is why a celery stick turns limp in salt water and crisp in plain water, and why plants wilt when they lack water.

Analogy

Perfume in a room and a club bouncer

Diffusion needs no energy, like perfume drifting. The bouncer (membrane) decides who can get in. Some cells also use energy to pump things through.

Concept 5 · Many cells

From one cell to a whole body

Cells divide to grow and to repair. Living things can be one cell or trillions. In big organisms, cells team up in levels: cells → tissues → organs → organ systems → organism.

Single-celled organisms do everything in one cell, but large organisms are made of many cells that have different jobs. Cells divide to grow and to replace those that are worn out or damaged, which is how a cut heals. In many-celled organisms, cells of one type working together form a tissue, such as muscle tissue. Different tissues work together as an organ, such as the heart. Organs that share a job form an organ system, such as the circulatory system, and all the systems together make up the whole organism. This building up in levels is called levels of organization. Each level can do things that the smaller pieces cannot, in the same way a team can do more than one player alone.

Concept 6 · Making more cells

Cells make more cells

A cell goes around a loop called the cell cycle: it grows, copies its DNA, then divides in two. The DNA is packed into chromosomes, and the division step is called mitosis. Each new cell gets a full, matching set.

Cells make more cells through the cell cycle. First the cell grows and copies its DNA, so there are two full sets of instructions. The DNA is organized into structures called chromosomes, and each chromosome is copied. Then in mitosis, the copies are pulled apart so each end of the cell gets one complete set. Finally the cell splits in two, and the result is two new cells that are genetically identical to the original. Mitosis is how organisms grow, how a wound heals, and how single-celled organisms like amoebas reproduce. Cells in different parts of the body divide at different rates. Skin cells divide often, and many nerve cells hardly divide at all. When cell division goes out of control, the result can be a tumor, as in cancer.

Analogy

In plain words

StepWhat happens
1 Coil upThe copied DNA coils into chromosomes you could see with a microscope. Each one is two matching copies joined together.
2 Line upThe chromosomes line up across the middle of the cell, like students lined up before a bell.
3 Pull apartThe two copies of each chromosome are pulled to opposite ends of the cell.
4 Split in twoA new nucleus forms at each end and the cell pinches into two cells.

Why bother? Mitosis is how you grow and how a scrape heals. It makes cells that match the original. Eighth grade goes deeper, including how sex cells are made differently: see Unit 8.8: Genetics & Heredity.

Real world

Tiny cells that work for us

People have used single cells for thousands of years. Yeast, a one-celled fungus, makes the gas bubbles that puff up bread dough. Bacteria turn milk into yogurt. Using living things to make something useful is called biotechnology.

Today scientists go further. Every living cell reads DNA with the same basic code, so a bacterium can read a human gene. Insulin for people with diabetes is now made this way: the human insulin gene is put into microbes, and they make the insulin as they grow in big tanks. See Unit 9 for what insulin does.

Eighth grade covers how genes work and the questions people ask about editing them: Unit 8.8.

Check yourself

Can you explain it?

Why does a leaf cell have chloroplasts but your skin cell doesn't, and why do both have mitochondria?

Show a model answer

Plants make their own food using sunlight, and chloroplasts carry out photosynthesis. Animals eat food instead, so skin cells don't need chloroplasts. Both plant and animal cells must release energy from sugar to do their work, and mitochondria do that, so both have them.

Tutor notes: hands-on kit, misconceptions, pacing

Kit (about $10): a clip-on phone microscope lens, an onion, iodine, a potato, salt, gummy bears, food coloring, clear cups, aquarium plant (Elodea) or a handful of spinach leaves, a lamp.

  • Concept 1: peel onion skin, dab iodine, and look for brick-shaped cells.
  • Concept 2: build a cell model with a clear bag of gel: add beads, a grape, and a ball.
  • Concept 3: count Elodea bubbles per minute at different lamp distances. Spinach leaf discs float when they make oxygen.
  • Concept 4: drop food coloring into warm vs cool water. Soak gummy bears overnight in plain water and salt water.
  • Concept 5: have students form a human "organ system" and name each level.

Common misconceptions:

  • "Plants get food from soil." They make sugar by photosynthesis; soil gives water and minerals.
  • "Plants don't do respiration." They do, all day and night.
  • "Bigger animals have bigger cells." They mostly have more cells.
  • "The nucleus is the cell's brain and does everything." It holds instructions; other organelles do the work.

Pacing: about five 30-minute sessions.
