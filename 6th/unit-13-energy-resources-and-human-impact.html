Energy Resources & Human Impact

Every light switch, car ride, and phone charge uses energy that came from somewhere. Where it comes from, how much we use, and what it does to the air, land, and water are choices people make, and engineers weigh them with data. Use the evidence skills from Unit 0: How Science Works to judge them.

Side trip (best after Unit 6): Unit 6 showed hazards that come from nature; here we look at a hazard that comes from our own choices about energy. You may already have learned that energy changes form (Units 2 and 3) and that sunlight powers the weather (Unit 4). Those ideas explain where our electricity comes from, what it costs the planet, and what we can do about it. Unit 7.7 returns to resources and human impact.

Concept 1 · Where energy comes from

Renewable and non-renewable resources

Non-renewable resources (coal, oil, natural gas, uranium) take millions of years to form, so we use them up faster than they are replaced. Renewable resources (sunlight, wind, flowing water, heat from underground, plants) are replaced naturally on a human timescale.

Analogy

Savings account vs. paycheck

Fossil fuels are stored sunlight from ancient plants: a savings account built over hundreds of millions of years. Sun and wind are the daily paycheck, arriving again and again. Spend the savings faster than it forms and it runs out.

Where it breaks: spending savings does not change the air. Burning fossil fuels adds carbon dioxide, which is why the account has a climate cost too (Unit 4).

Real world

Michigan's giant battery

The Ludington Pumped Storage Plant on Lake Michigan (built 1973) is among the largest in the world. At night, when power is cheap, it pumps water uphill into a reservoir; when demand is high it lets the water fall back through turbines. It stores energy as gravitational potential energy (Unit 3).

Concept 2 · Fossil fuels and climate

Burning carbon warms the planet

Fossil fuels formed from ancient plants and plankton buried for millions of years (Unit 7.7). Burning them releases the stored carbon as carbon dioxide (CO₂). CO₂ is a greenhouse gas: it absorbs heat rays leaving Earth and sends some back down, so more CO₂ means a warmer planet.

Analogy

Baseballs and beach balls

Sunlight is like baseballs: it passes straight through the air. The heat the ground gives off is like beach balls: greenhouse gases catch some and toss them in every direction, so some come back down. More CO₂ means more catchers, so more heat comes back. Without any greenhouse gases Earth would average about −18 °C (0 °F); with the natural ones it's about +15 °C (59 °F).

Data

Carbon dioxide in the air

Measured at Mauna Loa, Hawaii, since 1958 (the Keeling Curve; approximate values). Before the Industrial Revolution it was about 280 ppm for thousands of years. Ice-core bubbles show that today's level is higher than in the last 800,000 years.

Try it

More CO₂, more warming

Simple model: each doubling of CO₂ eventually adds about 3 °C. The world has already warmed about 1.2 °C, and because the oceans delay the response, more warming is still "in the pipeline."

Concept 3 · Renewable energy

Sun, wind, and water

Renewable sources convert natural flows of energy into electricity. Each has strengths and limits: solar works only when the Sun is up, and wind power grows with the cube of wind speed.

Analogy

A pinwheel in the wind

A pinwheel spins faster in a stronger breeze. A wind turbine works the same way, spinning a generator. Doubling the wind speed doesn't double the power: it gives eight times more (2³), because more air hits the blades and each bit of air carries more energy.

Build it

Engineering challenge: the solar oven

The problem: build an oven that uses trapped sunlight to melt a chocolate chip or marshmallow faster than one sitting out in the open.

Criteria: your oven's chip or marshmallow visibly melts before the uncovered control's does, in the same sunlight (or under the same lamp).

Constraints: 1 small cardboard box, foil, plastic wrap, black paper, and tape.

  1. Try it in the game first. Play Power the Town at the bottom of this page. Notice how solar only works when the Sun is actually up and shining.
  2. Plan. Sketch two different ideas on your design log. Label which parts reflect sunlight in and which part absorbs it and turns it into heat.
  3. Build and test. Put your oven and an uncovered control marshmallow or chip out at the same time, in the same light. Check both every 2 minutes and record when each one starts to melt.
  4. Improve. Change one thing, test again, and record it.

Print a design log for each team. Reflect light in, let a dark surface absorb it, then trap the resulting heat with plastic wrap: the same absorb/reflect idea from Unit 1, and the same trapped heat from the greenhouse demo in Unit 4.

Concept 4 · Using less

Efficiency and conservation

The cheapest, cleanest energy is the energy you don't use. Efficiency means getting the same job done with less energy; conservation means using less. Power (watts) × time (hours) = energy (watt-hours).

Analogy

Leaky bucket (again)

An old bulb is a leaky bucket: for every 100 units of electrical energy, about 10 become light and 90 leak out as heat. An LED leaks less: about 40 units become light. Efficiency = useful output ÷ total input.

Where it breaks: the "leaked" heat is not destroyed. In winter it warms the room, which is why an old bulb is a poor light but a small heater.

Try it

Home energy audit

Typical wattages; real devices vary. One kilowatt-hour (kWh) = 1,000 watts running for one hour. U.S. grid power averages about 0.37 kg of CO₂ per kWh (varies by region and year).

Data

Cost of a bulb over its life

Assumes $0.18 per kWh; a 60 W incandescent bulb ($1, lasts 1,000 h) vs. a 10 W LED ($3, lasts 15,000 h). Replacement bulbs added when they burn out.

Concept 5 · Making choices

Weigh costs, benefits, and trade-offs

Engineers and communities choose among energy options using criteria (cost, reliability, pollution, land use) and trade-offs: no option is perfect. Clear criteria and data make a decision defensible, even when people weigh criteria differently.

Analogy

Picking a phone

You compare price, battery, camera, and size, and what matters most depends on you. A decision matrix does the same for power plants: score each option on each criterion, weight the criteria by importance, and add up.

Try it

Choose a power source for a town

Slide each criterion to show how much it matters to you (0 = ignore, 5 = critical). Scores for each option are rough teaching values from 1 (poor) to 5 (excellent).

Words to know

renewable
Replaced naturally within a human lifetime.
non-renewable
Used up faster than it forms.
fossil fuel
Coal, oil, or gas formed from ancient organisms.
greenhouse gas
A gas such as CO₂ or methane that absorbs heat given off by Earth and sends some of it back down.
climate
The long-term pattern of weather in a region.
kilowatt-hour
A unit of energy: 1,000 watts for one hour.
efficiency
The share of input energy that does useful work.
conservation
Using less energy or resources.
trade-off
Giving up some of one benefit to get another.
criteria
The standards used to judge options.
Tutor notes: hands-on kit, misconceptions, pacing

Kit (about $10): a small solar cell with a motor or a solar calculator, a pinwheel, a kill-a-watt style plug meter (borrowed from a library if possible), two thermometers and a clear jar (greenhouse demo), a flashlight, a bag of mixed beads (carbon cycle). For the solar oven: a small cardboard box, foil, plastic wrap, black paper, tape, and a few chocolate chips or mini marshmallows.

  • Concept 1: sort pictured energy sources into renewable and non-renewable cards.
  • Concept 2: greenhouse test: two jars, one sealed with plastic, both in sun; record temperatures every minute (a fair test, Unit 0).
  • Concept 3: tilt a small solar cell toward and away from a lamp and read the motor speed or voltage. Then run the solar oven challenge (about 30 to 40 minutes).
  • Concept 4: measure the wattage of devices at home with a plug meter and fill in the audit.
  • Concept 5: students build a decision matrix for a real choice (e.g., which light bulb to buy).

Common misconceptions:

  • "The greenhouse effect is bad." The natural effect keeps Earth habitable; the problem is the extra we've added.
  • "CO₂ is a lid that traps heat." Heat still escapes to space; greenhouse gases send part of it back down, so Earth has to warm up before as much escapes.
  • "Weather and climate are the same." Weather is today; climate is the long-term average.
  • "Renewable means no impact." Every source has some environmental effects.
  • "A cold day disproves warming." One day is weather, not a long-term trend.

Pacing: about five 30-minute sessions. Method link: use the greenhouse jar test to practice variables and controls (Unit 0, Concept 4).
