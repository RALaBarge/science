Where We’re Going

Right now you are moving at about 230 kilometers every second. You, this room and the whole solar system are circling the center of the Milky Way. Here is what we are orbiting, how long a lap takes, and how the Sun and planets got here in the first place.

Concept 1 · You’re on a very long ride

The Sun and everything near it circle the center of the Milky Way. One lap takes about 230 million years.

Analogy

One lap = one day

Pretend one galactic lap took one real day. Then all of human history, about 300,000 years, would be under two minutes of that day. Everything written down in history, about 5,000 years, would be about two seconds. The last time the Sun was at this spot in the galaxy, the first dinosaurs were just appearing.

Diagram

We also bob up and down

While the Sun goes around, it also drifts up and down through the flat disk, like a horse on a carousel. A full up-and-down cycle takes roughly 60 to 70 million years, and the Sun stays inside the disk the whole time. Scientists are still refining these numbers.

Why doesn’t the Sun just fall into the center?

It is falling, in a way. Gravity keeps pulling the Sun toward the middle, but the Sun is also moving sideways very fast. Falling toward the center while moving sideways bends its path into a circle-like orbit, the same reason the Moon does not crash into Earth.

Move the Sun sideways more slowly and it would sink in closer. Move it faster and it would drift outward. About 230 km/s is the speed that balances gravity at our distance.

The galaxy is not a spinning record

On a record, every spot goes around in the same time. In a galaxy, stars farther from the center have a bigger circle to travel, so their laps take longer. Out where we are, stars move at nearly the same speed whether they are a little closer or a little farther. That surprised astronomers, because it means there is much more mass out there than we can see. Section 2 explains this.

Astronomers think the Sun orbits at close to the same pace as the spiral pattern itself (this is still debated). That is why the arms in the picture turn together with the Sun, and it is one reason the Sun crosses the arms only slowly.

How do we know any of this?

We cannot leave the galaxy to look back at it, so astronomers measure it from inside. They track how stars and gas move across the sky, use radio telescopes to measure the distances to star-forming regions, and watch stars orbit the black hole at the center. Different methods give slightly different answers, which is why the numbers above come as ranges.

Concept 2 · What are we orbiting?

Gravity pulls between anything that has mass, so the Sun feels the pull of every star, cloud of gas and clump of dark matter. We orbit the combined pull of everything inside our orbit, not just the black hole in the middle.

Try it

Whose pull keeps the Sun in orbit?

Not to scale. The real galaxy has hundreds of billions of stars; this sketch uses about a hundred dots. The black hole is about 4.3 million Suns of mass; everything else inside our orbit is roughly 100 billion Suns, about 25,000 times more. The total is uncertain by a third or so, but the black hole is a tiny part either way.

How can we weigh the galaxy?

If you know how fast something orbits and how far away it is, you can work out how much mass is pulling on it. For the Sun, that is about 230 km/s at 26,000 light-years, which gives roughly 100 billion Suns of mass inside our orbit. (The simple formula treats the mass as if it were in a ball, so the answer is rough.)

Do the same for stars much farther out and the total keeps growing, even where there are hardly any visible stars. That unseen mass is called dark matter. We can measure its pull but we do not yet know what it is made of.

Part B · Where we came from

Concept 3 · How the solar system began

About 4.6 billion years ago, a cloud of gas and dust collapsed into a Sun and a spinning disk. The planets grew out of that disk.

At 40 AU you can see where Neptune would be. The real disk was far bigger, about 100 AU across. Analogy

An ice skater pulling her arms in

A skater spinning with her arms out spins slowly. Pull her arms in and she spins faster. A collapsing cloud does the same. As it shrinks, its slow spin speeds up. Spinning stuff can fall in toward the middle along the spin axis, but sideways it keeps whirling around, so the cloud ends up as a flat disk with a Sun at the center.

Analogy

Dust bunnies under a bed

Dust and lint stick together into clumps, and bigger clumps pick up even more dust. In the disk, dust stuck into pebbles, pebbles into boulders, and boulders into mountain-sized lumps. Once a lump is big enough, its own gravity pulls in the rest.

Analogy

A cold glass on a hot day

Water vapor in the air turns to droplets on a cold glass. In the young solar system, close to the Sun it was too hot for ice. Farther out, past the frost line, it was cold enough for ice to form. More solid material out there let planet cores grow bigger and faster, and the biggest ones pulled in huge amounts of gas.

When did the Sun “turn on”?

It was gradual. At first the baby Sun glowed because squeezing it made it hot, like a bicycle pump warming up. Hydrogen fusion, the Sun’s long-term power source, needs a core of about 10 million degrees. The young Sun took tens of millions of years to shrink and settle into steady fusion. The picture shows the first glow.

Why a flat disk, and why do all the planets go the same way?

The cloud had a little spin to begin with. Pieces of the cloud above and below the middle fell toward the middle plane and bumped into each other, canceling their up-and-down motion. What was left was everything moving around in the same direction in a flat sheet. That is why the planets orbit in nearly the same plane and in the same direction.

What does “sweeping the orbit clear” mean?

A planet that grows big enough pulls in or flings away the smaller lumps near its path. Watch the model: once a body is large, the space around its orbit empties. Astronomers use this idea in the official definition of a planet. By the current IAU definition for our Solar System, a planet has to be round and orbit the Sun, and it has to have cleared its neighborhood. Pluto has not, which is part of why it is a dwarf planet.

What is this model leaving out?
  • It follows motion in one flat plane only.
  • Bodies are drawn and treated as bigger than real so they collide often enough to see growth in a few minutes.
  • Gas giants grab gas by a simple rule, not by real gas physics.
  • It does not include planets moving inward or outward over time, which many scientists think happened.
  • Mercury’s region is mostly left out, since the model starts at 0.5 AU.

Concept 4 · What we know, and what we are still arguing about

Science is full of settled facts and open questions, and good scientists can tell which is which.

Evidence check

Well supported

  • The solar system is about 4.57 billion years old (from dating meteorites).
  • The planets orbit in almost one flat plane, in the same direction the Sun spins.
  • The Sun holds about 99.8% of the solar system’s mass.
  • Rocky planets are close to the Sun and giants are far out.
  • Young stars around the galaxy are seen with disks of gas and dust around them.

Still debated

  • What pushed the cloud to collapse. A nearby exploding star is one idea; there are others.
  • Exactly how dust grew past the size of boulders.
  • How much the giant planets moved after they formed.
  • The exact timing of each stage.
  • The details of the crash that made the Moon.

Check yourself

1. About how long does one lap of the Sun around the Milky Way take?

One lap is about 230 million years. The Sun is about 4.6 billion years old, so it has made around 20 laps.

2. What mostly provides the gravity that keeps the Sun in orbit around the galaxy?

On the scale, the black hole is about 1 part in 25,000 of the mass inside our orbit. Everything inside our orbit adds up to the pull we feel.

3. Why did the collapsing cloud flatten into a disk?

Spin plus collisions. Up-and-down motion gets canceled, and what remains is a flat, spinning sheet.

4. Why did the biggest planets form beyond the frost line?

Beyond the frost line, ice added building material, so cores grew large and fast.
