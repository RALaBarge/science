States of Matter & the Particle Model

Everything you can touch (the desk, the water, the air) is made of particles too small to see, always moving. Whether something is a solid, liquid, or gas depends on how those particles are arranged and how fast they move. Use the same steps from Unit 0: How Science Works to test the ideas.

Core unit (best after Unit 2): Unit 2 showed that thermal energy is particles jiggling. You may already have learned that adding energy makes particles move faster. Here we use that idea to explain solids, liquids, and gases, and what happens when matter changes state. It also prepares you for atoms in Unit 7.1 and reactions in Unit 7.2.

Each box zooms in on the water inside. A water molecule is one big oxygen atom (blue) joined to two small hydrogen atoms (white). Orange marks show motion. Note: the white cloud over a kettle is not the gas itself. Water vapor is invisible; the cloud is tiny drops of liquid.

Concept 1 · The particle idea

Matter is made of tiny moving particles

Matter is anything that has mass and takes up space. All matter is made of particles (atoms and molecules) that are always moving, with empty space between them.

Heat and phase changes: for the energy side of melting, freezing, boiling and condensing, see Unit 2: Thermal Energy.

Analogy

A screen picture is made of pixels

From arm's length the picture looks smooth. Zoom in and it is made of separate squares. Matter is like that: it looks smooth, but it is made of separate particles that are too small to see.

Where it breaks: pixels are flat squares that never move. Particles are tiny bits of matter that are always moving, with empty space between them.

Evidence that you can't see particles: a drop of dye

Try it

Zoom in on the dye: what is pushing it around?

In real life water molecules are far too small to see, and they hit the dye over and over from every side. The big orange bit stands for a tiny clump of dye. Its wobbling path (the trail) is called Brownian motion: it is the evidence that the water is made of moving particles. Hide the water molecules and you see only what a scientist would see: a dye bit that jiggles by itself.

Real world

Smells prove particles move

You smell cookies from another room because particles from the cookies fly through the air to your nose. Perfume and a drop of food coloring spread on their own without stirring. The spreading is faster in warm water because particles move faster when they have more thermal energy (Unit 2).

Concept 2 · Solid, liquid, gas

Same particles, different arrangement

In a solid, particles vibrate in fixed places. In a liquid, they slide past each other but stay close. In a gas, they fly apart and fill whatever space they have.

Real world

Not so simple: in-between materials

Cornstarch and water ("oobleck") flows slowly but turns hard when you punch it. Glass is a solid that lacks an orderly pattern. A plasma (lightning, the Sun, neon signs) is a fourth state: gas so hot that its atoms break apart. Most of the visible universe is plasma.

Concept 3 · Changes of state

Adding or removing thermal energy

Adding thermal energy can melt or boil a substance; removing it can freeze or condense it. The particles are the same; only their motion and spacing change. That's why mass is conserved when ice melts.

Concept 4 · Density and gases

Why things float, and why gases squeeze

Density is mass per volume (how tightly packed the particles are). An object floats if it is less dense than the liquid. Squeezing a gas into less volume makes its pressure rise.

Analogy

A suitcase

Density = mass ÷ volume. Same volume, more particles (or heavier particles) means higher density.

At constant temperature, pressure × volume stays the same (Boyle's law): halve the volume and the pressure doubles. Why: the same number of particles hit the walls more often.

Build it

Engineering challenge: the penny boat

The problem: fold one sheet of foil into a boat that holds as many pennies as possible before it sinks.

Criteria: most pennies held while still floating, with the boat touching nothing but water. Count the penny right before it sinks as the one that sank it, not the one before.

Constraints: one 20 cm × 20 cm sheet of foil. No tape, no other materials.

  1. Try it in the game first. Play Density Dock at the bottom of this page. Notice how the same mass sinks or floats depending on how much volume it takes up.
  2. Plan. Sketch two different shapes on your design log. Label how wide and how deep each one is.
  3. Build and test. Add pennies one at a time, evenly spaced, and record the number right before it sinks. Try a second, different shape.
  4. Improve. Change one thing, test again, and record it.

Print a design log for each team. A wide, shallow boat pushes more water out of the way than a crumpled ball of the same foil, so it floats more weight, even though the foil's own density never changes.

Concept 5 · Mixtures and properties

Mixtures, solutions, and separating them

A pure substance has one kind of particle. A mixture combines substances that keep their own properties, so you can separate them using physical properties such as size, magnetism, or boiling point. A solution is a mixture that is the same all the way through (a homogeneous mixture). A mixture with parts you can see is heterogeneous.

Analogy

Trail mix vs. a bag of sugar

Trail mix is a mixture: every piece keeps its own identity, so you can pick out the raisins. A bag of sugar is a pure substance: every crystal is the same compound, sucrose. A solution is a mixture stirred so evenly that you cannot see the pieces, like salt water.

Where it breaks: trail mix separates by hand. Salt water needs evaporation or distillation, because the salt particles are far too small to pick out.

Diagram

Two kinds of mixtures

Heterogeneous mixture

"Hetero" means different. You can see the different parts, and one spoonful can differ from another spoonful.

Trail mix, pizza, soil, granite, oil and water, cereal in milk.

Homogeneous mixture

"Homo" means same. It looks the same all the way through, and every spoonful is the same. A homogeneous mixture is also called a solution.

Salt water, air, vinegar, clear apple juice, brass (a metal mixture).

Both kinds are still mixtures: the parts are not chemically joined, and you can separate them by physical means. A pure substance such as sugar or water is not a mixture at all.

Data

How much dissolves? Solubility vs. temperature

Grams that dissolve in 100 g of water (about the same as 100 mL), from standard chemistry tables, rounded. Most solids dissolve better in hot water, but by very different amounts: sugar and potassium nitrate soar, salt barely changes. This is why you can make rock candy by cooling a hot sugar solution.

The gas curve is upside down. Pick the CO₂ chip: it slopes down. Gas particles dissolved in water are held loosely. When the water warms they move faster and escape into the air, so warm soda goes flat faster, and warm lake water holds less oxygen for fish. Notice the scale too: the gas amounts are tiny, a fraction of a gram, while the solids are tens or hundreds of grams.

Check yourself

Can you explain it?

A sealed bag of ice sits on a table and melts into water. Did the mass change? What happened to the particles? Where did the energy come from?

Show a model answer

The mass stayed the same because no particles entered or left the sealed bag. The particles changed from vibrating in fixed positions (solid) to sliding past each other (liquid), because they absorbed thermal energy from the warmer room. The energy moved from the warm room into the ice.

Tutor notes: hands-on kit, misconceptions, pacing

Kit (about $8): ice cubes, zip bags, food coloring, three clear cups, a kitchen scale, a syringe without a needle, marshmallows, an oil-and-water bottle, coffee filters, salt, sugar, a magnet. For the penny boat: a sheet of foil, a bowl of water, and a roll of pennies.

  • Concept 1: drop food coloring in cold and hot water; time the spread.
  • Concept 2: students act out solid, liquid, gas as particles in a taped square.
  • Concept 3: weigh a sealed bag of ice before and after it melts.
  • Concept 4: float and sink objects in water, salt water, and oil; push a plugged syringe. Then run the penny boat challenge (about 20 to 30 minutes).
  • Concept 5: separate sand, salt, iron filings, and water.

Common misconceptions:

  • "Particles in a solid don't move." They vibrate.
  • "Particles themselves melt or expand when heated." They move faster and spread out; they don't change size.
  • "Steam is the white cloud." Water vapor is invisible; the white cloud is tiny liquid droplets.
  • "Heavy things sink." Density, not weight, decides (a huge ship floats).

Pacing: about five 30-minute sessions. Method link: ask students to write the hypothesis (Unit 0, Concept 3) for the dye and ice-bag tests before running them.
