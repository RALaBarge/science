Natural Hazards

Two powerful earthquakes hit two countries in the same year. The stronger one, in Chile, killed a few hundred people. The weaker one, in Haiti, killed over a hundred thousand. Earth's hazards obey the same physics everywhere. What changes the outcome is what people do before they happen.

You may already have learned that plates stick and then slip, and that storms draw energy from warm water. This unit asks a new question: given these forces of nature, how do people predict, prepare, and lower the damage?

Concept 1 · Hazard and risk

A hazard isn't a disaster

A hazard is a natural event that could cause harm. A disaster is what happens when it hits people who weren't ready. Risk = how strong the hazard is × how many are exposed × how vulnerable they are.

Analogy

Rain on a picnic

Rain is the hazard in both scenes. The open picnic gets soaked; the sheltered one is fine. Same hazard, different preparation. A hazard becomes a disaster only when people are exposed and vulnerable.

Where it breaks: a picnic shelter can stop rain, but no tent stops an earthquake. For big hazards, preparation means building codes, drills, and warnings, not just cover.

Concept 2 · Making buildings safer

Resonance: the shaking that matches

Every building has a natural sway rate. If the ground shakes at about that rate, the building sways bigger and bigger: resonance. Engineers design to avoid it or absorb it.

Analogy

Pushing a swing

Small pushes that match the swing's rhythm add up. Earthquake waves are the pushes, and the building is the swing.

Build it

Engineering challenge: the shake-proof tower

The problem: build a tower that stays standing through an earthquake, whether the ground shakes slowly or quickly.

Criteria: at least 30 cm tall, holds a cup with 5 coins on top, and survives 10 seconds of slow shaking and 10 seconds of fast shaking. Taller wins ties.

Constraints: 30 sticks of dry spaghetti, 20 mini marshmallows, and 50 cm of tape. The tower must stand on the shake tray by itself.

The shake tray: a baking tray sitting on 6 marbles. One person pushes it back and forth about 5 cm each way. Slow is 1 push per second, fast is 3 per second. Count out loud so every team gets the same quake.

  1. Try it in the game first. Play Shake Tower at the bottom of this page. Which heights sway most when the shaking is slow? Which sway most when it's fast?
  2. Plan. Sketch two different ideas on your design log. For each, write how it avoids or absorbs the sway: a wide base, X-shaped braces, keeping the weight low, or changing the height.
  3. Build and test. Shake slow for 10 seconds, then fast for 10 seconds. Write down which speed did more damage.
  4. Improve. Change one thing, test again, and record it.

Print a design log for each team. If a tower wobbles more and more during one shaking speed, you're watching resonance, the same idea as the swing above.

Concept 3 · Tsunamis

Giant, fast, shallow-water waves

A tsunami is a train of waves from an underwater earthquake or landslide. In deep ocean it is fast and low. In shallow water it slows and grows tall.

Analogy

A highway traffic jam

On an open highway, cars are fast and spread out. At a jam, the front slows while the back keeps arriving, so cars bunch up. A tsunami does the same in shallow water: the front slows, the back catches up, and the water piles upward because it has nowhere else to go.

Where it breaks: cars can stop and wait. Water cannot, so the pile-up becomes a wall of water instead of a delay.

Try it

Ride a wave to shore

Data

Wave speed depends on depth

Speed ≈ √(9.8 × depth). In 4,000 m water that is about 198 m/s, or 713 km/h, the speed of a jet airliner. The wave is only about half a meter tall out there, so ships barely notice.

Concept 4 · Floods and landslides

Water and slopes

Rain that can't soak in runs off fast and floods. Water-soaked slopes lose their grip and slide. Pavement and bare slopes make both worse.

Analogy

Pouring water on a sponge vs. a plate

Pavement is the plate; a forest floor is the sponge. In a forest, most rain soaks in and reaches the creek slowly. On pavement it cannot soak in, so it sheets off and can flood a street in minutes.

Where it breaks: a sponge stops soaking once it is full, and city storm drains carry some water away. The analogy explains the speed, not the whole flood.

Concept 5 · Warnings and preparing

Seconds, minutes, and a plan

We can't stop hazards, but warnings and preparation save lives. The more lead time and the better the plan, the less harm.

Analogy

Lightning, then thunder: how far away is the storm?

Lightning and thunder happen at the same moment, but you see the flash first. Light travels about 300,000 kilometers every second, so it reaches you almost instantly. Sound travels only about 0.34 kilometers every second, so thunder lags behind. The farther away the storm, the longer the gap. Count the seconds between flash and thunder and divide by 3 to get the distance in kilometers (divide by 5 for miles). A gap of 30 seconds or less means the storm is close enough to be dangerous. Go indoors.

Where it breaks: the rule of thumb is a rough estimate, and storms move. It tells you whether lightning is near, not exactly where it will strike next.

Check yourself

Can you explain it?

Why did a smaller earthquake in Haiti kill so many more people than a much bigger one in Chile?

Show a model answer

Hazard strength is only one part of risk. The Haiti quake was shallow and struck close to a crowded city with few enforced building rules, so many buildings collapsed. Chile's quake was bigger but deeper and offshore, and its strict building codes and drills meant people were better prepared. Risk = hazard × exposure × vulnerability.

Tutor notes: hands-on kit, misconceptions, pacing

Kit (about $10): uncooked spaghetti and mini marshmallows, a baking tray and 6 marbles (the shake tray), a paper cup and 5 coins, jello or sponge cake, a pan with sand and a spray bottle, a toy house, tape.

  • Concept 1: compare two toy houses, one with a sturdy base. Rate each hazard and exposure.
  • Concept 2: run the shake-proof tower challenge (about 45 to 60 minutes). Keep the pushing steady: same distance, counted out loud.
  • Concept 3: in a long tray of water with a sloping sand beach, push a block to make a wave and watch it grow at the beach.
  • Concept 4: spray sand piles at different slopes. Compare a dry pile, a wet pile, and one held with toothpick "roots".
  • Concept 5: assemble a real go-bag; walk through the family meeting place and plan.

Common misconceptions:

  • "Natural disasters just happen." The disaster part depends on human choices.
  • "Bigger quake means bigger disaster." Depth, distance, building quality, and preparation matter.
  • "Tsunamis look like big surfing waves." Often they look like a rapidly rising tide.
  • "Michigan has no hazards." Tornadoes, floods, ice storms, and heat waves are real.

Pacing: about five 30-minute sessions. Handle disaster examples with care; stay with preparation and engineering rather than losses.
