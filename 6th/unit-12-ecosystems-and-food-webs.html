Ecosystems & Food Webs

A forest, a pond, and a lake are not just collections of living things. They are systems where energy flows, matter cycles, and every species affects the others. Use the investigation steps from Unit 0: How Science Works when you study how changes ripple through a food web.

Concept 1 · What is an ecosystem?

Living and non-living parts, working together

An ecosystem includes all the living (biotic) and non-living (abiotic) things in an area and how they interact. Scientists study life at nested levels, from one organism up to the whole planet.

An ecosystem is all of the living and nonliving things in an area and the way they interact. The living parts are biotic factors, such as plants, animals, fungi, and bacteria. The nonliving parts are abiotic factors, such as sunlight, water, temperature, soil, and air. An ecosystem can be as small as a puddle or as large as a lake or forest. Scientists study life at nested levels: an organism is one living thing, a population is all the members of one species in an area, a community is all the populations living together, an ecosystem adds the nonliving parts, and the biosphere is all of Earth where life exists. Each level depends on the others.

Notice. Concept 2 · Food and energy

Producers, consumers, and decomposers

Producers (plants, algae) capture sunlight and make food. Consumers eat other organisms. Decomposers (fungi, bacteria) break down dead things. Energy flows one way and roughly 10% moves up to the next level on average (a teaching average, not a law; real food chains range from a few percent to about 20%).

Energy enters most ecosystems as sunlight. Producers, such as plants and algae, capture it by photosynthesis and store it in sugars. Consumers get energy by eating other organisms: herbivores eat plants, carnivores eat animals, and omnivores eat both. Decomposers, such as fungi and bacteria, break down dead organisms and wastes. A food chain shows one path of energy, and a food web shows the many connected paths. At each step in the chain, most of the energy is used by the organism for life processes or lost as heat, and only about 10 percent is stored in the body and passed on. For that reason there are far fewer top predators than plants, and an energy pyramid gets narrower toward the top.

Analogy

Passing a leaky bucket

Every living thing uses most of its food energy to move, grow, and stay warm, and releases it as heat (Unit 2). Only about one-tenth reaches the next level, so a bucket passed up the food chain gets emptier at every hand-off.

Where it breaks: the missing energy is not spilled. It leaves as heat and is no longer food, which is why food chains are short.

Real world

Why eating plants is "efficient"

Producing 1 kg of beef can take on the order of 25 kg of feed (published estimates range widely, from under 10 to over 25), while grains and beans feed people directly. If the plants go straight to people, you skip a level and keep roughly ten times as much of the Sun's energy. That's one reason food scientists study plant-based protein.

Concept 3 · Recycling matter

Matter cycles; energy flows

Unlike energy, matter is reused. The carbon in your body was once in a plant, and before that, in the air. Decomposers return nutrients to the soil so new plants can grow.

Energy flows through an ecosystem in one direction, and much of it is lost as heat, but matter is used over and over. The atoms in your body, like carbon, hydrogen, oxygen, and nitrogen, have been part of many other organisms. In the carbon cycle, plants take in carbon dioxide from the air and build it into sugars by photosynthesis. Animals eat the plants and use the carbon, and both plants and animals release carbon dioxide through respiration. When organisms die, decomposers break them down, returning carbon to the air and nutrients to the soil, where new plants take them up. Burning fossil fuels also puts carbon in the air. Without decomposers, nutrients would stay locked in dead material and ecosystems would run out of what they need.

Analogy

A library

Books (carbon atoms) are checked out by plants from the air, passed to readers (animals) and returned when they die or exhale, shelved again by decomposers. The library never makes or destroys books; they just move between hands. Burning fossil fuels is like returning books that had been in storage for 300 million years, all at once.

Concept 4 · Populations

Why population sizes rise and fall

A population grows quickly when resources are plentiful, then levels off at the carrying capacity, the number the environment can support. Predators and prey keep each other's numbers in cycles.

A population is all the members of one species living in the same area. When food, water, and space are plentiful, a population can grow quickly. But resources are limited, so growth eventually slows and levels off near the carrying capacity, the largest population the environment can support over time. Limiting factors such as food, space, water, disease, and predators keep populations from growing forever. Predators and prey also affect each other's numbers. When prey are plentiful, predators have more food and increase, then they eat more prey and the prey decrease, which leads to fewer predators, and the cycle repeats. Organisms also interact in other ways: competition for the same resource, mutualism where both benefit, and parasitism where one benefits and the other is harmed.

Analogy

A restaurant with 100 seats

The restaurant fills up fast at first, but only 100 people fit, no matter how many want a table. Food, water, shelter, and space are an ecosystem's "seats". When they run out, growth stops or the population crashes.

Concept 5 · Changing ecosystems

Remove one species, and the web shifts

Take away or add a species and the whole web can shift. Removing or adding a species, like an invasive species, causes a ripple effect through the food web. Many Great Lakes problems came from species that arrived from other places.

Ecosystems are connected, so a change to one species can affect many others, a ripple effect. If a species is removed, the organisms that depended on it for food may decline, and the ones it ate may increase. A keystone species has an especially large effect, as sea otters do by eating sea urchins that would otherwise destroy kelp forests. An invasive species is one brought from another place, by accident or on purpose, that spreads quickly because it has no natural predators or diseases there. Zebra mussels, sea lampreys, and Asian carp have harmed Great Lakes food webs. Ecosystems can recover when people take action, such as controlling invasive species, protecting habitat, and restoring native species, though it can take many years.

Analogy

Pulling a block from a tower

In a game of Jenga, some blocks barely matter; others make the whole tower tilt. Species that many others depend on are the tower's key blocks: scientists call them keystone species. Sea otters, wolves, and beavers are famous examples.

Check yourself

Can you explain it?

Zebra mussels filter huge amounts of plankton out of a lake. Predict what happens to zooplankton and to the alewife that eat them, and explain how energy moves along the chain.

Show a model answer

Zebra mussels eat phytoplankton, so they compete with zooplankton for the same food. Less phytoplankton means fewer zooplankton, which means less food for alewife, and fewer fish are fed higher up. Energy flows from the Sun to phytoplankton to its consumers, losing about 90% at each step, so a loss at the bottom is felt by everyone above.

Tutor notes: hands-on kit, misconceptions, pacing

Kit (about $8): index cards and yarn (human food web), a clear jar with soil, leaves and a bit of water (mini ecosystem, or a closed bottle ecosystem), a meter-square frame, a hand lens, a bag of beans and poker chips (predator–prey game).

  • Concept 1: scavenger hunt in a yard or park: list 10 biotic and 5 abiotic things.
  • Concept 2: each student is an organism; connect them with yarn; one drops out and everyone connected tugs.
  • Concept 3: bury a banana peel, a piece of paper and a plastic bag; dig them up later to compare.
  • Concept 4: predator-prey game: "wolves" collect "moose" tokens; record numbers each round and graph.
  • Concept 5: role-play adding an invasive species to the yarn web.

Common misconceptions:

  • "Arrows in a food web point from the eater to the eaten." They show energy flow: from the eaten to the eater.
  • "Decomposers aren't part of the food web." They're essential: no decomposers, no recycling.
  • "Predators are bad for prey populations." They often keep prey healthy by removing the sick and preventing overgrazing.
  • "Energy is recycled like matter." Energy flows one way and leaves as heat.

Pacing: about five 30-minute sessions. Method link: have students write an "If we remove…, then…, because…" hypothesis (Unit 0, Concept 3) before using the ripple tool.
