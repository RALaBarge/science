Forces, Motion & Energy

Why does a roller coaster go fastest at the bottom of a hill? Why is a full shopping cart so hard to stop? Why does an egg survive a fall onto a pillow but not onto the floor? Every motion you see comes from forces and energy, and both follow rules you can calculate.

Where we left off: Unit 2 showed that thermal energy is particles jiggling, and that energy moves from hot to cold. You may already have learned that energy changes form rather than disappearing. Here we use the same energy ideas for moving objects, and we will see that friction is one way motion turns into heat.

Concept 1 · Describing motion

Speed is distance per time

Speed tells you how far something goes in each second: speed = distance ÷ time. On a distance–time graph, a steeper line means faster.

Analogy

Two delivery drivers

Both drivers cover the same 10 miles. Driver A arrives in 10 minutes; Driver B takes 20. Same distance, less time means a higher speed. Speed = distance ÷ time, so A averages 1 mile per minute (60 mph) and B averages 0.5 (30 mph).

Where it breaks: a real car speeds up, stops at lights, and slows in traffic. Average speed hides all of that; a distance–time graph shows it.

Concept 2 · What changes motion

Balanced and unbalanced forces

A force is a push or a pull. If forces are balanced, motion doesn't change. If they're unbalanced, the object speeds up, slows down, or turns. A bigger net force or a smaller mass means more acceleration. Mass also measures inertia: how hard something is to start moving or to stop. A full cart is harder to get going and harder to stop.

Analogy

Tug of war

Net force = left pull minus right pull. When it isn't zero, the winner's direction is where the motion changes.

Concept 3 · Two forces you always meet

Friction and gravity

Friction pushes against motion between touching surfaces. Gravity pulls everything toward the center of Earth. Together they explain why things stop and why things fall.

Analogy

Microscopic Velcro

Zoom in on glass, ice, or a basketball court and you find tiny hills and valleys. Press two surfaces together and the bumps catch like the hooks and loops of Velcro. Sliding makes them snag, and the snagging pushes against the motion. That is friction, and it is also why rubbing makes heat.

Where it breaks: Velcro is built to stick, and it wears out. Friction is not a choice: it happens between every pair of surfaces that touch and slide.

Diagram

All the forces on a sliding book

Up and down cancel, so the book doesn't sink or float. Friction has no partner, so the book slows down.

Try it

How far does it slide?

Concept 4 · Two kinds of energy

Stored and moving

Potential energy is stored energy (height makes gravitational potential energy). Kinetic energy is energy of motion. A roller coaster trades one for the other all ride long.

Analogy

Savings account and spending money

Total money stays the same. You just move it between "saved" and "spent". Energy works the same way.

Concept 5 · Conservation

Energy changes form, never disappears

Energy can't be created or destroyed. It changes form, and in real life some always ends up as thermal energy (heat) and sound.

Analogy

A leaky bucket

The water that leaked didn't vanish, it just went somewhere you didn't want. Wasted energy is still energy, usually heat.

Data

Each bounce keeps a fixed fraction of the energy. The missing energy went to heat (the ball and floor warm up a tiny bit) and sound (the thud).

Concept 6 · When things collide

Equal pushes, softer stops

When two things collide, they push on each other with equal and opposite forces. That's Newton's third law. You can't make the force go away, but you can make it smaller by stretching the stop over more time.

Analogy

Catching a water balloon

The balloon has to lose all its speed either way. Losing it slowly takes a gentle push; losing it all at once takes a huge one.

Diagram

Car meets truck

The pushes are exactly equal. The car's motion changes nine times more because it has less mass, so less inertia. That's why the people in the small car get jolted hardest.

Try it

Drop an egg from 1 meter

Data

Longer stop, smaller force

Every egg hits at the same speed, about 4.4 m/s. Softer surfaces stretch the stop out, so the push is smaller. Rounded teaching values; a real egg cracks somewhere around 25 N.

Build it

Engineering challenge: the egg lander

The problem: design something that lets a raw egg survive a drop from 2 meters (about the height of a top bunk).

Your materials: up to 10 sheets of paper, 1 meter of masking tape, and 10 straws. Nothing else. The egg must come out so you can check it.

  1. Plan. Sketch two different ideas. For each one, write one sentence on how it makes the egg's stop take longer.
  2. Build the idea you think is better.
  3. Test from 1 meter first, then 2 meters. Write down what happened each time.
  4. Improve. Change one thing, test again, and record it. Real engineers repeat this loop many times.

Tie-breaker: if two landers both work, the lighter one wins. Drop over a trash bag. Print a design log for each team.

Check yourself

Can you explain it?

Why is a roller coaster fastest at the bottom of the first hill, and why can't the second hill be taller than the first?

Show a model answer

At the top of the first hill the car has lots of stored (potential) energy. Rolling down turns it into moving (kinetic) energy, so speed is highest at the bottom.

Energy can't be created. Friction and air resistance even take some away as heat. So the car can never climb higher than where it started.

Words to know

speed
Distance traveled per unit time.
force
A push or a pull, measured in newtons.
net force
All forces on an object added together.
balanced
Forces cancel; motion doesn't change.
acceleration
How quickly speed or direction changes.
inertia
How much an object resists a change in its motion. More mass means more inertia.
friction
Force between touching surfaces that opposes sliding.
gravity
Pull between objects with mass; on Earth, it pulls things down.
potential energy
Stored energy, such as from height or stretch.
kinetic energy
Energy of motion.
conservation of energy
Energy changes form but the total stays the same.
Newton's third law
When two objects push on each other, the forces are equal in size and opposite in direction.
Tutor notes: hands-on kit, misconceptions, pacing

Kit (about $10): a toy car or marble, a ramp (board and books), tape measure, stopwatch (phone), spring scale or rubber band, sandpaper, wax paper, a few balls, a skateboard-like toy, raw eggs, paper, masking tape, and straws for the egg lander.

  • Concept 1: mark a 5 m line, time a classmate's walk, jog, and hop, then compute speeds.
  • Concept 2: stretch a rubber band tied to a loaded vs empty cart and compare stretch to acceleration.
  • Concept 3: slide the same block down a ramp lined with sandpaper, plain board, and wax paper.
  • Concept 4: release a marble from different ramp heights and measure how far it launches a cup.
  • Concept 5: drop different balls from 1 m and photograph the bounce height next to a meter stick.
  • Concept 6: the egg lander challenge (one longer session). Push two rolling carts or skateboards together to feel equal and opposite pushes.

Common misconceptions:

  • "Moving things need a continuing force." Without friction, motion would continue by itself.
  • "Heavier things fall faster." In air without much drag, they fall at the same rate.
  • "Energy gets used up." It changes form; some becomes heat.
  • "No motion means no forces." Balanced forces can be present.
  • "In a crash, the bigger object pushes harder." The forces are equal; the smaller object's motion changes more.
  • "Padding makes the force go away." It makes the force smaller by making the stop take longer.

Pacing: about six 30-minute sessions, plus one longer session for the egg lander.
