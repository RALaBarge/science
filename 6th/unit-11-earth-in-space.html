Earth in Space

Day and night, the seasons, the phases of the Moon, eclipses, and ocean tides are all caused by motion: Earth spinning, Earth orbiting the Sun, and the Moon orbiting Earth. Use the model-building steps from Unit 0: How Science Works to see why the same pattern repeats.

Core unit (best after Unit 3): Unit 3 introduced gravity and motion, and Unit 1 showed how light travels in straight lines. You may already have learned that gravity keeps things in orbit. Here we use those ideas to explain day and night, seasons, moon phases, eclipses, and tides, and we scale up to the whole Solar System. Unit 8.6 returns to orbits with real numbers.

Concept 1 · Earth spins

Rotation gives us day and night

Earth rotates once about every 24 hours around its axis. The side facing the Sun has day; the side facing away has night. The Sun doesn't move across the sky; we do.

Try it

Spin the Earth

Predict. It is noon in Ann Arbor and the marker faces the Sun. Predict where the marker will be 6 hours later, and 12 hours later. Does the Sun move or does the marker move?

Do. Slide the time of day from 12 to 18, then to 24 (midnight), then back to 6 and 12 again. Read the sentence below the picture each time.

Notice. The Sun and its light stay put on the left. The marker turns with Earth: it rides into darkness at sunset, is on the far side at midnight, and comes back into sunlight at dawn. Each hour the marker turns 15°, so 24 hours is one full turn of 360°.

Why it matters. Day and night are not caused by the Sun moving or going out. They are caused by where your spot on a spinning Earth faces, which is why places far east of you have their sunrise first.

Real world

Proof the Earth rotates

Leave a camera shutter open for hours and the stars turn into streaks. The camera is not moving, so the sky only seems to turn because Earth is rotating beneath it. Stars trace circles around one fixed point in the sky: in Ann Arbor that is the North Star. (The long-exposure photo below was taken in Chile, so its circles center on the point above the south pole.) Satellites launched eastward also get a free speed boost of up to about 460 m/s from Earth's spin at the equator, which is why launch sites are placed as close to the equator as possible.

Build it

Engineering challenge: the shadow clock

The problem: build a sundial that another team can read, using a lamp moved along a marked arc to stand in for the Sun's path across the sky.

Criteria: the other team's guessed time is within 1 hour of the hour you actually set the lamp to.

Constraints: 1 straight stick or pencil, a flat base, a marker, and a lamp. Mark the lamp's position for 9 AM (low, to one side), 12 PM (highest, overhead), and 3 PM (low, to the other side) before anyone reads it.

  1. Try it in the game first. Play Moon Photographer at the bottom of this page, then revisit the "Spin the Earth" slider above, which shows the Sun's angle changing through the day.
  2. Plan. Sketch two different ideas on your design log. Predict where the shadow will point and how long it will be at each marked hour.
  3. Build and test. Set the lamp to each marked hour and mark the shadow it casts. Then set it to a mystery hour and see if your partner team can read the time from the shadow alone.
  4. Improve. Change one thing, test again, and record it.

Print a design log for each team. The shadow is shortest at noon and swings because Earth, not the Sun, is turning: the same idea as the "Spin the Earth" slider above.

Concept 2 · Earth orbits

The seasons come from tilt, not distance

Earth orbits the Sun once a year with its axis tilted 23.4°. When the Northern Hemisphere tilts toward the Sun, sunlight hits more directly and days are longer: summer. Earth is actually closest to the Sun in early January.

Analogy

A flashlight on a tilted page

Same flashlight, same energy. Slanted light spreads over more ground, so each square meter warms less.

Try it

Ride around the Sun

Predict. Is Earth closer to the Sun in summer? Make a guess about the distance in June and in December before you move the slider.

Do. Start at day 172 (June 21). Slide to day 355 (December 21), then to day 80 (March) and day 266 (September). At each, read the noon Sun height, the hours of daylight, and the Earth–Sun distance. Watch which way the tilted axis points; it never changes.

Notice. The axis keeps pointing the same direction all year. In June the Northern Hemisphere leans toward the Sun, the noon Sun is high and the days are long. In December it leans away, and the Sun is low and days are short. The distance barely changes, and Earth is actually closest to the Sun in early January.

Why it matters. Seasons come from tilt, not distance. A high Sun spreads the same sunlight over a smaller patch of ground and gives more hours of daylight, so summer is warmer. When it is summer in Michigan it is winter in Australia.

Concept 3 · The Moon

Moon phases

The Moon always has one half lit by the Sun. As it orbits Earth, the cycle of phases repeats about every 29.5 days, and we see different amounts of the lit half. Phases are not caused by Earth's shadow.

Analogy

A ball and a lamp

Hold a ball at arm's length in a dark room with one lamp. The side facing the lamp is always lit. Turn slowly in a circle: sometimes you see the dark side (new moon), sometimes the lit half (full), and in between a slice.

Top view. The ball is always half lit, on the side facing the lamp. Walk the ball around your head counterclockwise and you will see it go A, B, C, D. The sizes and distances are not to scale.

That is the exact model we use to understand the Moon. Try it with a tennis ball and a flashlight.

Try it

Orbit the Moon

Predict. Is the Moon’s phase caused by Earth’s shadow? Predict what shape you will see when the Moon is on the side of Earth facing the Sun, and when it is on the far side.

Do. Drag the day from 0 through 29.5, stopping at days 0, 7, 15, 22. Compare the left picture (top view) with the right picture (what you see) and read the percent lit.

Notice. The Moon is half lit at every position, always on the side facing the Sun. At day 0 the lit half faces away from you, so you see almost none (new). At day 15 the whole lit half faces you (full). In between you see a growing, then shrinking, slice. Earth’s shadow is not involved.

Why it matters. Phases are a view-angle effect. The same thing happens with a ball and a lamp: the pattern repeats every 29.5 days, which is where the idea of a month came from.

Concept 4 · Alignments

Eclipses and tides

An eclipse happens when the Sun, Moon, and Earth line up. The Moon's gravity also pulls on ocean water, causing tides, and the biggest tides come when the Sun and Moon pull along the same line.

Diagram

Two kinds of eclipse

Analogy

A tilted hula hoop

The Moon's orbit is tilted about 5° from Earth's orbit, like a hula hoop tipped against another one. Most months, the Moon passes a little above or below the Sun–Earth line, so its shadow misses. We get 2 to 5 solar eclipses a year somewhere on Earth, but any one place sees a total one only about once in 375 years.

  • Blue ring: Earth's orbit around the Sun
  • Orange ring: the Moon's orbit around Earth (tilt drawn much bigger than real)
  • Small circles: the two nodes, where the rings cross. The dashed line through them keeps the same direction all year.
  • Yellow dot: the Moon at new moon. Its dotted drop line shows how far it is above or below the flat blue plane.

Two hula hoops that cross at only two spots (the nodes). The Moon's hoop keeps pointing the same way in space all year, so the line through the nodes lines up with the Sun only twice a year: those two stretches are eclipse seasons. Even then the Moon must also be new or full. The tilt is drawn much bigger than the real 5°. In the real sky the nodes slowly turn, so eclipse seasons slide about 19 days earlier each year.

Real world

Tides around the world

Typical tidal range (high minus low). The Bay of Fundy in Canada has the world's biggest. The Great Lakes have tides of only a few centimeters, too small to notice, because the lakes are small compared to the oceans.

Concept 5 · Gravity and scale

The solar system is mostly empty space

Gravity holds planets in orbit around the Sun and the Moon around Earth. Planets farther out move slower and take longer to orbit. The distances are so vast that a scale model is the only way to feel them.

Have you ever wondered how things stay up in the air, or how the planets stay in their paths? Things do not really stay up by themselves. Throw a ball and it falls back down. The planets are falling too, but they are moving sideways so fast that they keep missing the Sun. Here are the key words before the analogy.

  • Force: a push or a pull on an object (more in Unit 3).
  • Gravity: the pull every object has on every other object. Bigger objects pull harder, so you feel Earth pulling you but not your desk.
  • Motion: how fast and in what direction something is moving. Without a pull, a moving object just keeps going in a straight line.
  • Orbit: the curved path an object takes around another because gravity keeps bending its straight-line motion into a loop, like the Moon around Earth.
  • Scale model: a smaller copy that keeps distances in proportion, so huge space distances fit into a room or a field.

Gravity plus sideways motion makes an orbit. Space is also mostly empty: the planets are tiny compared with the huge gaps between them.

Analogy

A ball on a string

An orbit is a continuous fall around the Sun: motion forward plus gravity pulling inward.

Try it

Walk the scale model

Predict. If the Sun were a 10 cm ball, how far away do you think Earth would be: across a table, across a room, or across a field? And Neptune?

Do. Press each planet’s button in order, from Mercury to Neptune. Read the distance in meters and in steps. If you can, walk off Earth’s distance outside (about one step is 0.75 m).

Notice. Earth is about 11 m from the Sun in this model, roughly a classroom and a hallway away, and Earth itself would be a grain of sand, about 1 mm across. Neptune is more than 300 m away. The distances grow very fast: the gaps between the outer planets are far wider than the whole inner solar system.

Why it matters. Space is almost entirely empty. This is why sunlight takes 8 minutes to reach Earth but more than 4 hours to reach Neptune, and why space probes take years to cross the solar system.

Scale: the Sun is a 10 cm ball.

Check yourself

Can you explain it?

A friend says: "It's colder in winter because Earth is farther from the Sun." Use evidence to explain why that is wrong and what really causes the seasons.

Show a model answer

Earth is actually closest to the Sun in early January, which is winter in the Northern Hemisphere. Also, the hemispheres have opposite seasons at the same time, so distance can't be the cause. The seasons come from Earth's 23.4° axis tilt: in winter, our hemisphere tilts away from the Sun, so sunlight arrives at a slant (spread over more ground) and days are shorter, giving less heating.

Tutor notes: hands-on kit, misconceptions, pacing

Kit (about $10): a lamp, a tennis ball and a pencil, a globe or large ball, a flashlight, a ball on a string, a roll of toilet paper (scale model: 1 sheet = about 1 million km), a meter stick, a sticky note for "Ann Arbor". For the shadow clock: a straight stick or pencil, a flat base, and a marker.

  • Concept 1: turn a globe in front of a lamp; mark Ann Arbor with a sticky note and say when it is day. Then run the shadow clock challenge (about 30 to 40 minutes).
  • Concept 2: shine a flashlight at a tilted vs. flat sheet of grid paper; count lit squares.
  • Concept 3: the ball-and-lamp moon phase model.
  • Concept 4: demonstrate eclipses with a lamp, tennis ball, and a small ball.
  • Concept 5: pace out the scale model outside (10 m to Earth if the Sun is a 10 cm ball, a pinhead).

Common misconceptions:

  • "Seasons are caused by distance from the Sun." They are caused by axis tilt.
  • "Moon phases are Earth's shadow." The shadow causes lunar eclipses only.
  • "The Moon only comes out at night." It is often visible in daytime.
  • "There's no gravity in space." Gravity is what holds orbits together; astronauts float because they are in free fall.

Pacing: about five 30-minute sessions. Method link: have students make a prediction (Unit 0, Concept 3) before turning each model, then compare to what they see.
