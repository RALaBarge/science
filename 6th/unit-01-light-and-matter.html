Concept 1 · How light travels

Light goes straight

In one uniform material (like air), light travels in straight lines called rays until something gets in its way. That is why shadows exist. Light can bend where it crosses from one material into another, but that comes later in this unit.

Analogy

Straight arrows, not sprinkler drops

Light does not spray like a garden sprinkler. A water drop follows a curved arc because gravity pulls it down. A light ray does not sag: it keeps going in a straight line until it hits something.

Imagine tiny arrows flying out in every direction. A book stops the arrows that hit it, so the space directly behind gets none. That arrow-free space is the shadow.

Diagram

Pinhole box: proof the rays are straight

The ray from the top of the arrow goes down through the hole. The ray from the bottom goes up. Straight lines that cross make a flipped picture. Your eye and a camera work the same way.

Concept 2 · What light does when it hits something

Bounce, pass, or soak

When light hits a material it can be reflected, transmitted, or absorbed. Usually it does a mix of all three.

Analogy

A ball hitting three different things

Reflect

A ball hits a wall and bounces back. Mirrors, snow, and white paper send most light back.

Transmit

A ball flies through an open window and keeps going. Clear glass and clean water let most light through.

Absorb

A ball lands in a sponge and stays there. Its energy gets soaked in. Dark cloth and asphalt soak up light and warm up.

Concept 3 · Why materials are different

See-through, sort of, or not at all

Materials are transparent, translucent, or opaque depending on how their tiny particles handle light.

Analogy

Three spaces: hallway, crowd, wall

Empty hallway = transparent

Almost nothing in the way. Light walks straight through, so you see a clear picture on the other side.

Crowded mall = translucent

People get through, but they bump around and leave in random directions. Light is scattered the same way: you see a glow, not a clear picture.

Locked wall = opaque

Nobody gets through. Some people bounce back and the rest stop. No light reaches your eye from behind it, so you see a shadow.

What changes is the direction of the light. Transparent: straight through, clear picture. Translucent: scattered, glow but no picture. Opaque: stopped, shadow.

Where it breaks: real materials are messy. One sheet of wax paper lets you read the words; stack five sheets and the words blur into a glow.

Light is not one blob. Think of it as a stream of tiny packets of energy. The dark dots are the material's particles. Glass: few collisions, so most light passes. Concept 4 · Color

Why a banana looks yellow

White light is a mix of colors. An object bounces back some colors and soaks up the rest. The colors that reach your eye are the color you see.

Analogy

A bag of marbles

A red apple is a wall that bounces red marbles and swallows green and blue ones. Only red comes back to your eye, so the apple looks red.

Where it breaks: real objects reflect a blend of colors, not one pure marble. The blend still peaks in red, so your eye says "red."

Diagram

A prism splits white light

White light was never just white. It was a stack of colors traveling together. The prism bends each one by a different amount.

Concept 5 · Light is energy

Absorbed light becomes heat

Light carries energy. When a material absorbs it, the material's particles jiggle faster and the object gets warmer.

Analogy

100 coins of sunlight

Think of sunlight as 100 coins landing on a car hood. Reflected coins get thrown back out. Absorbed coins stay in the pocket, and that energy shows up as heat.

Build it

Engineering challenge: the sun shade

The problem: build a free-standing shade that keeps an ice cube cooler than an identical ice cube with no shade, under the same lamp.

Criteria: less meltwater than the uncovered control cube after 10 minutes. Least meltwater wins.

Constraints: 4 straws, 1 meter of tape, and one sheet each of foil, black paper, white paper, wax paper, and plastic wrap. The shade must stand on its own, not taped to the lamp.

  1. Try it in the game first. Play Beam Bounce at the bottom of this page. Notice which materials reflect a beam away and which absorb it.
  2. Plan. Sketch two different ideas on your design log. Label which parts reflect light away and which would absorb it if light got through.
  3. Build and test. Set your shaded cube and the bare control cube under the same lamp at the same time. After 10 minutes, pour off and measure each one's meltwater in mL.
  4. Improve. Change one thing, test again, and record it.

Print a design log for each team. A shade that reflects light away works differently than one that just blocks it and absorbs the heat itself.

Check yourself

Can you explain it?

Answer the big question from the top: why is a window see-through, and why does black get hotter than white?

Show a model answer

Window: glass is transparent. Light travels through it in straight lines, so you see a clear picture on the other side. A door is opaque, so the light is reflected or absorbed and none gets through.

Black vs. white: a black shirt absorbs almost all the light that lands on it. Absorbed light energy turns into thermal energy, so the shirt warms up. A white shirt reflects most of the light, so less energy stays behind.

Tutor notes: hands-on kit, misconceptions, pacing

Kit (about $10): a flashlight, wax paper, aluminum foil, black and white construction paper, clear plastic wrap, a CD or small prism, ice cubes, a sunny windowsill or a lamp. For the sun shade: 4 straws and a meter of tape.

  • Concept 1: make a shadow puppet, then move the flashlight closer and farther. Tape a pinhole in black paper and project a window onto a wall.
  • Concept 2: shine the flashlight on foil, plastic wrap, black paper, and white paper. Predict first, then sort.
  • Concept 3: layer wax paper over printed text, one sheet at a time, like the slider.
  • Concept 4: view colored objects through red, green, and blue plastic.
  • Concept 5: put identical ice cubes on black and white paper in a sunny window. Time them. Then run the sun shade challenge (about 30 minutes including the 10-minute test).

Common misconceptions to listen for:

  • "We see because light comes out of our eyes." Light travels into the eye after bouncing off things.
  • "Only mirrors reflect." Everything you can see is reflecting light, just in scattered directions.
  • "Absorbed light disappears." It becomes thermal energy.
  • "Black reflects black." Black objects reflect almost nothing, which is why they look dark.
  • "A shadow is a thing." A shadow is just the place the rays could not reach.

Pacing: about five 30-minute sessions, one per concept, with the check-yourself questions at the start of the next session as a warm-up.

Ask these out loud: What would change if the glass were thicker? Why is the inside of a car hotter than the outside? What color would the apple look under blue light, and why?
