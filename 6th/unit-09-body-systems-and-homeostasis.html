Body Systems & Homeostasis

Your body is about 30 trillion cells working as teams. Organ systems pass oxygen, food, and messages between them, and constantly adjust to keep your inside conditions steady, whether you are sleeping or sprinting.

Where we left off: Unit 7 showed how cells get energy and materials, and Unit 8 showed how many different kinds of living things there are. You may already have learned that many-celled organisms have specialized cells. This unit follows those cells into tissues, organs, and systems that work together, and that keep your body in balance using the same heat ideas from Unit 2 and the same senses that detect light from Unit 1.

Concept 1 · Working together

Cells, tissues, organs, systems

Similar cells form a tissue, tissues form an organ, and organs that share a job form an organ system. No system works alone: each one depends on the others.

Where this fits: this lesson is the organs-and-systems view of the body. Unit 7.4 covers the same body at the level of molecules and enzymes.

Analogy

The body is a city

Try it

Zoom out

Predict. Your body is built in levels. Put these in order from smallest to largest before you click: organ, cell, organism, tissue, organ system. Which level do you think the heart belongs to?

Do. Press each level button from Cell to Organism. For each one, read the sentence and look at how the picture changes: what is the new level made of?

Notice. Each level is made of many of the one before it: cells make tissue, tissues make an organ (the heart has muscle, nerve and lining tissue), organs team up as a system (the circulatory system), and the systems together make you.

Why it matters. When a body part fails, doctors ask at which level the problem is: a single cell (a mutation), a tissue (a torn muscle), an organ (a damaged heart), or a whole system. Knowing the levels tells you where to look.

Concept 2 · Delivery

Heart, blood, and lungs

The lungs add oxygen to blood and remove carbon dioxide. The heart pumps that blood to every cell. Muscles working harder need more oxygen, so heart rate and breathing rise together.

Analogy

A delivery loop

One full loop takes about a minute. Your heart makes roughly 100,000 beats a day.

Try it

Exercise and your heart

Predict. How many times does your heart beat each minute when you are sleeping, and when you sprint? Predict a number for each, then check.

Do. Press Sleeping, then Sitting, Walking, Jogging, and Sprinting. Watch the heartbeat trace speed up and read the beats per minute and beats per second.

Notice. The beat rate climbs from about 60 per minute asleep to about 190 when sprinting. The trace gets more crowded: the pattern stays the same, just faster. At 60 beats a minute the heart beats once per second.

Why it matters. Working muscles need more oxygen and nutrients, and they make more waste. The heart speeds up to deliver blood faster, with the lungs breathing faster to match. This is a feedback loop that keeps the body in balance.

Data

A jog, minute by minute

Teaching model. A fitter heart pumps more blood per beat, so it beats less often for the same job and recovers faster.

Concept 3 · Fuel

Digestion and nutrients

Digestion breaks food into pieces small enough to pass into the blood. Mechanical digestion (chewing, churning) tears food apart; chemical digestion (acids, enzymes) breaks it down into nutrients.

Analogy

A disassembly line

Food spends about 6 hours in the stomach and small intestine and up to a day in the large intestine.

Data

Length matters: the surface for absorbing

The small intestine is about 6 meters long, but its folds and tiny finger-like villi give it a surface area of roughly 30 m², about the size of a studio apartment. More surface means faster absorption, like a sponge versus a plate.

Concept 4 · Messages

Nerves, senses, and reaction time

Your nervous system collects information from senses, your brain interprets it, and nerves tell muscles what to do. Messages are electrochemical signals and travel faster than a cheetah.

Analogy

Hot-stove relay

A reflex skips the brain's full "thinking" step. The spinal cord reacts first and the brain finds out a moment later.

Try it

Reaction time test

Click to start. Wait for green, then click fast!

Data

Signal speeds

Fast nerve signals: up to ~120 m/s (270 mph). Slow pain signals: about 1 m/s. Sound: 343 m/s. Typical human reaction time to a visual signal is about 0.25 s, mostly brain processing time.

Concept 5 · Keeping balance

Homeostasis: the body's thermostat

Homeostasis is keeping conditions inside your body in a narrow, healthy range. It works by negative feedback: when something drifts too far one way, the body pushes it back the other way.

Analogy

A house thermostat

A thermostat does not "know" the right temperature. It compares the sensor to a set point and switches the furnace or AC on until the gap closes. Your body does the same: sensors report, the brain compares, and sweat glands or shivering muscles correct. Negative feedback means the response cancels the change.

Where it breaks: a house thermostat runs one loop. Your body runs hundreds at once, for temperature, water, sugar, and more.

Illustrative. Insulin tells cells to take up sugar. Type 1 diabetes occurs when the body doesn't make enough insulin, so people replace it by injection or pump.

Real world

Where does insulin medicine come from?

People with type 1 diabetes need insulin from outside the body. For many years it was taken from the pancreases of farm animals such as pigs and cows. Since the early 1980s, much of it has been made by microbes that were given the human insulin gene. The microbes grow in tanks and make the hormone as they live, and it is then purified.

This is biotechnology: using living cells as tiny factories. It is a medical use of DNA that you will study in Unit 8.8.

Optional extra · Staying healthy

Defending the body

Some tiny living things and viruses, called germs, can make you sick. Your body defends itself in layers: barriers keep germs out, white blood cells fight the ones that get in, and a vaccine trains those cells ahead of time.

Picture

Three layers of defense

  • Barriers: unbroken skin, sticky mucus in your nose, and acid in your stomach stop most germs. That is why a cut should be cleaned and covered.
  • White blood cells: these patrol the blood. They swallow germs or tag them so other cells can destroy them. After an infection, some remember that germ.
  • Vaccines: a vaccine shows your body a harmless piece or weakened form of a germ. Your white blood cells practice, so they act fast if the real germ shows up.
  • Fever: when you are sick, your body raises its temperature set point on purpose. Many germs grow more slowly when it is warmer. This is the same thermostat idea as homeostasis.

Fighting germs is a whole-body job done by cells and organs. Unit 7.4 in seventh grade is about the chemistry inside the body. Antibiotics help against bacteria but do not work on viruses.

Check yourself

Can you explain it?

You run hard on a hot day. Describe at least three body systems that respond and how each helps keep your body in balance.

Show a model answer

Muscles burn more fuel, so the respiratory system speeds up breathing to bring in oxygen and remove CO₂, and the circulatory system raises heart rate to deliver it. Heat builds up, so the nervous system detects it and tells sweat glands (in the skin) to release sweat, which cools you as it evaporates. Each response is negative feedback that pushes conditions back toward normal.

Words to know

tissue
A group of similar cells working together.
organ
A body part made of tissues doing a specific job.
organ system
Organs working together on a larger job.
circulatory system
Heart, vessels, and blood that transport materials.
enzyme
A protein that speeds up a chemical reaction, such as digestion.
neuron
A nerve cell that carries electrochemical signals.
reflex
A fast automatic response.
stimulus
A change that your body detects.
homeostasis
Keeping internal conditions steady.
feedback
When the result of a process changes the process itself.
Tutor notes: hands-on kit, misconceptions, pacing

Kit (about $10): stopwatch or phone timer, a 30 cm ruler, a zip bag with a sponge and water (digestion), a funnel and tubing or a balloon in a bottle (lung model), a pair of pantyhose with bread and soda (stomach squeezing), a thermometer.

  • Concept 1: trace around a student on butcher paper and place system "cards" on the outline.
  • Concept 2: measure resting pulse, then pulse after 1 minute of jumping jacks, and graph it. Build a balloon-and-bottle lung.
  • Concept 3: squeeze bread, a little water, and crackers in a zip bag to model mechanical and chemical digestion.
  • Concept 4: ruler-drop test: one partner drops, the other catches. Record the distance and convert to time.
  • Concept 5: record a cup of warm water's cooling versus one in an insulated sleeve. Discuss how the body adds a "furnace" and "air conditioner".

Common misconceptions:

  • "Food goes into the blood right away." It must be broken down first and absorbed in the small intestine.
  • "Blood is blue in veins." It is always red; veins just look blue through skin.
  • "The lungs are like balloons that push air." The diaphragm muscle pulls air in by expanding the chest.
  • "Homeostasis means nothing changes." Values wobble around a set point constantly.

Pacing: about five 30-minute sessions.
