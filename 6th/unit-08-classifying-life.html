Classifying Life

Scientists have named about 2 million kinds of living things, and probably millions more are still unknown. To keep track, they sort life into groups, like organizing a giant library. Learn the system and you can place any living thing on the tree of life.

Where we left off: Unit 7 showed that all life is made of cells, and that some living things are one cell and others are many. You may already have learned the difference between cells with a nucleus and cells without. We use exactly that feature to start sorting life into groups.

Concept 1 · Sorting life

From broad groups to one species

Scientists sort living things into nested groups, from huge to tiny: domain, kingdom, phylum, class, order, family, genus, species. Each living thing gets a two-part scientific name: genus + species.

There are millions of kinds of living things, so scientists need a system to organize them. Taxonomy sorts organisms into groups based on shared features and evolutionary relationships. The groups are nested, like boxes inside boxes, with the largest group first: domain, kingdom, phylum, class, order, family, genus, and species. A species is a group of organisms that can mate and have fertile offspring. Each kind of organism has a two-part scientific name, using the genus and species, such as Homo sapiens for humans. This system, called binomial nomenclature, was developed by Carl Linnaeus in the 1700s. Scientific names are in Latin and are the same worldwide, so scientists can be sure which organism is meant even when common names differ, as with a cougar, puma, and mountain lion.

Good to know: these groups are tools people made to organize what we see. Modern grouping follows evolutionary relationships (who shares a recent ancestor), not just traits you can see. Two animals can look alike and still be only distant relatives.

Concept 2 · The big groups

Three domains, six kingdoms

Life splits into three domains. One of them, Eukarya (cells with a nucleus), holds four kingdoms: protists, fungi, plants, animals. The other two, Bacteria and Archaea, are single cells without a nucleus.

Scientists group all life into three domains. Two of them, Bacteria and Archaea, are made of single cells with no nucleus. Archaea often live in extreme places, like hot springs or very salty water. The third, Eukarya, includes all organisms whose cells have a nucleus, and it is divided into four kingdoms. Protists are mostly single-celled and include amoebas and algae. Fungi, like mushrooms and molds, absorb nutrients from dead or living matter. Plants make their own food by photosynthesis. Animals are many-celled organisms that eat other organisms. Scientists decide which group a living thing belongs to by asking how many cells it has, whether those cells have a nucleus, and how it gets food.

Try it

Kingdom detective

Predict. Pick a mystery organism, say a mushroom. Before you answer any questions, guess its kingdom. Which of the three questions (nucleus? how many cells? how does it eat?) will matter most?

Do. Press a "Try" button to see a worked example, or press Start over and answer the three questions yourself for each of the five organisms. You may be told the kingdom before you answer all three.

Notice. Some questions end the hunt early: no nucleus means Bacteria or Archaea right away, and one cell with a nucleus means Protista. Only many-celled organisms with a nucleus need the food question to separate plants, animals and fungi. Mushroom and oak both have many cells; their food question tells them apart.

Why it matters. Scientists sort life by a few observable features, not by looks. Mushrooms look like plants but are closer to animals, and the key shows why: it digests food outside and absorbs it.

Concept 3 · Identifying living things

Dichotomous keys

A dichotomous key is a series of two-choice questions. Each answer sends you down one branch until you reach a name. Every question should be about something you can observe.

A dichotomous key is a tool for identifying an unknown organism. Dichotomous means "divided into two parts": at each step the key gives two choices about a physical trait, and your choice sends you to the next step or to a final name. The questions must be about features you can observe clearly, such as whether a leaf is needle-like or flat, or whether the leaf edge is smooth or toothed, rather than opinions like "pretty." Following the steps in order narrows down the possibilities until only one remains. Field guides, scientists, and park rangers use keys to identify trees, insects, birds, and rocks. You can also write your own key by looking for traits that split a group of objects into two smaller groups at each step.

Guess Who?

In the board game, each yes/no question halves the candidates. A good key does the same thing for species.

Try it

Identify a Michigan tree from its leaf

Predict. Look at Mystery leaf 1. Before you use the key, write down one thing you can see (shape, edge, needle or flat) that might help sort it.

Do. Choose a mystery leaf. Answer each yes or no question about the big leaf drawing and the key moves down one branch, lighting up your path. Keep going until it names a tree, then see whether it matches. Do all five leaves. Worked example: the pine has needles, bundled in fives, so Yes then Yes gives white pine.

Notice. Every question has exactly two answers, and each answer cuts out a whole group of trees. Five trees need at most three questions. If the key names the wrong tree, go back: one of your answers was probably not what you could really observe.

Why it matters. This is how field guides and species apps are built. A good key asks about things anyone can observe, in the same order every time, so two people with the same leaf get the same name.

Concept 4 · Animals with backbones

Five vertebrate groups

Animals with backbones fall into five groups: fish, amphibians, reptiles, birds, mammals. Think of a branching tree, not a ladder: each branch keeps the traits it inherited and adds its own.

Vertebrates are animals with a backbone. There are five main groups. Fish live in water, breathe with gills, and have scales. Amphibians, like frogs, spend part of life in water and part on land and have moist skin. Reptiles have dry, scaly skin and lay eggs with leathery shells. Birds have feathers, wings, and hard-shelled eggs, and they are warm-blooded. Mammals have hair or fur, feed their young milk, and are also warm-blooded. Scientists show how the groups are related using a branching tree. Each new branch keeps the features from earlier branches and adds new ones, which reflects shared ancestry. Vertebrates are a small share of animals, since most animal species, such as insects, are invertebrates with no backbone.

Analogy

A branching tree

Branches share older traits (backbone, then limbs, then shelled eggs) and add new ones (feathers, fur).

Where it breaks: the picture is simplified; real trees have many more branches. "New" does not mean "better." Each group is a branch that survived with its own mix of traits; a fish is not an older model of a mammal.

Concept 5 · Biodiversity

Why variety matters

Biodiversity is the variety of life in a place. More variety makes ecosystems more stable, and bigger habitats hold more species.

Biodiversity is the variety of living things in an area, including the number of different species, the genetic variety within each species, and the variety of habitats. Places with high biodiversity, like a coral reef or a rainforest, usually have many different kinds of producers, consumers, and decomposers. This makes the ecosystem more stable, because if one species declines, others can fill its role. Larger habitats can generally support more species, and when habitats are broken into small pieces by roads and buildings, many species disappear. Biodiversity also helps people through food, clean water, medicines, and materials. Michigan has a wide range of living things, from dune grasses and wetland plants to forest mammals and Great Lakes fish, and protecting their habitats protects the variety of life.

Check yourself

Can you explain it?

A mystery organism has cells with a nucleus, is made of many cells, and absorbs food from the dead log it grows on. What kingdom is it, and how do you know?

Show a model answer

Fungi. A nucleus means it's in Eukarya (not bacteria or archaea). Being made of many cells rules out most protists. It doesn't make its own food like a plant and it doesn't eat food like an animal; it absorbs nutrients from its surroundings, like a mushroom on a log.

Tutor notes: hands-on kit, misconceptions, pacing

Kit (about $10): a bag of mixed buttons, a hand lens, a field guide or the Seek/iNaturalist app, a clipboard, index cards, string, a meter-square frame made from PVC or yarn.

  • Concept 1: sort buttons many ways, then make a nested "address" for each.
  • Concept 2: sort 12 photo cards of organisms into kingdoms using the detective questions.
  • Concept 3: write a dichotomous key for the contents of a pencil case, then swap with a partner to test it.
  • Concept 4: sort animal photo cards into five groups and justify each choice with a trait.
  • Concept 5: mark a square meter in a yard and count the different species you find.

Common misconceptions:

  • "Whales and dolphins are fish." They breathe air, have hair, and nurse young, so they are mammals.
  • "Bats are birds." They are mammals.
  • "Fungi are plants." They are closer to animals and don't photosynthesize.
  • "A species is just a name." It is a real group that interbreeds.

Pacing: about five 30-minute sessions.
