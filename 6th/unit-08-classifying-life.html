Classifying Life

Scientists have named about 2 million kinds of living things, and probably millions more are still unknown. To keep track, they sort life into groups, like organizing a giant library. Learn the system and you can place any living thing on the tree of life.

Where we left off: Unit 7 showed that all life is made of cells, and that some living things are one cell and others are many. You may already have learned the difference between cells with a nucleus and cells without. We use exactly that feature to start sorting life into groups.

Concept 1 · Sorting life

From broad groups to one species

Scientists sort living things into nested groups, from huge to tiny: domain, kingdom, phylum, class, order, family, genus, species. Each living thing gets a two-part scientific name: genus + species.

Good to know: these groups are tools people made to organize what we see. Modern grouping follows evolutionary relationships (who shares a recent ancestor), not just traits you can see. Two animals can look alike and still be only distant relatives.

Concept 2 · The big groups

Three domains, six kingdoms

Life splits into three domains. One of them, Eukarya (cells with a nucleus), holds four kingdoms: protists, fungi, plants, animals. The other two, Bacteria and Archaea, are single cells without a nucleus.

Concept 3 · Identifying living things

Dichotomous keys

A dichotomous key is a series of two-choice questions. Each answer sends you down one branch until you reach a name. Every question should be about something you can observe.

Analogy

Guess Who?

In the board game, each yes/no question halves the candidates. A good key does the same thing for species.

Concept 4 · Animals with backbones

Five vertebrate groups

Animals with backbones fall into five groups: fish, amphibians, reptiles, birds, mammals. Think of a branching tree, not a ladder: each branch keeps the traits it inherited and adds its own.

Analogy

A branching tree

Branches share older traits (backbone, then limbs, then shelled eggs) and add new ones (feathers, fur).

Where it breaks: the picture is simplified; real trees have many more branches. "New" does not mean "better." Each group is a branch that survived with its own mix of traits; a fish is not an older model of a mammal.

Diagram

Vertebrate family tree

Simplified. Real family trees show birds are a kind of reptile. Many fish, amphibians, and reptiles are cold-blooded; birds and mammals are warm-blooded.

Concept 5 · Biodiversity

Why variety matters

Biodiversity is the variety of life in a place. More variety makes ecosystems more stable, and bigger habitats hold more species.

Analogy

A sports team

A cornfield with one crop can be wiped out by a single disease. A prairie with 100 plants usually survives.

Check yourself

Can you explain it?

A mystery organism has cells with a nucleus, is made of many cells, and absorbs food from the dead log it grows on. What kingdom is it, and how do you know?

Show a model answer

Fungi. A nucleus means it's in Eukarya (not bacteria or archaea). Being made of many cells rules out most protists. It doesn't make its own food like a plant and it doesn't eat food like an animal; it absorbs nutrients from its surroundings, like a mushroom on a log.

Words to know

The science of naming and classifying life.

Tutor notes: hands-on kit, misconceptions, pacing

Kit (about $10): a bag of mixed buttons, a hand lens, a field guide or the Seek/iNaturalist app, a clipboard, index cards, string, a meter-square frame made from PVC or yarn.

  • Concept 1: sort buttons many ways, then make a nested "address" for each.
  • Concept 2: sort 12 photo cards of organisms into kingdoms using the detective questions.
  • Concept 3: write a dichotomous key for the contents of a pencil case, then swap with a partner to test it.
  • Concept 4: sort animal photo cards into five groups and justify each choice with a trait.
  • Concept 5: mark a square meter in a yard and count the different species you find.

Common misconceptions:

  • "Whales and dolphins are fish." They breathe air, have hair, and nurse young, so they are mammals.
  • "Bats are birds." They are mammals.
  • "Fungi are plants." They are closer to animals and don't photosynthesize.
  • "A species is just a name." It is a real group that interbreeds.

Pacing: about five 30-minute sessions.
