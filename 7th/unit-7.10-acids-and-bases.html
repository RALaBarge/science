Acids & Bases

Lemon juice is sour, soap feels slippery, and red cabbage juice changes color in both. This unit follows one number, pH, from the stomach to the swimming pool: what it measures, how to read it with a color change, why every step on the scale is a tenfold jump, and what happens when an acid meets a base. Use the fair-test steps from Unit 0: How Science Works whenever you compare two liquids.

Acid-base reactions are one of the most common kinds. This unit gives you a way to measure one property of a liquid, how acidic it is, before and after a reaction.

Concept 1 · Acids, bases and the pH scale

A scale from 0 to 14

Liquids can be sorted by how acidic or basic they are. The pH scale gives that a number: below 7 is acidic, 7 is neutral (pure water), above 7 is basic. Never taste or sniff an unknown liquid to find out.

Analogy

A tug-of-war with a flag in the middle

Think of pH 7 as a flag at the center of a rope. Acids pull it toward 0 and bases pull it toward 14. Pure water is a tie, so the flag stays at 7.

The analogy breaks in one way: a liquid is not pulled by two teams. The number comes from how many hydrogen ions it has, which Concept 3 explains.

Concept 2 · Indicators

Reading pH with a color change

An indicator is a dye that changes color with pH. Litmus is red in acid and blue in base. Red cabbage juice shows a whole rainbow, so it tells you roughly how acidic or basic a liquid is. A pH meter gives a number, and pH paper gives a color you match to a chart.

Analogy

A dye with moods

The purple molecules in red cabbage change shape when hydrogen ions are plentiful or scarce, and the new shape absorbs different colors of light. You are seeing the molecule change its shape.

Not every dye does this. Some food dyes keep the same color at every pH, so they cannot be used as indicators.

Concept 3 · Hydrogen ions

Each step on the scale is ten times

In water, an acid adds hydrogen ions (H+) and a base adds hydroxide ions (OH-). pH counts hydrogen ions on a logarithmic scale: a liquid with pH 3 has 10 times as many as pH 4 and 100 times as many as pH 5.

Analogy

Earthquake scale, again

Like the magnitude scale in Unit 6, one step is not one more of the same amount. It is ten times as much. A pH 2 lemon juice does not have 2 times more hydrogen ions than pH 4 vinegar; it has 100 times more.

Where it breaks: for pH, a lower number means more hydrogen ions, the opposite of how most scales work.

Real world

Why small pH changes matter

Blood stays between about 7.35 and 7.45. A drop of only half a unit would be a medical emergency. Ocean surface water is about 8.1, and a fall of 0.1 means about 26% more hydrogen ions. Corals and shellfish notice.

Concept 4 · Acid + base

Mixing an acid with a base

When an acid and a base react, they neutralize each other. The hydrogen ions and hydroxide ions join to make water, and the other ions make a salt. Add a strong base to a strong acid a little at a time and the pH rises slowly, then jumps near 7.

Analogy

Two teams that cancel

Think of H+ as one team and OH- as the other. Each pair that meets makes a water molecule and drops out of the game. When the teams are equal, the liquid is neutral. If one team has more players, the leftover ones decide whether the liquid is acidic or basic.

Check yourself

Can you explain it?

A pool test strip shows a pH of 6. The pool should be 7.2 to 7.8. Is the water acidic or basic, would adding a small amount of baking soda or vinegar help, and how would you check that your fix worked?

Show a model answer

Acidic, since 6 is below 7. A small amount of a base (baking soda, or a pool pH increaser) would neutralize some acid and raise the pH; vinegar would lower it. Add a little, mix, wait, and test again so you change one thing at a time and can see which change worked (Unit 0).

Words to know

acid
A substance that adds hydrogen ions (H+) when dissolved in water.
base
A substance that adds hydroxide ions (OH-) or removes hydrogen ions in water.
pH
A number that tells how acidic or basic a liquid is, on a scale of about 0 to 14.
neutral
Neither acidic nor basic; pH 7.
indicator
A dye that changes color depending on the pH.
neutralization
The reaction of an acid with a base that makes water and a salt.
salt
The other product of neutralization; table salt is one kind.
logarithmic scale
A scale where each step is ten times the one before.
Tutor notes: hands-on kit, misconceptions, pacing

Kit (about $8): half a red cabbage, hot water, clear cups, droppers or spoons, lemon juice, vinegar, club soda, baking soda, hand soap, antacid tablet, distilled water, pH paper (optional), goggles.

  • Concept 1: Sort labeled household items by predicted pH, then check with cabbage juice. No tasting.
  • Concept 2: Make cabbage juice (blend or soak, strain). Test each liquid. Build a color chart from the results.
  • Concept 3: Dilute vinegar 1 part in 10, then 1 in 100, and test each. Compare the color.
  • Concept 4: Add baking soda solution to vinegar with cabbage juice in the cup and watch the color run from red toward blue. Do it with drops.

Common misconceptions:

  • "All acids are dangerous." Lemon juice and vinegar are safe to eat; strength matters.
  • "Bases are safe because they are not acids." Strong bases such as drain cleaner burn too.
  • "pH 6 is a little less acidic than pH 5." It has 10 times fewer hydrogen ions.
  • "Neutral means nothing is in it." Salt water can be neutral.

Pacing: about four 30-minute sessions. Method link: in Concept 4, change only one thing per trial (amount of base) and keep the acid the same.
