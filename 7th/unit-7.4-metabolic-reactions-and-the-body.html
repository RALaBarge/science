Metabolic Reactions & the Body

Your body is a chemistry lab with trillions of tiny reactors. Last year you met cells and body systems (Units 7 and 9); in 7.2 you learned that atoms rearrange in reactions. Now we connect them: how a sandwich becomes you. Use the evidence steps from Unit 0: How Science Works when we read the data.

Where we left off: Unit 7.2 and 7.3 showed that reactions rearrange atoms and release or absorb energy. You may already have learned about cells (Unit 7) and body systems (Unit 9). Now we see that your body runs thousands of those chemical reactions, and that digestion and cellular respiration are how food becomes matter and energy for you.

Concept 1 · Breaking food down

Digestion: from sandwich to small molecules

Digestion uses physical (chewing, churning) and chemical (enzymes, acid) changes to break big food molecules into small ones that can cross into the blood.

Analogy

Taking apart a LEGO castle

Food molecules are huge builds. Your gut takes them apart into single bricks (sugars, amino acids, fatty acids) so the blood can carry them.

Concept 2 · Cellular respiration

Releasing energy from food in your cells

Cellular respiration is a chemical reaction in cells: sugar + oxygen → carbon dioxide + water + usable energy. It is exothermic, and it is the reason you breathe in oxygen and exhale CO₂.

Analogy

A campfire without flames

Burning wood and respiration use the same reactants and make the same products. Cells just do it slowly and in steps so the energy is captured instead of released as a fire.

Concept 3 · Delivery system

Lungs and heart deliver the reactants

Lungs bring oxygen in and let CO₂ out across thin, wet surfaces (gas exchange). The heart pumps blood to carry oxygen and nutrients to every cell and carry CO₂ away.

Analogy

A delivery fleet

The blood is a fleet of trucks. Lungs are the loading dock for oxygen; the heart is the dispatcher pumping trucks out; cells are the customers.

Concept 4 · Systems working together

No system works alone

Digestive, respiratory, circulatory, and others depend on each other. A failure in one affects the rest (Unit 9: homeostasis).

Analogy

A restaurant kitchen

Digestive = kitchen prep; circulatory = waiters; respiratory = air supply; nervous = manager. Remove one and dinner fails.

Concept 5 · Matter and energy

Where does the food go?

Food supplies matter (atoms used to build and repair you) and energy (released by respiration). Atoms are conserved: they become body, or leave as CO₂, water, or waste.

Analogy

A building supply truck

Some bricks become new walls (growth), some are burned for heat (respiration), some are leftover and removed.

Check yourself

Can you explain it?

A student says "the food I eat disappears, because I only weigh a bit more each year." Use conservation of mass and cellular respiration to correct this.

Show a model answer

Atoms do not disappear. Most of the carbon and hydrogen in food is combined with oxygen in cellular respiration and leaves as CO₂ and water (which you exhale or excrete). Only a small part becomes new body matter, so weight changes slowly. The total number of atoms is conserved.

Tutor notes: hands-on kit, misconceptions, pacing

Kit (about $8): bread, iodine or starch test (adult), zip bag + crackers + water (stomach model), a straw + limewater or bromothymol blue (CO₂ test), a stopwatch, stethoscope or phone for pulse.

  • Concept 1: Chew a cracker 2 minutes and notice the sweetness (amylase).
  • Concept 2: Squeeze crackers with water in a bag to model the stomach.
  • Concept 3: Exhale through a straw into bromothymol blue and watch it turn yellow.
  • Concept 4: Measure resting and exercising pulse and breaths per minute; graph it.
  • Concept 5: Draw a "food journey" with the atoms labeled.

Common misconceptions:

  • "Food turns to energy." Energy is released by reactions; matter is not converted into energy here.
  • "We breathe in only oxygen." Air is 78% nitrogen.
  • "Plants breathe CO₂ and we breathe O₂ so they are opposite." Plants respire too.

Pacing: about five 30-minute sessions. Method link: have students form a hypothesis about how pulse changes with exercise, plan a fair test, and use a graph and CER (Unit 0).
