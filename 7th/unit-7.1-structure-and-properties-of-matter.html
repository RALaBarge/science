Concept 1 · Atoms and elements

The building blocks of everything

An atom is the smallest unit of an element. It has a tiny nucleus of protons (+) and neutrons (neutral), surrounded by electrons (−). The number of protons decides which element it is.

Analogy

LEGO bricks

One box of bricks can build a car, a house, or a robot. About 118 kinds of atoms build every material (air, water, rock, you); the atoms are the same, only their arrangement changes. Where it breaks: LEGO bricks snap together the same way every time. Atoms bond by electron rules, and the same atoms can make soft graphite or hard diamond.

Data

What is everything made of?

Percent by mass (or by number of atoms for the universe). The universe is mostly hydrogen and helium; Earth's crust is mostly oxygen and silicon; your body is mostly oxygen, carbon, and hydrogen.

Real world

How small is an atom?

An atom is about 0.2 nanometers wide. About 300,000 atoms in a row would span the width of a human hair, and a single drop of water holds around 1.7 sextillion (1,700,000,000,000,000,000,000) water molecules. The nucleus is about 100,000 times smaller than the atom: if the atom were a football stadium, the nucleus would be a pea at the center, and the rest is mostly empty space where electrons move.

Concept 2 · The periodic table

Elements in an organized map

Elements are arranged by atomic number (protons) in the periodic table. Elements in the same column (group) behave alike because they have the same number of outer electrons.

Analogy

A seating chart by behavior

Students in the same column share a habit, just as elements in a periodic-table group share outer electrons. Mendeleev built the first table in 1869 by sorting known elements by mass and grouping similar behaviors. He left gaps and predicted elements nobody had found yet; later, gallium and germanium filled them. That is a hypothesis tested by evidence (Unit 0).

Data

Outer electrons across a row

Outer (valence) electrons for period 3. Elements with 1–2 outer electrons give them away (metals); elements with 6–7 tend to gain (nonmetals); argon's full shell of 8 makes it a noble gas that rarely reacts.

Concept 3 · Compounds and molecules

Atoms join to make new substances

When atoms bond they form molecules or compounds. A compound always has a fixed ratio of atoms (water is always H₂O), and its properties are very different from the elements it contains.

Analogy

Letters make words

26 letters make every word in English. About 100 elements make every substance.

Concept 4 · Identifying substances

Properties are a substance's fingerprint

Characteristic properties (density, melting point, magnetism, conductivity, solubility) do not depend on the amount of the sample. Scientists use them to identify an unknown substance.

Analogy

Concept 5 · How we know

Models change when evidence changes

Nobody has seen an atom with a simple microscope, so scientists built models and tested them. When a famous experiment surprised them, the model changed. Our picture of the atom is a good example of science correcting itself.

Diagram

Five models of the atom

Try it

Rutherford's gold foil experiment

In 1909 Geiger and Marsden fired alpha particles at gold foil. Most went through, but about 1 in 8,000 bounced back at large angles, "like firing a cannon shell at tissue paper and it comes back." Rutherford concluded that the atom's positive charge is packed into a tiny, dense nucleus. (Our simulation exaggerates how often big deflections happen so you can see them.)

Check yourself

Can you explain it?

Water (H₂O) is made of hydrogen, a flammable gas, and oxygen, a gas that supports burning. Yet water puts out fires. Explain why a compound's properties can be so different from the elements it contains.

Show a model answer

When atoms bond, their electrons rearrange and a new substance with a new structure forms. The properties come from that new arrangement, not from averaging the original elements. Hydrogen and oxygen atoms joined as H₂O molecules behave like neither element: water is a liquid at room temperature and is not flammable.

Tutor notes: hands-on kit, misconceptions, pacing

Kit (about $8): a bag of different colored beads or LEGO bricks, pipe cleaners and gumdrops or marshmallows with toothpicks (molecule models), a magnet, a small scale, a graduated cylinder or measuring cup, a battery and bulb circuit (conductivity), and index cards for an "element bingo" game.

  • Concept 1: Build atoms of H, C, O from colored beads (nucleus and shell).
  • Concept 2: Build the periodic table from index cards: each student gets an element and finds the neighbor with similar properties.
  • Concept 3: Build H₂O, CO₂, CH₄ with gumdrops and toothpicks; count the atoms.
  • Concept 4: Identify three unknown white powders using a magnet, water test, and a conductivity test.
  • Concept 5: Rutherford analog: roll marbles at a hidden shape under a cloth and map it from the bounces.

Common misconceptions:

  • "Atoms are tiny solid balls." They are mostly empty space.
  • "Atoms of different elements are different sizes only." They differ in protons.
  • "A molecule is the same as an atom." Molecules are joined atoms.
  • "A mixture and a compound are the same." A compound has a fixed ratio and new properties.

Pacing: about five 30-minute sessions. Method link: after the mystery-substance test, ask which property gave the strongest evidence and why you needed more than one test (Unit 0, Concept 5).
