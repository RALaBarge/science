Light & the Electromagnetic Spectrum

In Unit 1: Light and Matter you learned what light does when it meets objects. Now zoom out: the light you see is a tiny slice of a huge family of waves that includes radio, microwaves, and X-rays. All of them are the same kind of thing, with different wavelength and energy. As in Unit 0: How Science Works, we describe each wave with measurements, not just words.

Where we left off: Unit 1 taught reflect, absorb, and transmit for visible light. You may already have learned that colors come from reflected wavelengths. This bridge unit shows that visible light is just a thin slice of the electromagnetic spectrum, and prepares you for waves in Grade 8.

Concept 1 · A family of waves

Light is one slice of a huge spectrum

Electromagnetic (EM) waves travel through empty space at the speed of light (300,000 km/s). They differ in wavelength and frequency: longer wavelength means lower frequency and less energy.

Analogy

A piano keyboard

Low notes are slow, long waves; high notes are fast, short waves. The visible light "octave" is a single small part of a very large keyboard.

Concept 2 · Measuring waves

Wavelength, frequency, amplitude

Wavelength is crest-to-crest distance. Frequency is waves per second. Amplitude is wave height (brightness). Speed = wavelength × frequency, and for light the speed is fixed.

Analogy

Jump rope waves

Shake the rope faster (higher frequency) and the bumps get closer together (shorter wavelength). Shake harder (higher amplitude) and the bumps get taller.

Concept 3 · Reflect, absorb, transmit

What a material does with each wave

Different materials reflect, absorb, or transmit different wavelengths. Glass transmits visible light but absorbs much UV; metal reflects radio waves; dark cloth absorbs most visible light.

Analogy

Sieves of different sizes

A screen door blocks mosquitoes but lets air through: the mesh size matters. A material can also let some electromagnetic waves through and block others, but the rule is usually the wave's energy and the material's electrons, not a physical hole. Where it breaks: light does not need a hole to pass through glass.

Concept 4 · Why some waves are harmful

Shorter wavelength carries more energy

Higher-frequency waves carry more energy per photon. UV, X-rays, and gamma rays have enough to damage cells; visible, IR, and radio do not. Amount (brightness) also matters.

Analogy

Pebbles and bullets

A flood of pebbles (radio) feels like nothing. One speeding bullet (gamma) can do damage. Energy per hit matters more than the number of hits.

Concept 5 · Mixing light

Light colors add, pigments subtract

Screens make colors by adding red, green, and blue light. Paints and dyes make colors by absorbing (subtracting) some wavelengths and reflecting the rest.

Analogy

Stage lights vs. paint

Shining red and green stage lights on one spot makes yellow. Mixing red and green paint makes a muddy brown because each paint absorbs more.

Check yourself

Can you explain it?

A friend says "UV is just another kind of light, so it should be as safe as the light from a lamp." Use wavelength and energy to respond, and name one way to reduce UV exposure.

Show a model answer

UV and visible light are both EM waves but UV has a shorter wavelength and higher frequency, so each photon carries more energy, enough to damage skin cells. Sunscreen absorbs UV, and clothing, hats, and shade block it.

Tutor notes: hands-on kit, misconceptions, pacing

Kit (about $10): prism or CD, a UV-sensitive bead set ($6), a phone camera and TV remote (shows IR), colored cellophane, flashlights, red/green/blue LEDs or a color-mixing app, sunscreen paper.

  • Concept 1: Split light with a prism or CD and sketch the order of colors.
  • Concept 2: Use a phone camera to see a TV remote's infrared.
  • Concept 3: Test UV beads under windows, sunglasses, and sunscreen.
  • Concept 4: Mix colored flashlight beams on a wall and then paints on paper.
  • Concept 5: Make a "spectrum ruler" on a long strip of paper with the bands to scale.

Common misconceptions:

  • "Light is the only EM wave." It is a sliver.
  • "Radio waves are sound." They are EM waves; sound needs matter.
  • "Only UV from the sun is risky." Tanning beds emit UV too.
  • "Mixing colored lights works like paint." Light adds; paint subtracts.

Pacing: about five 30-minute sessions. Method link: have students form hypotheses about which materials block UV (Unit 0), test with UV beads, and record data in a table.
