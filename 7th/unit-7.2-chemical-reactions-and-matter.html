Chemical Reactions & Matter

A match burns, an egg turns solid, iron turns to rust. In each case, new substances form. In this unit you learn how to tell a chemical change from a physical one, why the total mass never changes, and what the atoms are doing. Use the fair-test steps from Unit 0: How Science Works to design each investigation.

Where we left off: Unit 7.1 showed that atoms build molecules and that substances have characteristic properties. You may already have learned the difference between a physical change (Unit 10) and the idea that heating or cooling changes state. Now we meet chemical change: atoms rearranging into new substances.

Concept 1 · Physical vs. chemical

Did a new substance form?

In a physical change the substance stays the same (just its form, shape, or state changes). In a chemical change (a chemical reaction) atoms re-bond and new substances with new properties form.

Analogy

Cutting vs. cooking

You can't uncook an egg: the proteins have changed shape permanently.

Concept 2 · Investigating reactions

Collect evidence like a chemist

Chemists observe carefully and record several kinds of evidence. A reaction can be exothermic (releases heat) or endothermic (absorbs heat), and some mixtures look dramatic but are only physical.

Analogy

A detective's notebook

Write what you see (observations), not what you think: "tiny bubbles rise from the powder" rather than "it's making gas." Conclusions come after the evidence (Unit 0).

Observation: tiny bubbles, temperature dropped 4 °C, powder disappeared. Inference: a gas was produced, and the reaction absorbed heat.

Concept 3 · Conservation of mass

Matter is never created or destroyed

In a chemical reaction the total mass stays the same. If the mass seems to change, atoms (often as a gas) have entered or left your system. This is the law of conservation of mass.

Analogy

Rebuilding a LEGO tower

Atoms are the bricks. A reaction rebuilds them in a new pattern, never losing any.

Concept 4 · What the atoms do

Atoms rearrange, they don't change

In a reaction, bonds break and new bonds form, but the atoms are the same. A chemical equation shows reactants → products, and the number of each kind of atom must balance on both sides.

Analogy

Square dancers

Dancers swap partners: bonds break and form. Same number of dancers on the floor.

Concept 5 · Making new substances

Synthetic materials from natural ones

People use chemical reactions to make synthetic materials (soap, plastics, medicines, fabrics) from natural starting materials. The new substances have different properties than what they came from, which is why they are useful.

Analogy

Baking a cake

Eggs, flour, and sugar are very different from the cake that comes out. Reacting and rearranging gives a new thing you could not get by just mixing.

Oil + a strong base (lye) is a very different pair from soap: fat and lye react to make soap and glycerin.

Check yourself

Can you explain it?

A student heats a sealed jar containing steel wool and air, and the total mass stays the same. She then burns steel wool on a scale in the open and the mass goes up. Explain both results using the law of conservation of mass.

Show a model answer

In the sealed jar no atoms can leave or enter, so the mass can't change, even though iron reacts with oxygen inside. When steel wool burns in the open, iron combines with oxygen from the air to form iron oxide; the oxygen atoms added from the air make the product heavier than the iron alone. Mass is conserved overall (iron + oxygen = iron oxide).

Words to know

physical change
A change in form that makes no new substance.
chemical change
A change that forms new substances.
reactant
A substance that goes into a reaction.
product
A substance made by a reaction.
precipitate
A solid that forms from a reaction in a liquid.
exothermic
Releases thermal energy to the surroundings.
endothermic
Absorbs thermal energy from the surroundings.
conservation of mass
Total mass stays the same in a closed system.
synthetic
Made by people through chemical reactions.
coefficient
The number in front of a formula showing how many units.
Tutor notes: hands-on kit, misconceptions, pacing

Kit (about $10): baking soda, white vinegar, zip bags, a kitchen scale, steel wool, milk, droppers, clear cups, a thermometer, red cabbage juice (an indicator), safety goggles. (Copper sulfate demonstration, if used, is for the adult to run.)

  • Concept 1: Sort 20 photo cards of changes into physical and chemical.
  • Concept 2: Run all five mixtures, recording a clue chart and temperature every 30 seconds.
  • Concept 3: Weigh baking soda + vinegar in an open cup and in a sealed zip bag.
  • Concept 4: Use colored beads to model reactant and product molecules and make a balanced equation.
  • Concept 5: Make soap or slime from a kit and compare it with the ingredients.

Common misconceptions:

  • "Dissolving is a chemical reaction." Salt in water is physical: the salt is recoverable.
  • "Burning destroys matter." The atoms become gases and ash.
  • "Bubbles always mean a reaction." Boiling and opening a soda are physical.
  • "A bigger flame means more mass lost." Mass is conserved if you count the gases.

Pacing: about five 30-minute sessions. Method link: have students write a hypothesis before each mixture (Unit 0, Concept 3), and decide which clue is evidence versus assumption.
