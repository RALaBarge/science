Concept 1 · Exothermic and endothermic

Some reactions release heat, some absorb it

An exothermic reaction releases energy to its surroundings (they get warmer). An endothermic reaction absorbs energy (they get colder). Energy is moved, not made.

Analogy

Downhill and uphill

Rolling down gives off energy. Pushing up takes it in.

Concept 2 · Why energy changes

Break bonds (costs), form bonds (pays)

Breaking bonds takes energy in; forming new bonds releases energy. If more is released than was put in, the reaction is exothermic; if less, it is endothermic.

Analogy

Cost and refund

Think of breaking bonds as paying to take apart old LEGO builds, and forming bonds as getting money back for new ones. A positive balance means leftover energy, which shows up as heat.

If you pay 5 and get 8 back, you gain 3 (exothermic). If you pay 8 and get 5 back, you are down 3 (endothermic).

Concept 3 · Reaction rate

Why things react faster when warm

Reactions need particles to collide with enough energy. Higher temperature, more surface area, and higher concentration all mean more (and harder) collisions, so a faster reaction rate.

Analogy

A crowded dance floor

More people and more speed means more bumps per minute. Cold, slow dancers rarely collide hard enough to matter.

Concept 4 · Engineering a hand warmer

Design challenge: a safe, warm hand warmer

Engineers use criteria (what it must do) and constraints (limits like cost and safety). Here: warm for hours, but not hot enough to burn.

Analogy

Goldilocks

Too cold is useless, too hot is dangerous. We want "just right": about 40 to 55 °C for a few hours.

Concept 5 · Test, improve, repeat

Improving designs one change at a time

Great designs come from iteration: change one variable, test, compare to the criteria, repeat. Changing many things at once hides which change mattered (Unit 0, fair tests).

Analogy

Tuning a recipe

Bakers adjust one ingredient per batch. If you change flour, sugar, and time together, you will not know what fixed the cake.

Data

Trial 4 changed only the amount of salt, so we know salt caused the improvement.

Tutor notes: hands-on kit, misconceptions, pacing

Kit (about $10): baking soda, citric acid, calcium chloride ice melt (adult handles), a thermometer, zip bags, iron powder and salt (or steel wool + salt), vermiculite, fizz tablets, clear cups, timer.

  • Concept 1: Measure temperature while mixing baking soda + citric acid (cold) and calcium chloride + water (warm).
  • Concept 2: Time fizz tablets whole vs crushed, cold vs warm water.
  • Concept 3: Build a hand warmer from iron, salt, water and measure the peak.
  • Concept 4: Run three trials changing one variable each; score them.
  • Concept 5: Use a decision table to pick the final design.

Common misconceptions:

  • "Cold packs produce cold." They absorb heat from your hand.
  • "Reactions that release energy create it." Energy was stored in bonds.
  • "Heating always helps." Some reactions or materials can become unsafe.

Pacing: about five 30-minute sessions. Method link: have students write a hypothesis before each trial ("If I double the salt, then…") using Unit 0; discuss variables kept the same.
