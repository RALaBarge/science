Ecosystem Dynamics

Sixth grade showed you who eats whom (Unit 12: Ecosystems and Food Webs). Now ask the harder question: what makes populations rise and fall, and what happens when a forest burns, a storm hits, or a new species arrives? Use data and the CER habit from Unit 0: How Science Works.

Where we left off: Unit 7.5 followed matter and energy through producers, consumers, and decomposers. You may already have learned about food webs and populations (Unit 12). This unit asks what makes ecosystems change: competition, limits, disturbances, and biodiversity.

Concept 1 · Biotic and abiotic

Living and nonliving parts interact

Biotic factors are living (or once living); abiotic factors are nonliving (sunlight, water, temperature, soil). Together they make up the ecosystem.

Analogy

A school

Students and teachers are the biotic parts; the building, schedule, heat, and lunch menu are abiotic. Remove the heat in winter and attendance changes!

Concept 2 · Relationships

Competition, predation, and teamwork

Organisms compete for resources, prey on each other, or form mutually helpful (mutualism), parasitic, or commensal partnerships. These shape who can live where.

Analogy

Sharing a lunchroom

Two students want the same last slice (competition). One steals another's fries (predation-like). Two classmates swap food so both are happy (mutualism).

Concept 3 · Carrying capacity

Resources limit population size

A population can grow quickly at first, but food, water, space, and disease set the carrying capacity, the largest number the environment can support.

Analogy

A restaurant with 50 seats

Sixty customers cannot all sit down. Some leave or wait: the population stops growing even if more are coming.

Data

Deer population on an island

Reindeer introduced to St. Matthew Island (1944) grew to thousands, overate lichen, and crashed. A real example of overshoot.

Concept 4 · Change over time

Fires, floods, and new arrivals

A disturbance (fire, flood, storm, disease, human development, a new species) changes an ecosystem. Many ecosystems recover through succession: pioneers first, then a mature community.

Analogy

Rebuilding after a flood

First, cleanup crews (fast weedy plants), then houses (shrubs), finally the full neighborhood (forest).

Concept 5 · Variety is strength

Biodiversity keeps ecosystems stable

Biodiversity is the variety of life. More kinds of organisms means more backup plans: if one species fails, others fill the role.

Analogy

A diversified investment

One crop in a field can be wiped out by one disease (the Irish potato famine). A mix of crops spreads the risk.

Check yourself

Can you explain it?

After a wildfire, a hillside is covered by grass in year 2 and shrubs by year 10. Explain succession and name two abiotic and two biotic factors that change.

Show a model answer

Fire clears the area (disturbance); fast-growing pioneer plants like grasses return first, then shrubs, then trees as soil builds. Abiotic: sunlight reaching the ground, soil nutrients, moisture. Biotic: plants, insects, animals returning.

Tutor notes: hands-on kit, misconceptions, pacing

Kit (about $6): a pond water sample (or a bagged soil sample), magnifier, graph paper, dice, beans/paper clips for a predator-prey game, cards for relationships.

  • Concept 1: Quadrat survey: count plants in 1 m² squares in two places and compare.
  • Concept 2: Play a predator-prey simulation with dice and graph the rounds.
  • Concept 3: Draw a food web, then remove one species and predict effects.
  • Concept 4: Create a succession timeline with pictures.
  • Concept 5: Compare biodiversity in a lawn and a wild corner of a yard.

Common misconceptions:

  • "Predators are bad for prey populations." They keep them healthy and in balance.
  • "An ecosystem always returns to how it was." It may become something else.
  • "All invasive species are dangerous." Many are, but not all.

Pacing: about five 30-minute sessions. Method link: have students collect their own data, pick a fair comparison (Unit 0), then write a claim-evidence-reasoning paragraph.
