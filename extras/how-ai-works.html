How AI Works

Chatbots, image generators, and recommendation systems are everywhere, and most people have never seen how they work inside. In six short readings you will learn what an LLM really does, why it can seem to forget, how AI turns meaning into numbers in space, how diffusion makes pictures out of static, and what to watch out for now and later. Reading 1 · What is an LLM?

A very good guesser of the next word

A large language model does one job, over and over: it predicts the next token. Answering questions and writing stories both come out of that one move, repeated.

Passage

Predicting, one piece at a time

When you type a message to an AI chatbot, the program answering you is called a large language model, or LLM. At its core, an LLM does one job over and over: given some text, it predicts which piece of text is most likely to come next.

Those pieces are called tokens. A token is a chunk of text. Sometimes it is a whole word like “science,” sometimes part of a word, and sometimes just a comma or a space. In English, one token averages about three-quarters of a word, so a 300-word essay is roughly 400 tokens. Models use tokens because a fixed list of chunks is much easier for a computer to handle than every word in every language.

Here is how a reply gets built. The model reads your message as tokens and gives every token on its list a score for how likely it is to come next. It picks one, though not always the top-scoring one, adds it to the text, and repeats the whole process with the longer text. After “The capital of France is,” the token “Paris” scores so high that the answer is nearly always the same. After “Write a story about a dragon,” thousands of tokens are reasonable, so every reply comes out different.

This also explains something surprising: an LLM is not looking facts up in a table of answers. It learned patterns from huge amounts of writing, and “Paris” follows “capital of France” because that pattern is extremely common. When the patterns are thin or contradict each other, the model can still produce fluent text that sounds right but is wrong. Reading 5 returns to that problem.

Go deeperHow the scores are really computed

Inside the model, your tokens are first converted into embeddings (the lists of numbers from Reading 3). Those numbers pass through many layers of arithmetic. At the end, the model produces one raw score for every token in its vocabulary, which is often tens of thousands of tokens or more. A math step called softmax turns those raw scores into probabilities that add up to 100%.

A setting called temperature controls how the next token is picked. At a low temperature the model almost always takes the highest-probability token, so answers are steadier and more repetitive. At a higher temperature the probabilities are flattened, so less likely tokens get picked more often, which feels more creative but also makes mistakes more likely. That is one reason two replies to the same prompt can differ even though the model itself has not changed.

Reading 2 · Training, using, and the context window

Why a chatbot seems to forget

A model learns during training; afterward it reads and responds without changing. What it can use in a reply is limited to what fits in its context window.

Passage

Two phases and one desk

An LLM has two very different phases of life. In training, the model reads enormous amounts of text, such as books, websites, articles, and code, and plays a guessing game: hide the next token, guess it, check the answer, and adjust. The adjustments are made to billions of numbers inside the model called parameters. Training a top model takes weeks or months on thousands of specialized chips and can cost millions of dollars or more. Afterward, developers usually train the model further so it follows instructions and gives helpful answers, partly using feedback from people who rate its responses.

Using a finished model is called inference. During inference the parameters stay fixed. When you chat with a model, it is not learning from you in the moment; it is just being handed text and predicting what comes next. A company may still save conversations and use them to train a later version, depending on its settings and policies.

What the model can “see” while it writes a reply is called its context window: the maximum amount of text, counted in tokens, that it can take in at one time. That includes your messages, its own earlier replies, and any documents you paste in. Early models handled a few thousand tokens, about several pages. Many current models handle hundreds of thousands, and a few advertise more than a million. Think of a desk. You can spread out only so many papers, and when the desk is full, older papers have to go on the floor, where you can’t use them.

That is why a chatbot can seem to forget something from early in a long conversation. It is not forgetting the way a person does; the older text no longer fits, or the app trimmed it to make room. Apps that seem to remember you from day to day usually save short notes about you and paste them back into the window the next time. Bigger windows help, but they do not make a model perfect. Studies have found that models can miss details buried in the middle of very long text.

Go deeperHow attention picks the relevant tokens

Most modern LLMs are built on a design called the transformer, and its key part is attention. As the model processes each token, it asks, in effect, “which earlier tokens matter for understanding this one?” Each token produces a “query” (what it is looking for), and each earlier token has a “key” (what it offers). The model compares the query with every key and turns the matches into weights that add up to 100%. It then blends in information from the earlier tokens in proportion to those weights. In “The trophy didn’t fit in the suitcase because it was too big,” attention can help link “it” to the right noun.

This happens in many layers, and in each layer many “attention heads” work in parallel, so different heads can pick up different relationships.

Go deeperWhy context windows are limited

In the standard transformer design, attention compares every token with every earlier token, so the work grows roughly with the square of the length: ten times as much text means about a hundred times as many comparisons. The model must also keep notes about every token in the window (called the key-value cache), and that uses up fast memory on the chips. Engineers use many tricks to stretch windows, but each one has a cost in speed, money, or accuracy.

A model also has to be trained to handle long inputs, and even then, details in the middle of a long text are sometimes used less reliably than details at the start or the end.

Reading 3 · Embeddings and vector storage

A map of meaning

AI turns words into lists of numbers so that similar meanings land close together in space. Finding related things becomes a question of distance.

Passage

From words to coordinates

Computers work with numbers, not meaning, so AI systems turn words, sentences, and even images into lists of numbers called embeddings, or vectors. The key trick is that the numbers are learned during training so that things with similar meanings end up with similar numbers.

Picture a map. Every town has two numbers: how far east it is and how far north. Towns that are close on the map are close in the real world. Now imagine a map of ideas instead of towns. “Cat” and “dog” would sit near each other, while “pizza” and “taco” would form their own neighborhood somewhere else. Two numbers per item give you a flat map. Add a third number and you have a 3D space, like a cloud of points floating in a room. In either case, the distance between two points measures how different their meanings are.

Real embeddings use far more than three numbers. Models commonly use hundreds to a few thousand, for example 768 or 1,536 numbers for each piece of text. Nobody can draw 1,536 dimensions, but the idea carries over: every extra number is another direction along which two things can be alike or different. More directions let the map hold shades of meaning that a flat map would have to squash together. Most of those directions do not have simple labels like “is it alive?” or “is it food?”; they are patterns the model found on its own.

A vector database stores millions of these lists of numbers and is built to answer one question quickly: which stored items are closest to this one? When you ask a question, an app can turn it into an embedding, search the database for the nearest documents, and paste them into the model’s context window. That lets a model use information it was never trained on, such as your school’s handbook. The same idea powers “similar songs” and “related articles”: searching by meaning instead of exact words.

Go deeperWhy real embeddings need hundreds or thousands of dimensions

Meaning has many independent aspects at once: topic, tone, formality, whether something is a person or a place, tense, sentiment, and thousands of finer distinctions. With only 2 or 3 numbers, unrelated ideas get squeezed into the same neighborhood by accident, as you may have seen when switching between 2D and 3D above. Each extra dimension gives the model another direction to separate things.

High-dimensional spaces also have a surprising property: they have room for far more directions that are nearly at right angles to each other than there are dimensions. That lets a model store many more distinct features than it has dimensions, though the features overlap a little. Researchers call this “superposition,” and it is an active area of study, with much of the evidence so far coming from small test models.

Two practical details. First, systems often measure closeness by the angle between two embeddings (called cosine similarity) instead of straight-line distance, because the angle focuses on direction rather than length. Second, in very high dimensions it is slow to compare a query with every stored item, so vector databases usually build special indexes that find approximately the nearest items much faster. The number of dimensions is a design choice (for example 768, 1,536, or 3,072), and bigger is not automatically better.

Go deeperBias can live inside the map of meaning

Embeddings are learned from human writing, so the map can pick up the stereotypes in that writing. In a well-known 2016 study, researchers found that word embeddings trained on news text completed analogies such as “man is to computer programmer as woman is to homemaker.” The model was not told to be biased; it copied patterns in its data. Researchers have since developed methods to reduce such bias, but they do not remove it completely.

This matters because embeddings are used for search, hiring tools, and recommendations. If the map places some groups closer to some jobs, every system built on the map can inherit that tilt. To check for it, test the same request with different names or groups and compare the results.

Reading 4 · How image generators work

Pictures out of static

A diffusion model learns to remove noise. To make a new picture, it starts from pure static and cleans it up step by step, steered by your words.

Passage

From static to a picture

Many popular image generators, such as Stable Diffusion, use a method called diffusion. The name comes from physics, where a drop of ink spreads out in water until it is evenly mixed. Diffusion models borrow the idea in a different form: a clear picture can be gradually covered by random speckles, like TV static, until nothing is left of it.

During training, the model is shown a real image, and a little random noise is added. Then more, in many small steps, until only static remains. At each step the model is shown the noisy version and learns to predict the noise that was added. After millions of images, it gets good at telling which parts look like noise and which parts look like a real picture hiding underneath.

To generate an image, the process runs backward. The system starts with a canvas of pure random noise. At each step the model estimates the noise and removes a little of it. Rough patches of light and dark appear first, then shapes and edges, then fine details. A typical image takes tens of steps; some newer models are tuned to need only a handful.

Where does your prompt come in? Your words are turned into an embedding, the same kind of number list from Reading 3, and that embedding nudges every denoising step toward pictures that match the description. The starting noise is random, so the same prompt with a different seed makes a different image, while the same prompt, seed, and settings usually reproduce the same one. The model does not paste together pieces of stored photos. It builds an image from patterns it learned, though researchers have shown that it can occasionally produce near-copies of images that appeared many times in its training data.

Go deeperWhat the denoising model actually predicts

At each step the model receives three things: the current noisy image, a number saying how far along the process is, and the embedding of your prompt. In many designs it outputs a prediction of the noise in the image. The system subtracts a scaled-down version of that predicted noise, which gives a slightly cleaner image, and repeats. Some designs predict a different but closely related quantity instead (such as the clean image itself), but the idea is the same.

To make the result match your prompt more closely, many systems use guidance: they run the model twice at each step, once with your prompt and once without, and push the picture further in the direction of the difference. A higher “guidance scale” follows the prompt more strictly but can make images look over-processed.

Many systems also do this work not on full-size pixels but in a smaller, compressed “latent” space, and then convert the final result into a full image. That makes generating pictures much cheaper.

Passage

Not every language model is built the same way

Reading 2 described the design behind most chatbots: a transformer that predicts one token at a time and uses attention to look back over the text. As of late 2026 that is still the main recipe for the strongest general-purpose models. It is not the only recipe, though, and researchers keep testing others. Two problems drive the search. Attention gets more expensive as the text gets longer, because the model keeps a growing memory of everything it has read. And writing one token at a time is slow.

Answer one: hybrids

A hybrid keeps a few attention layers and swaps most of the others for layers that carry forward a fixed-size summary instead of re-reading everything. AI21 Labs’ Jamba mixes attention layers with Mamba layers, a kind of “state space” layer. IBM’s Granite 4.0 hybrids mostly use Mamba-2 layers, with a transformer block about one layer in ten, and IBM says this cuts the memory needed for long inputs by more than 70 percent (a company figure). NVIDIA’s Nemotron models take a similar path. Qwen3-Next and Kimi Linear use a related idea called linear attention, with roughly three efficient layers for every full attention layer. Moonshot AI, the maker of Kimi Linear, says it needs up to 75 percent less cache memory than full attention. That figure comes from the company.

Hybrids are not a clean win. The company MiniMax first used an efficient linear attention in one model, then went back to full attention in the next, saying the efficient version was harder to make reliable for reasoning and long back-and-forth chats.

Answer two: write the whole answer at once

A diffusion language model changes how the text is produced. It starts with the whole answer hidden behind blanks and fills in many tokens at a time over several rounds, much like the picture getting clearer in the demo above. Inception Labs sells models of this kind, called Mercury, through a paid service. Google has shown an experimental one, Gemini Diffusion, and a research model called LLaDA is openly available. The makers report large speed gains.

There are trade-offs. When several neighboring tokens are guessed in the same round, none of them has seen the others’ final choices, so they can clash. Answers cannot easily be shown word by word as they are written, and calling outside tools is harder. A well-known researcher who surveyed these designs in late 2025 called diffusion text models solid but not state of the art.

Where things stand

For now, the standard transformer that writes one token at a time is still the model to beat. Hybrids are the alternative gaining the most ground in real products. Diffusion for text is newer and more experimental. Most of the speed and cost numbers come from the companies selling the models, and this area changes quickly, so check the date on anything you read about it. That is the same habit as Reading 6: keep what is documented apart from what is claimed or predicted.

Go deeperWhy attention gets expensive, and what a fixed-size summary gives up

When a transformer writes a token, each attention layer looks back at the stored information for every earlier token. That stored information is called the cache, and it grows by one entry per token for every attention layer. Double the length of the text and the cache doubles. The work of comparing each new token with all the earlier ones also grows. That is why very long documents are costly.

A state-space or linear-attention layer works more like a running summary. After each token it updates a fixed-size state, so it does not grow with the text. The price is that the summary has limited room. Details can be squeezed out, and in tests such layers can do worse on tasks that need an exact detail from far back. Hybrids keep a few full attention layers so the model can still look something up precisely, and use the cheap layers for the rest.

Where this comes from: Sebastian Raschka’s November 2025 survey, Beyond Standard LLMs, plus the companies’ own pages, such as Inception Labs, AI21 (Jamba) and IBM (Granite 4.0). Checked in November 2026.

Reading 5 · Warnings for today

Where today’s AI goes wrong

These tools are useful and they fail in predictable ways. Knowing how they fail is part of using them well.

Checkpoint

Before you read: your school’s AI rules

Reading 5 includes advice on privacy. Before you start, find out what your school or district actually requires. Look in the student handbook, the acceptable-use policy, or ask your teacher. Write down what it says in your own words. If you cannot find a written policy, ask your teacher or school office instead of guessing.

Then list what you should never type into an AI tool. A strong list covers at least these kinds of things:

  • Passwords, login codes, and anything that gives access to an account.
  • Your full name together with your address, phone number, school, or schedule.
  • Other people’s private information or photos, without their permission.
  • Health, financial, or government ID details.
  • Anything your teacher has said not to share, such as a test or someone else’s unpublished work.
Passage

Seven known problems

Today’s AI tools are helpful for many tasks, and they have problems that are already well documented. Each one below is labeled Well documented or Still debated.

1. Hallucination Well documented

An LLM predicts plausible text; it does not check facts. So it sometimes states false things in a confident voice, including invented quotes, numbers, and sources. In 2023, two New York lawyers were fined after submitting a court filing that cited legal cases a chatbot had made up.

2. Bias Well documented

Models learn from human writing and images, so they can pick up human stereotypes and gaps. If some groups or languages are rare in the training data, the model usually performs worse for them. In 2018, news reports said a large company scrapped an experimental hiring tool after it learned to downgrade résumés that mentioned women’s activities.

3. Privacy Well documented

Anything you type may be stored, reviewed, or used to train future models, depending on the company and your settings. A good rule is to type nothing you would not want a stranger to read, such as passwords, home addresses, or other people’s private details.

4. Deepfakes and misinformation Well documented

AI can now make realistic fake images, voices, and video cheaply. In January 2024, voters in New Hampshire received robocalls with an AI-generated voice imitating President Biden, telling them not to vote in the primary. Cloned voices are also used in scams, such as a fake call from a “relative” asking for money.

Stop and check: which two problems so far could a person spot just by looking at the output?

Hallucination and bias can sometimes be spotted by a careful reader who knows the topic, but they are often hidden. Deepfakes are designed to look real, and privacy problems are not visible in the output at all. That is why checking with another source matters.

5. Over-reliance Still debated

If a tool does the thinking, you may not build the skill. Researchers are still studying how AI use affects learning, and results so far are mixed: used to explain or check your work it can help, and used to skip the effort it can hurt.

6. Copyright and consent Still debated

Training data often includes books, artwork, and photos made by people who were never asked. Authors, artists, and news organizations have sued AI companies; courts are still working out what the law allows, and rulings so far have not all pointed the same way.

7. Prompt injection Well documented

A model treats everything in its context window as text to follow or use. If an assistant reads a web page or email that contains hidden instructions, it can be tricked into obeying them. Developers are working on defenses, but the problem is not fully solved.

Go deeperWhy hallucinations happen, in more detail

During training, a model is rewarded for predicting text that looks like the text it was trained on. Nothing in that goal checks whether a statement is true. For common facts, the right answer is also the most likely text, so the model usually gets it right. For rare facts, such as an obscure court case or a small town’s population, the model has only weak patterns. It still has to produce a next token, so it produces text with the right shape: a name that looks like a name, a citation that looks like a citation.

Some researchers also point out that many training and testing methods reward giving an answer over saying “I don’t know,” which can encourage guessing. Fixes include teaching models to admit uncertainty, letting them look up real documents first (the vector-database idea from Reading 3), and connecting them to tools such as calculators or search engines. These reduce hallucinations but do not remove them, which is why important claims still need a second source.

OptionalReading: how recommendation feeds choose what you see

Video apps, music apps, and social feeds use recommendation systems. The system records signals such as what you watch, skip, replay, like, or share, and often how long you stay. It also uses embeddings: your history and each item are turned into numbers, and items close to what you already liked are ranked higher. The goal is usually to predict what will keep you engaged.

Two things are well supported: feeds are personalized, so two people can see very different results from the same app, and what gets shown is a choice made by the system. Two things are still debated: how much feeds lock people into narrow “filter bubbles,” and how much they affect mood and attention. Researchers have found mixed results, and findings vary by platform and by person.

Question to think about: which of your signals (watching to the end, pausing, replaying) tell the system the most, and could you change your feed on purpose by changing them?

Reading 6 · Warnings for the future

What is coming, and what no one knows

Honest experts disagree about the future. A careful reader keeps evidence, debate, and prediction apart.

Passage

Five open questions, and one checklist

Nobody knows exactly how AI will develop, and honest experts disagree. A good reader keeps three kinds of statements apart: things that are well supported by evidence, things that are debated, and predictions that no one can know yet.

Jobs

AI already automates some tasks, such as drafting routine emails, transcribing and translating speech, and suggesting computer code. What is debated is how many jobs will change, how fast, and whether new kinds of work will appear quickly enough to replace what is lost. Past technologies both removed and created jobs, but the transitions were hard for the people in the middle of them.

Agents

The newest tools are moving from answering questions to taking actions: browsing websites, filling out forms, running code, sending messages, even making purchases. Actions raise the stakes, because a mistake can have real consequences that cannot easily be undone. A common safeguard is requiring a person to approve actions that cost money or cannot be reversed.

Stop and check: in Jobs and Agents, which statements are documented and which are debated?

Documented: some tasks, such as drafting routine emails or suggesting code, are already automated, and agents already take actions. Debated: how many jobs change, how fast, and whether new work appears quickly enough.

Trust and proof

As fake images, audio, and video get cheaper to make, a second problem appears: real evidence can be dismissed as fake. Researchers call this the “liar’s dividend.” Tools such as content credentials, which attach a record of how a file was made, and watermarks can help, but none is foolproof, so careful readers rely on checking sources, not on looks alone.

Energy, water, and hardware

AI runs in data centres: buildings full of computers. These use a lot of electricity, both for the one-time work of training and for the everyday work of answering millions of requests, which can add up to more than training. The computers give off heat, and many data centres cool them with water or with extra electricity for air conditioning, so the water and energy used depends on the place and the design. The chips are also made from mined materials in energy-intensive factories, and old equipment becomes electronic waste. Companies publish some figures, but estimates vary widely and change quickly, so be careful with any single number you see. What is clear: the cost is real, it depends on where the power comes from, and it is a fair question to ask when deciding how much to use these tools.

Who holds the power

Training the largest models takes huge amounts of computing power, electricity, and money, so a small number of companies and countries lead the field. People debate whether that concentration is a risk, whether rules or openly shared models would help, and who should decide how these systems are used.

Stop and check: does the energy question belong with evidence, debate, or prediction?

The existence of high energy use is documented evidence. How big it will become, and whether efficiency gains will offset growth, is debated and partly prediction.

The biggest uncertainty

Some experts warn that future AI systems could become far more capable than today’s, and that the risks deserve serious preparation now. Others think these worries are overstated and distract from problems that exist today. Both sides can point to evidence and arguments, but no one can prove what will happen.

What a careful reader and user does

  1. Check important claims with a second, independent source.
  2. Protect private information.
  3. Keep a person in charge of decisions that matter.
  4. Ask who built a tool, what it was trained on, and who profits from it.
  5. Use AI to help you learn, not to skip the learning: try first, then ask it to check you.
  6. Follow your school’s rules, and say when you used AI.
Go deeperHow content credentials work, and why they are not a lie detector

A content credential is a small record attached to a file that says how it was made and edited, such as which camera or software was used. The record is protected by a digital signature, so changes to it can be detected. An industry standard called C2PA describes the format.

But the record can be stripped out when a file is re-saved or screenshotted, so a missing credential does not prove a file is fake, and a present one proves only what the signer claims. Invisible watermarks embedded in the pixels or audio can be harder to remove, but they can be damaged, and they exist only if the AI maker added them. That is why these tools help but cannot settle every question.

Try it

Evidence, debate, or prediction?

Sort each statement into the category it belongs in today.

Three kinds of statement. Evidence: something that has been measured or documented and that experts mostly accept (“Some AI tools can already suggest computer code”). Debate: informed experts disagree about it (“AI will create more jobs than it removes”). Prediction: a claim about the future that nobody can check yet (“By 2040 every home will have an AI assistant”). Worked example: “Data centres use a lot of electricity” is evidence, because it can be measured today; “AI power use will double by 2030” is a prediction. A quick test is to ask “could I look this up and find the same answer from several sources?” If yes, evidence. If experts split, debate. If it is about what has not happened, prediction. The feedback explains each one.

  1. Ask. Ask an AI tool a factual science question that has a checkable answer (for example, “How far is the Moon from Earth?” or “What causes the seasons?”). Copy the answer. Follow your school’s rules for which tools you may use.
  2. Check. Verify it with two independent sources, such as a science agency, a university, or a textbook. Independent means they do not simply copy each other. Note each source and its date.
  3. Write a short CER paragraph. Claim: was the AI answer correct, partly correct, or wrong? Evidence: what do the two sources say, with names and dates? Reasoning: why do those sources deserve more trust than the AI alone, and what does this tell you about when to check an AI answer?

Rubric and model answer

Rubric (12 points):

  • Explanation (4): 4 = explains how the tool works accurately in own words with three or more course terms used correctly; 3 = mostly accurate, minor slips; 2 = only two terms or vague; 1 = mostly copied or inaccurate.
  • Warnings (4): 4 = one current and one future warning that fit this tool, each correctly labeled documented, debated, or prediction, with a reason; 2 = warnings named but labels or fit are weak; 1 = generic.
  • Actions (2): 2 = two specific, realistic actions; 1 = vague, such as “be careful.”
  • Clarity (2): 2 = organized, 200–250 words, own voice; 1 = hard to follow or far off length.

Model answer (about 200 words, a chatbot): A chatbot is powered by a large language model that reads my message as tokens and predicts the next token, over and over, until a reply is built. It learned patterns during training, when its parameters were adjusted, and during inference the parameters stay fixed. It can only use what fits in its context window, so in a long chat it may seem to forget early messages. One current warning is hallucination, which is well documented: the model predicts plausible text and does not check facts, so it can invent sources. One future warning is that AI agents may take real actions such as buying things; how risky that becomes is a prediction, because nobody knows how widely they will be used. To use it carefully, I will check any important claim with two independent sources, and I will not type passwords or other people’s private details into it. I will also try a problem myself first and then ask the chatbot to check my work, so I keep learning, and I will say when I used it.

Words to know

LLM
Large language model: a program that predicts the next token in text.
token
A chunk of text a model reads and writes; about three-quarters of an English word.
parameters
The billions of adjustable numbers inside a model that training sets.
training
The phase where a model’s parameters are adjusted using lots of examples.
inference
Using a finished model; its parameters do not change.
context window
The most text, in tokens, a model can take in at one time.
embedding (vector)
A list of numbers that stands for the meaning of a word, sentence, or image.
dimension
One of the numbers in an embedding; one direction along which items can differ.
vector database
A store built to quickly find the embeddings closest to a query.
diffusion
A method that makes images by removing noise step by step.
seed
The number that sets the starting random noise.
prompt
The instructions or description you give a model.
hallucination
A fluent, confident answer that is false or made up.
bias
A systematic skew in outputs, often inherited from training data.
deepfake
A realistic fake image, voice, or video made with AI.
prompt injection
Hidden instructions in text an AI reads that trick it into obeying them.
agent
An AI system that takes actions, not just answers questions.
CER
Claim, Evidence, Reasoning: a way to structure a short argument.
liar’s dividend
The way real evidence can be dismissed as “probably fake.”
Tutor notes: pacing, discussion, misconceptions, model answers

Pacing: six sessions of 30–40 minutes, one reading each, plus one for the closing reflection. Have students read the passage once for the gist, once with a pencil (underline claims, circle terms), then do the Try-it and the questions.

Reading skills practiced: citing textual evidence (the Find the evidence items), determining central ideas, working out meaning of technical terms from context, and separating well-supported claims from debated ones and predictions. These line up with the grades 9–10 informational-text reading standards (for example, citing strong textual evidence and evaluating claims), but check your own standards document for exact codes.

Common misconceptions:

  • “The AI looks up the answer.” It predicts likely text from learned patterns (Reading 1).
  • “It learns from my chat as we talk.” Its parameters stay fixed during use; a company may use saved chats to train later versions (Reading 2).
  • “It forgets like a person.” The older text fell out of the window or was trimmed (Reading 2).
  • “More dimensions means a bigger picture.” Each is another measured direction; we can’t picture them, but distance still works (Reading 3).
  • “Image generators collage existing photos.” They build from learned patterns, with rare near-copies of heavily repeated training images (Reading 4).

Discussion starters: Which of the seven current warnings worries you most, and is it documented or debated? When is it fine to use an AI tool for schoolwork, and who should decide? What evidence would change your mind about the future questions in Reading 6?
