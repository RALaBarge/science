 Pushes and Pulls
 How can a push or pull change how something moves?
 Pushes and pulls are how we make things move. You will try pushing and pulling objects to start, stop, speed up, slow down or turn them, find out how strength and direction change what happens, and design something that changes motion safely.

 Concept 1
Pushes and pulls start, stop, and change motion
 A push or pull can make an object start moving, stop, speed up, slow down, or turn.
 Analogy
 A child on a swing.
“A push starts me. A hand stops me.”

 Diagram
 Ball with arrows: push arrow forward, pull arrow back.

 [SVG IMAGE] The arrow shows which way the push or pull goes. A push goes away from you. A pull comes toward you.Text version of this picture

 Try it
 Guess first: If you tap a toy car gently, will it go far or not far?
What you need: one toy car, a flat floor or table, a line of tape.
Do: Put the car on the tape line. Give it a gentle push. Watch where it stops. Now roll it back, push again, and this time use your hand to stop it before it goes far.
Look for: The car did not start by itself. Your push made it go. Your hand made it stop.
What it shows: A push can start motion, and a push from your hand can also stop it.
Safety: Keep small cars away from mouths. Roll on the floor, not near stairs.
“Make it go. Make it stop.”

 Concept 2
Strength and direction matter
 A stronger push or a different direction can change how far or where an object goes.
Push a toy car softly, medium or hard, with or without a wall in the way.
 Try it
 Blow gently on a cotton ball sitting on a table, from the side (never suck on a straw or put the cotton ball near your mouth): soft blow vs. strong blow.
“Soft blow. Strong blow. Which goes farther?”
 Data

“Soft push: 2 tiles. Strong push: 6 tiles.”

 Concept 3
We can design things to change motion safely
 A good design can slow, stop, or redirect motion on purpose.
 Diagram
 Sketch of a ramp and a stopper block.
“First draw. Then build. Then test.”
 [SVG IMAGE] The car rolls down the ramp. The stopper block should stop it at the finish line. First draw it, then build it, then test it.Text version of this picture
A ramp with a toy car at the top, a floor with a finish line and a stopper block. Three steps below: 1 Draw, 2 Build, 3 Test.
 Try it
 Guess first: Will your stopper keep the car from rolling past the line? Draw it first.
What you need: toy car, a ramp (a book with cardboard), tape for the finish line, a foam piece, a paper cup, or a cardboard block.
Do: Tape a finish line on the floor. Roll the car down the ramp with no stopper to see where it goes. Then build a stopper and put it on the line. Roll the car again. Did the car stop before the line?
Look for: A soft stopper (foam) can slow the car gently. A hard one can make it bounce back. Change one thing and test again.
What it shows: A design is an idea you test. If the car rolls past, the design can be fixed.
Safety: Keep small stoppers and cars out of mouths. Wipe up any spills so no one slips.
“Can your block stop the car?”
 Data
 Compare two stoppers over 3 tries.
“Foam stopped it 3 times. Cardboard stopped it 1 time.”

 Hands-on · Design or investigate
Try it at home or in class
 Safe Stopper Design. Problem: a toy car rolls too far. Students sketch, build, and test two stoppers or ramp ends. They test each design 3 times and choose the better one using evidence.

 Try itWhat you need
toy car or spool, cardboard ramp, tape, foam piece, paper cup, cotton ball, paper strips or graph paper.
Safety: No coin cells or magnets are needed. Keep small cars and stoppers away from mouths, and clean up spills so no one slips.
 Try it
 What you do
 Station 1, pushes (about 10 minutes). Roll a toy car with a soft push and a strong push. Put a sticker where each one stops. Count floor tiles. Which push went farther?
 Station 2, Safe Stopper (about 20 minutes). Draw a ramp and a stopper. Build two different stoppers (for example foam and cardboard). Roll the car 3 times at each and tally how many times it stopped before the line.
 Look for: the stopper that works most of the 3 tries. Say: “Foam stopped it ___ times. Cardboard stopped it ___ times.”
 No time or no good weather? Use this data
This data is a backup. It lets you practice reading evidence, but the standard expects you to collect your own when you can.
If you cannot run the physical test, use this data: soft push = 2 floor tiles; strong push = 6 floor tiles. Foam stopper stopped the car 3 out of 3 tries. Cardboard stopper stopped the car 1 out of 3 tries.

 Tutor notes: misconceptions, standards, pacing
 Pacing: 5 sessions of 30 minutes.
 Common misconceptions to listen for:
“Objects stop because they run out of push.” Correct: another push or pull, such as rubbing or a barrier, can slow or stop motion.
“Only people push or pull.” Correct: wind, water, and gravity can also push or pull.
“A bigger push always gives the same result.” Correct: object shape, surface, and barriers matter too.
“A push is only with hands.” Correct: a push can be air, water, or another object.
“A pull moves something away from you.” Correct: a pull moves something toward you.
“One try is enough.” Correct: we repeat tests to be more sure.
“Changing many things makes a fair test.” Correct: a fair test changes one thing and keeps the rest the same.
 Limits and cautions:
Friction is not named deeply; K–2 students only need observable slowing/stopping.
Gravity may come up as a pull; this is accurate but not required by K-PS2.
K-PS2-2 needs repeated trials; one test is not enough.
Small parts need supervision.
Design choice: counting floor tiles or paper strips as K distance data
