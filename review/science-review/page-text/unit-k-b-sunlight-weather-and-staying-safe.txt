 Sunlight, Weather, and Staying Safe
 What does sunlight do to places, and how do weather forecasts help us stay safe?
 Sunlight and weather are part of every day, and we can learn from them. You will see how sunlight warms and lights surfaces, how shade keeps things cooler, how weather repeats in patterns when we keep a record, and how forecasts help us get ready and stay safe.

 Concept 1
Sunlight warms and lights surfaces
 Sunlight makes surfaces brighter and often warmer; dark surfaces often warm more.
Put light and dark paper in the sun or the shade and watch the thermometers.
 Analogy
 The Sun is like a lamp far away.
“Sunlight travels to Earth.”
 [SVG IMAGE] The Sun and a lamp both send out light. The Sun is much, much farther away, but its light still reaches us and warms what it shines on.Text version of this picture

 Concept 2
Shade can keep things cooler
 A shade blocks sunlight, so it can reduce warming.
 Diagram
 Ice cube under shade and ice cube with no shade.
“Shade blocks sunlight.”
 [SVG IMAGE] The card blocks the sunlight. The shaded ice cube gets less warming, so it should melt more slowly.Text version of this picture
Left: ice cube under a shade card, sunlight blocked. Right: ice cube in direct sunlight.
 Try it
 Guess first: Two ice cubes are in the same sunny spot. One gets a shade. Which one melts slower?
What you need: 2 ice cubes of the same size, 2 trays, a paper card and craft sticks (or a paper cup turned over) for the shade, a sunny window or a lamp.
Do: Put one ice cube on each tray in the sun. Build a small shade over only one. Count slowly to 60, or wait 10 minutes, then look. Draw how big each cube is now. Do it again if you can.
Look for: The shaded cube is bigger and has fewer drops of water around it.
What it shows: Shade blocks sunlight, so less warming happens. That is why a shady spot feels cooler.
Safety: An adult handles any warm lamp. Wipe up water so no one slips.
“Which ice cube melts slower?”
 Data
 Melt chart with drops or puddle size.
“Shaded ice: less melt. Sun ice: more melt.”
 Real world
 A beach umbrella helps people stay cooler.
“A beach umbrella helps.”
 [SVG IMAGE] The umbrella blocks sunlight and makes a shady spot. On a Michigan beach, shade helps people stay cooler.Text version of this picture

 Concept 3
Weather has patterns over time
 We can record weather over time and see patterns.
 [IMG: Illustration of two children in winter coats and boots building a snowman in a snowy front yard, away from the street, while snow falls.] Snow falls in Michigan winters. We dress warm and play away from the road.
 [SVG IMAGE] A 20-day Michigan fall sample: 8 sunny, 6 cloudy, 4 rainy and 2 snowy days. Sunny days were the most common.Text version of this graph
Bar graph, Weather over 20 days (Michigan fall sample). Sunny 8 days, Cloudy 6 days, Rainy 4 days, Snowy 2 days.
 Try it
 Guess first: What will the weather be like today: sun, cloud, rain, or snow?
What you need: a class calendar, weather stickers or crayons (sun, cloud, rain, snow).
Do: Each morning, look out the window together. Say what you see. Put the matching sticker or drawing on today’s square. Do this for many days.
Look for: After a few weeks, count each kind. Which kind came most? Did the weather change when the seasons changed?
What it shows: One day does not show a pattern. Many days written down do.
“Sun, cloud, rain, snow. Mark today.”
 Data
 Tally of sunny, cloudy, rainy, snowy days. The 20 days in order are in the tutor notes.
“This month had 8 sunny days.”

 Real world
 Snow in Michigan winters.
“Snow falls in winter. We dress warm and stay safe near roads.”
 Concept 4
Forecasts help us get ready and stay safe
 Forecasts tell us what weather may come so we can get ready and stay safe.
 Analogy
 A forecast is like a helper note about tomorrow. Forecast = the note. Weather coming = what the note tells you. Your coat or boots = what you do because of it.
A note cannot be perfect, because weather can change, so we check the forecast again.
“A forecast helps you choose a coat.”
 Try it
 Picture-sort “What do we do?” for thunderstorm, tornado, and blizzard.
“Thunderstorm: go inside. Tornado: go to safe room. Blizzard: stay off roads.”
Picture sort: pick the safe thing to do for each kind of weather. A grown-up reads each card out loud.
 Data
 Ask and answer questions about a real forecast.
“Forecast says rain. We need raincoats.”

 Hands-on · Design or investigate
Try it at home or in class
 Sun-Safe Beach Helper. Problem: a toy helper at a Michigan beach needs a cool place. Students build a small shade for an ice cube or toy. They compare melting or warmth with an uncovered control. Criteria: the shaded spot stays cooler or melts less than the uncovered control. If sunlight is unavailable, use a lamp.
 [IMG: Photo of two ice cubes in small metal trays on a sunny windowsill. One has a small paper shade held up by a craft stick. The other is uncovered.] The finished Sun-Safe Beach Helper test: one shaded ice cube, one uncovered control, same window.
 [SVG IMAGE] The ice cube in the shade left 5 drops of melt. The ice cube in the sun left 12 drops.Text version of this graph
Bar graph, Melt drops after the same time. Shaded ice 5 drops, Uncovered ice 12 drops.
 Try itWhat you need
black paper, white paper, lamp or sunny window, 2 ice cubes, 2 small trays or cups, paper or cardstock for shade, craft sticks, tape, weather calendar stickers or crayons, weather picture cards.
Safety: Adult handles warm lamps or warm surfaces. If a flashlight is used, use a ready-made one and do not open the battery compartment. Clean up water spills right away.
 Try it
 What you do
 1. Black and white paper (10 minutes). Lay both papers in the sun or under a lamp. Guess first: which will feel warmer? Wait a few minutes, then touch each.
 2. Shade for an ice cube (15 minutes). Build a shade over one ice cube, leave the other uncovered, and compare the melting. Run it two times.
 3. Weather calendar (2 minutes a day). Mark the weather each day for a few weeks. Count the days at the end.
 4. Safety sort. Use the picture sort above for thunderstorm, tornado, and blizzard.
 Look for: black paper warmer than white; shaded ice melting slower; which weather came most days.

 Tutor notes: misconceptions, standards, pacing
 Pacing: 5 sessions of 30 minutes.
 Common misconceptions to listen for:
“Sunlight only makes light, not warmth.” Correct: sunlight can also warm surfaces.
“Sunlight makes surfaces colder.” Correct: sunlight can warm surfaces.
“Dark surfaces stay cooler than light surfaces in sunlight.” Correct: dark surfaces often warm more.
“Shade makes cold.” Correct: shade blocks sunlight and reduces warming; it does not make cold.
“Weather has no patterns.” Correct: weather can show patterns over days and weeks.
“A forecast makes the weather happen.” Correct: a forecast tells what may happen so we can prepare.
“Severe weather means panic.” Correct: we follow a calm safety plan.
 Limits and cautions:
K-ESS2-1 needs repeated observations. If the unit is compressed, the provided dataset is a partial path (analysis of provided data) and is not enough for full coverage.
K-PS3-2 is strongest when students build and test their own shade with a control. A teacher demo alone is only partial.
K-ESS3-2 needs a real or realistic forecast, such as a printed weather forecast or classroom weather report.
Severe weather must be taught calmly: prepare, do not scare.
This unit does not cover materials in a light beam or signal devices; those belong in 1-A Sound and Light.
Design choice: using shade over an ice cube as a K-safe warming-reduction test
