 Weather, Climate, and Hazards
 What weather can we expect in different places and seasons, and how can we reduce harm from weather hazards?
 Weather is what happens today, and climate is the pattern over many years. This unit shows how weather is measured, how graphs reveal seasonal patterns, why different places have different climates, and how designs can reduce the damage from storms and floods but never remove every risk.

 Concept 2
We measure weather
 Scientists measure temperature, precipitation, wind, and clouds.
 Try it
 Rain gauge
cup with ruler.
“After rain, measure how deep the water is.”
You did this before / what is new: You noticed weather in K-B; now you measure it and record data.
Predict first: How deep will the water be after a rain? A little, or a lot?
You need: a clear cup or jar with straight sides, a ruler, tape, a pencil, paper.
Tape the ruler on the cup with the 0 at the bottom.
Put it outside in an open spot, away from roofs and trees.
After it rains, read the line where the water stops, in centimeters.
Write the date and the number. Pour out the water.
Do it for at least five rainy days.
What you should see: You will get small numbers, such as 0.5 cm one day and 1 cm another. Rain amounts change from day to day.
Why it matters: Weather people measure rain the same way so they can compare days and months.
 Try it
 Wind vane
paper arrow on pencil.
“A wind vane points into the wind. The wind comes FROM that way. Put the tail on the other end.”
Predict first: Which way will the arrow point when the wind blows from the west?
You need: a pencil with an eraser, a pin or thumbtack, a straw, paper, tape, a card with N, E, S, W.
Cut a paper arrow head and a bigger tail. Tape them to the ends of a straw.
Push the pin through the middle of the straw into the pencil eraser.
Take it outside and hold it up. Use the card to find north.
Wait until it stops moving. The arrowhead points to where the wind comes FROM.
Notice: The big tail catches the wind and swings away, so the head points into the wind.
Why it matters: Knowing the wind direction helps us tell what weather may come next.

 Concept 5
Designs can reduce weather-hazard harm
 Solutions such as barriers, drains, and rules can reduce damage, but they cannot remove all risk.
 Analogy
 Helmet
bicycle helmet.
“A helmet does not stop falls. It reduces harm.”
What matches what: the helmet is the design (a barrier, a safe room, a strong roof). A fall is the hazard (a flood, a tornado, a storm). Neither stops the hazard from happening. Both make the harm smaller.
 Try it
 Barrier test
soil slope, water, cardboard/foil barriers.
“Test barriers. Which one keeps soil in place?”
You did this before / what is new: You tested erosion guards in 2-C; now the question is a design problem: which barrier reduces harm from a flood? The new measurement is spoonfuls of sediment moved, and you compare a no-barrier run with a cardboard fence and a grass model. One messy trial is not the finding: test each barrier several times the same way and compare before you claim.
Predict first: Which will keep more soil in place, a cardboard fence or a grass patch?
You need: a tray with soil piled at one end, a cup, a spoon, cardboard, foil, a piece of sod or grass.
Tilt the tray a little. Pour one cup of water the same slow way each time.
Run one trial with no barrier. Count spoonfuls of soil that washed to the low end.
Add one barrier and repeat. Keep the water and slope the same.
Run each barrier at least three times and take the middle number.
What you should see: With no barrier you might move about 12 spoonfuls. A barrier should move fewer. Your numbers will differ.
Why it matters: Real towns test designs like this before they build one to protect a river bank.

 Tutor notes: misconceptions, standards, pacing
 Pacing: five 30-minute sessions.
 Common misconceptions to listen for:
“Weather and climate are the same.” Correct: weather is short-term; climate is long-term.
“One cold day means climate changed.” Correct: climate needs long-term patterns.
“Seasons happen because Earth is closer to the Sun in summer.” Correct: tilt causes seasons; detailed cause comes later.
“Clouds make cold.” Correct: clouds can block sunlight or trap heat.
“Hazards cannot be reduced.” Correct: designs can reduce harm, but they cannot eliminate all risk.
 Limits and cautions:
This unit does not fully teach the water cycle; Unit 4 does that.
It does not teach climate change; later units handle human impact.
Cause of seasons is simplified here and handled more fully later.
Barrier tests are models; real flood control involves engineering, land use, and public policy.
