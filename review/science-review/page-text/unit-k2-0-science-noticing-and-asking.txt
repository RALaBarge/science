 Science Noticing and Asking
 How do we find out about the world?
 Science starts with looking closely. In this short unit children practice noticing what is really there, turning what they notice into a question, and making a fair comparison by changing only one thing, then sharing what they found with drawings and words.

 Concept 1
Scientists notice things
 Science starts when we look closely and say what we really see.
 Analogy
 A scientist is like a detective. A detective looks closely at clues with a magnifying glass and says only what they really see. The magnifying glass = looking closely. The clue = the thing you look at.
Where it breaks: a scientist also tests ideas, not only looks.
“I look. I say what I see.”
 Try it
 Guess first: What small thing on a leaf, a pine cone, or a stone will you notice that others might miss?
What you need: a tray with a pine cone, a leaf, and a stone; a magnifier; paper and crayons.
Do: Pick one object. Look at it for one whole minute, using the magnifier. Say out loud what you see, feel, and smell (do not taste). Draw ONE small detail, like a line on the leaf or a bump on the stone.
Look for: Is it smooth or bumpy? What color is it, really? What did you see only because you looked longer?
What it shows: Careful noticing is where every science question starts.
Safety: Wash your hands after touching outdoor objects. Do not taste anything.
“Draw one small thing you see.”

 Concept 2
Questions help us learn
 A question points us to what we can find out next.
 Analogy
 A question is like a flashlight in a dark room. The dark room = what we do not know yet. The flashlight = our question. The spot where it shines = where to look next.
A good question points us to something we can look at, measure, or try.
“A question shows where to look.”
 Try it
 Sort question cards: “Can we test it?” vs. “Cannot test it yet.”
“Can we test it? Yes or no.”

 Concept 3
We compare one thing at a time and share what we find
 A fair comparison changes one thing and keeps the rest the same; drawings and words help others understand our evidence.
 Analogy
 Two cookies are the same, but one has raisins. Raisins = the one thing we changed. Same dough, same oven, same time = the things we keep the same. If one tastes different, the raisins are the reason.
“Change one thing. Keep the rest the same.”
 Try it
 Test two paper towels of the same size with the same dropper and the same drop size. Add drops one at a time until a towel first drips, and count the drops.
“Same size towel. Same dropper. Count the drops until it drips.”
Do each towel three times with a fresh piece. One try can be a fluke, so we repeat and compare the counts.
 Data
 Picture graph with drops.
“Towel A soaked up about 5 drops each time. Towel B soaked up about 8 drops each time. We repeated, and the counts stayed close.”

Try it at home or in class
 Classroom Problem Finder. Students observe a real situation, such as crayons rolling off a table or markers drying out. They draw what they notice, state one problem, and sketch one simple tool or rule that might help. This addresses K-2-ETS1-1.

 Try itWhat you need
paper, crayons, 2–3 magnifiers, leaves/pinecones/smooth stones, 2 identical cups, paper towels, dropper or sponge, small tray.
Safety: No coin cells or magnets are needed. Wash hands after handling outdoor objects. Keep water spills wiped up.
 Try it
 What you do
 1. Notice walk or tray (10 minutes). Look closely at three things. Draw one small detail of each.
 2. Fair paper towels (15 minutes). Same size towels, same dropper, same drop size. Count drops until each towel first drips. Do each three times with a fresh piece.
 3. Problem finder (10 minutes). Watch crayons roll off a table. Draw it, say the problem, and sketch one tool or rule that might help.
 No time or no good weather? Use this data
This data is a backup. It lets you practice reading evidence, but the standard expects you to collect your own when you can.
If you cannot run the towel test, use a picture table of repeated tries: Towel A held about 5 drops each time, Towel B held about 8 drops each time. If you cannot collect objects, use a picture tally: 4 smooth things (a stone, a marble, a button, a maple leaf) and 5 bumpy things (a pinecone, tree bark, an acorn cap, a sponge, a brick).
 Near you: Observe local safe objects: maple leaves, pine cones, lake-smooth stones, snow photos, or acorns. Child text: “Michigan has trees, water, rocks, and snow.”

 Tutor notes: misconceptions, standards, pacing
 Pacing: 3 sessions of 30 minutes.
 Common misconceptions to listen for:
“Only mixing chemicals is science.” Correct: noticing, comparing, and asking are science too.
“Science is only experiments.” Correct: careful observation and recording are science too.
“One try is enough.” Correct: we repeat to be more sure.
“The loudest answer wins.” Correct: evidence decides.
“Drawings are not science.” Correct: drawings can be data.
 Limits and cautions:
