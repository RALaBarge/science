 Land and Water, Fast and Slow
 How does land change fast and slow, and how can we model land and water?
 The land around us is always changing, sometimes in a day and sometimes over thousands of years. This unit looks at quick and slow changes, how wind and water move soil and sand and what slows them down, how to model the land and water near you, and where water is found as liquid or ice.

 Concept 1
Earth events can be quick or slow
 Some changes to land happen fast, like floods and volcanoes; others happen slowly, like erosion and canyon carving.
 [IMG: Photo of layered tan sandstone cliffs with rust-orange and green-gray streaks running down the rock, above dark blue lake water, with pines on top.] Water and ice have worn these cliffs over a very long time. Some changes are slow.
 Analogy
 Some changes are fast like a door slamming. Some are slow like a crayon wearing down. Door slam = a flood or a volcano: done quickly. Crayon wearing down = a canyon or a dune: it takes a very long time, a little at a time.
“Some changes are quick. Some are slow.”

 Concept 2
Slowing wind and water
 Wind and moving water can move soil and sand; plants, rocks, and barriers can help.
Blow wind over bare sand and over sand with grass.
 Try it
 Fan air gently over dry sand or soil with a folded card; pour a little water. Add grass, sticks, or stones and test again.
“Wind moved sand. Grass helped stop it.”
You did this before / what is new: Earlier units tested plants and pushes; here the new variable is the guard you place on the sand, and the new measurement is finger widths of sand moved. This is the first guard test in the series; later units count spoonfuls of sediment from different trays, so do not compare those numbers with these. One messy trial is not the finding: run the same blow or pour three times for each guard and compare the tries before you choose.

 Concept 3
Model your local land and water
 A model can show the shapes and kinds of land and water near us.
 Diagram
 Simple Michigan-like map or photo with lake, river, hill, and dune.
“Land can be hills. Water can be lakes.”
 [SVG IMAGE] Land can be hills. Water can be lakes and rivers. Sand can pile into a dune. A model can show all of them.Text version of this picture
Map with a lake, a river, a hill, and a dune.
 Try it
 Guess first: What do the land and water near you look like from above? Which shapes will your model need?
What you need: clay or play dough, a tray or cardboard, blue paper, paper, crayons, a simple map or photo.
Do: Look at the map. Build a model: a hill from clay, a lake from blue paper, a river from a blue paper strip, a dune from sand-colored clay. Add a label for each.
Look for: Does it have a lake, a river, a hill, and a dune? How is it different from the real place?
What it shows: A model shows the shapes of land and water so we can talk about them.
“Make a lake. Make a dune.”
 Data
 Model feature checklist. After building, tick each box that your model really has: lake, river, hill, dune. Count the ticks, and write one sentence: “Our model has a lake and a river.”
Compare with a neighbor. What did they include that you did not?
“Our model has a lake and a river.”

 Hands-on · Design or investigate
Try it at home or in class
 Erosion Guard Test. Problem: wind or water moves sand in the tray. Students sketch and build two guards, such as grass, stones, or a paper barrier. They test each guard 3 times with the same blow or pour, measure sand movement, and choose the better solution using evidence.
 [IMG: Photo of a plastic tray holding a sand slope, a patch of grass on one side, a few stones on the other and a small cup of water.] The Erosion Guard tray before the test: sand, two guards (grass and stones) and water to pour.
 [IMG: Illustration of three students at a classroom table with a sand erosion tray: a girl pours water from a cup, a boy checks the sand with his finger and a third student writes down the result.] Teams share the jobs in the Erosion Guard test: pour the same amount each time, measure the sand that moved, and record it.
 [SVG IMAGE] Sand moved in 3 tries for each guard. Grass moved the least sand. With no guard the sand moved 4 to 5 finger widths.Text version of this graph
Grouped bar graph of sand moved in finger widths over 3 tries. No guard: 4, 5, 4. Grass: 1, 1, 2. Stones: 2, 1, 2. Paper barrier: 3, 3, 2.
 Try itWhat you need
tray, sand or soil, straw, small cup or dropper, grass clippings/twigs/stones/paper barriers, clay or paper, blue paper, picture cards or printed photos, simple Michigan map, crayons, paper.
Safety: Wash hands after sand/soil trays. Do not blow on sand. Use a folded card to fan it and keep sand away from faces and eyes. No coin cells or magnets are required.
 Try it
 What you do
 1. Fast or slow (10 minutes). Sort picture cards into fast and slow changes, and name the Earth material.
 2. Erosion Guard Test (30 minutes). Put sand in a tray. Fan it gently with a folded card, with no guard, then with grass, stones, or a barrier. Measure how far the sand moved in finger widths. Do each 3 times.
 3. Land and water model (20 minutes). Build a Michigan-like model with a lake, river, hill, and dune.
 4. Solid or liquid (10 minutes). Sort water pictures and make a chart of where you find water.

 Tutor notes: misconceptions, standards, pacing
 Pacing: 5 sessions of 30 minutes.
 Common misconceptions to listen for:
“Land never changes.” Correct: land changes fast and slow.
“Only earthquakes and volcanoes change land.” Correct: wind, water, and ice also change land, often slowly.
“Wind is just clouds moving.” Correct: wind is moving air.
“Water is only in lakes.” Correct: water is also in rivers, ice, snow, clouds, and underground.
“A model is the same as the real place.” Correct: a model is smaller and simpler, but it helps show ideas.
 Limits and cautions:
Sand/soil trays require handwashing and gentle blowing rules.
Quick events like floods or earthquakes should be taught calmly; focus on evidence and preparation, not fear.
Models are simplified; they are not the real place.
If an extension uses lights or magnets, use ready-made lamps only and keep magnets away from Chromebooks.
