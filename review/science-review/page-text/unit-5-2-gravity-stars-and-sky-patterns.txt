 Gravity, Stars, and Sky Patterns
 How do Earth’s motions, gravity, and distances create patterns we can observe?
 Gravity, distance and Earth's motions explain patterns we can watch in the sky. You will see why the Sun looks so bright, why gravity pulls things toward Earth's center, how Earth's spin makes day, night and changing shadows, and why different stars show up in different seasons.

 Concept 2
Gravity pulls down
 Earth’s gravitational force on objects is directed down, toward Earth’s center.
 [SVG IMAGE] 
Where you stand on Earth: 
Drop the ballPut it back
Move around the globe and drop the ball from anywhere: it always falls toward the center of Earth.
 Try it
 Drop test
dropped ball and sideways toss.
“Both fall down. Gravity pulls down.”
You did this before / what is new: You may have seen drop tests with forces; now you write an argument that “down” means toward Earth’s center.
Predict first: If you toss a ball sideways, will it fall down like a dropped ball?
You need: a soft ball or bean bag.
Drop the ball from shoulder height. Watch where it goes.
Toss it gently sideways. Watch it curve.
Toss it up. Watch it come back.
What you should see: All three come down. Gravity pulls toward the ground, which is toward the center of Earth.
Why it matters: It does not matter how you toss it. Gravity pulls toward Earth.

 Concept 3
Shadows and day/night show a daily pattern
 Earth spins once each day; the side facing the Sun has day, and shadow length changes in a pattern.

 Try it
 Globe and flashlight
globe and flashlight Sun.
“Turn the globe slowly. One side is day. One side is night.”
You did this before / what is new: You used day/night models in 1-C; now you add measured shadow data.
Predict first: When you spin the globe, will the lit side stay in the same place?
You need: a globe or ball, a flashlight in a dark room, a sticker for Michigan.
Put the sticker on Michigan. Shine the flashlight on the globe.
Turn the globe slowly counter-clockwise (the way Earth spins, seen from above the North Pole).
Say "day" when Michigan is lit and "night" when it is dark.
What you should see: The flashlight lights half the globe all the time. Spinning moves your sticker from day to night and back.
Why it matters: It is Earth that spins. The Sun does not go around us.

 Concept 4
Different stars appear in different seasons
 Earth orbits the Sun once each year, so the night sky changes by season.

 Concept 5
Review only: Moon phases
 The Moon reflects sunlight and changes appearance; this is review, not a new grade-5 PE claim.
 Try it
 Ball and flashlight
ball as Moon, flashlight as Sun.
“Move the ball. Only the sunlit side shines.”
You did this before / what is new: You saw Moon cards in 1-C; this is review only.
Predict first: Where should the ball be, compared with the flashlight, to see it all lit? To see none lit?
You need: a ball, a flashlight in a dark room.
Put the ball at arm's length from you, with the flashlight behind you shining past you onto the ball: you see the full lit face (full Moon).
Move the ball to your right or left, a quarter turn around you: you see half lit.
Put the ball between you and the flashlight so the lit side faces away: you see almost none lit (new Moon).
What you should see: Only the half of the ball facing the flashlight shines. What you see depends on where you stand.
Why it matters: This is review. The lit half is always half; the angle changes what we see.

 Tutor notes: misconceptions, standards, pacing
 Pacing: five 30-minute sessions.
 Common misconceptions to listen for:
“The Sun is bright because it is the biggest star.” Correct: it looks bright because it is close.
“Day and night happen because the Sun moves around Earth.” Correct: Earth rotates.
“Seasons happen because Earth is closer to the Sun in summer.” Correct: tilt changes sunlight spread and day length.
“The Moon makes its own light.” Correct: it reflects sunlight.
“Gravity needs air to work.” Correct: gravity works even without air.
“Heavier objects always fall faster.” Correct: gravity pulls down on all objects; air resistance can change falling.
“Down is just toward the nearest ground.” Correct: down is toward Earth’s center.
“Stars turn off in summer.” Correct: Earth’s orbit changes which stars we see at night.
 Limits and cautions:
Moon phases are review only and do not earn a new grade-5 PE.
Solar-system scale is not taught here; it belongs later in Unit 8.6.
Air resistance is mentioned but not deeply taught; the grade-5 argument is about the direction “down.”
Seasonal star visibility depends on latitude, clouds, and light pollution; the provided chart guarantees evidence even without night observing.
