 Matter, Mixtures, and Changes
 What is stuff made of, and how can we tell if it changed?
 All matter is made of particles too small to see, and that idea explains a lot. This unit connects particles to solids, liquids and gases, uses properties to identify materials, separates mixtures such as salt and sand, and shows that when things change or dissolve the total amount of matter stays the same.

 Try it
 Air takes space
a zip bag being blown up and sealed.
“Blow air into a bag. Seal it. Press it. The air takes space.”
Predict first: Will the sealed bag feel empty or full? Can you squash it flat?
You need: a zip bag.
Open the bag and sweep it through the air. Zip it nearly closed.
Blow air in and seal it quickly.
Press on it with your hands. Try to squash it flat.
What you should see: The bag stays puffy. You can squeeze it a little but not flat, because air takes up space.
Why it matters: Air is a gas, and gases are matter too even though we cannot see them.

 Concept 3
Properties help us identify materials
 We can identify materials by observing properties like hardness, magnetism, solubility, and texture.
 Try it
 Property sort
objects: paper clip, eraser, wood chip, plastic bead.
“Sort by hard/soft, magnetic/not magnetic, sinks/floats.”
Predict first: Which of the four things will be magnetic? Which will float?
You need: a paper clip, an eraser, a wood chip, a plastic bead, a magnet, a bowl of water.
Hold the magnet to each object. Is it magnetic? Write yes or no.
Drop each object in water. Does it sink or float?
Press each object. Hard or soft?
What you should see: The paper clip is magnetic and sinks. The wood chip floats. The eraser is soft. The plastic bead is hard and usually sinks.
Why it matters: Properties are clues that tell materials apart.

 Concept 4
Mixtures can be separated
 A mixture keeps the properties of its parts, so we can often separate it.
 [SVG IMAGE] Salt that dissolves is still in the water as bits too small to see. Filtering removes the sand, and evaporating leaves the salt behind.Text version of this diagram
Four steps left to right. 1 Mix: salt grains and sand in a cup of water. 2 Stir: the salt dissolves, so the salt bits spread through the water as dots too small to see, and the sand stays at the bottom. 3 Filter: the sand stays on the filter paper and the salt water drips into the cup below. 4 Evaporate: the water leaves a dish and white salt crystals remain. Dissolved salt is not gone. Filtering takes out the sand, and evaporating brings the salt back.
Mix, stir, filter and evaporate, and zoom in on the salt.

 Concept 5
Changes can be physical or chemical, but matter is conserved
 Heating, cooling, mixing, and dissolving may change appearance, but total matter stays the same; some changes make new substances.
 [SVG IMAGE] Mass before and after. The sealed cup of melting ice stays 82 g. The sealed bag with baking soda and vinegar stays 45 g. Each pair of bars is the same height: matter did not disappear.Text version of this graph
Grouped bar graph of mass in grams. Sealed cup of ice melting: 82 before, 82 after. Sealed bag with baking soda and vinegar: 45 before, 45 after. Each pair is the same height.
 Diagram
 Physical vs chemical
ice melting vs fizzing bag.
“Melting is still water. Fizzing can make a new gas.”
 [SVG IMAGE] A physical change changes form or shape, but not what the stuff is. A chemical change makes new stuff.Text version of this diagram
Two panels. Physical change: ice melts into water, which is still water and can freeze again. Chemical change: vinegar and baking soda in a bag make bubbles of new gas, a new substance.
 Try it
 Sealed bag reaction
zip bag with vinegar and baking soda sealed inside.
“Mix inside the bag. The bag puffs with gas. If sealed, nothing escapes.”
Predict first: What will happen when the vinegar and baking soda meet? Will the bag weigh more, less, or the same?
You need: a zip bag, 2 spoons baking soda, a small cup with 1/4 cup vinegar, a kitchen scale, safety glasses.
Put the baking soda in the bag. Stand the cup of vinegar upright inside without spilling it. Zip the bag.
Weigh the whole bag and write the number.
Tip the cup over to mix inside the bag. Watch.
Weigh the sealed bag again.
What you should see: The bag puffs up and the number on the scale stays the same (or nearly).
Why it matters: Matter is not lost in a change. The gas that formed is still in the bag.

 Try itWhat you need
zip bags, cups, spoons, salt, sugar, sand, coffee filters, rubber bands, small bags of baking soda and vinegar, two balloons, straw or hanger for balance, paper clips, small magnet, paper towels.
Safety: wash hands after sand/soil/salt work; adult handles warm water; keep magnets away from Chromebooks; keep iron filings in a closed bag and hands away from eyes; use a spoonful of baking soda and a splash of vinegar so the sealed bag puffs without popping; seal it over a tray and look away from the bag while it fizzes. An open bag lets gas escape, so its mass drops: that is a leak, not proof that gas weighs nothing.

 Words to know

gasmatter that spreads out to fill space.
mixturetwo or more substances mixed but not chemically joined.
solutiona mixture where one substance dissolves.
propertya clue like color, hardness, or magnetism.
dissolvewhen a substance breaks into tiny pieces in a liquid.
conserveto stay the same total amount.
 Tutor notes: misconceptions, standards, pacing
 Pacing: five 30-minute sessions.
 Common misconceptions to listen for:
“Air is nothing.” Correct: air is matter; it has mass and takes space.
“Only things you can see are matter.” Correct: matter includes things you cannot see, like air.
“Dissolved stuff disappears.” Correct: it is still there as tiny particles.
“Dissolved stuff becomes air or water.” Correct: it remains as the same substance, mixed in tiny pieces.
“Dissolving makes a new substance.” Correct: dissolving is a mixture; the substances stay themselves.
“Particles get bigger when heated.” Correct: particles move more; the substance may expand.
“Melting makes a new substance.” Correct: melting is a physical change; it is still the same substance.
“Gas has no weight.” Correct: gas has mass.
 Limits and cautions:
Without a good balance, conservation of mass is harder to show. A homemade hanger balance helps but is less precise. The full path is weighing and graphing your own measurements; the provided dataset is only a partial path (graphing given numbers), not measuring.
Chemical reactions are simplified: students observe signs of change but do not identify all products.
This unit does not cover temperature scales or detailed heat transfer; those belong in later energy units.
If magnets are used, keep them away from Chromebooks and cards.
