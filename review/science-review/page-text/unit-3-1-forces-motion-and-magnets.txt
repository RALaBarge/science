 Forces, Motion, and Magnets
 Why do objects speed up, slow down, turn, or stay still?
 Forces explain why things speed up, slow down, turn or stay put. You will compare balanced and unbalanced forces, use patterns in motion to predict what happens next, explore magnets and static electricity that push and pull without touching, and design a way to move something using magnets.

 Concept 1
Balanced and unbalanced forces
 Balanced forces keep motion the same; unbalanced forces change motion.
 [SVG IMAGE] Balanced forces do not change motion. An object can be moving and still have balanced forces. Unbalanced forces change the motion.Text version of this diagram
Two pictures of an object with force arrows. Top: a push forward and friction backward are the same length, so the forces are balanced and the object keeps moving at the same steady speed. Bottom: the push forward is longer than the friction arrow, so the forces are unbalanced and the object speeds up.
Push a puck from both sides and see what balanced and unbalanced forces do.

 Concept 2
Motion patterns help predict
 If motion repeats, we can predict what happens next.
 [SVG IMAGE] Equal gaps between dots mean steady speed. A real ball on a floor slows down, so its dots bunch closer together.Text version of this diagram
Two dot tapes, each showing a ball position every second from 0 to 4 seconds. Top, a model ball with no friction: the dots are equally far apart, so the speed is steady. Bottom, a real ball on a floor: the gaps between dots get smaller each second, so the ball is slowing down, and friction pushes against the motion. Close dots mean slow and far apart dots mean fast.
Roll a ball on ice or on a real floor and read the dot tape.

 Concept 3
Forces without touching
 Magnets and static electricity can push or pull without direct touch.
 [IMG: Photo of a red horseshoe magnet holding two steel paper clips. A penny and a piece of aluminum foil lie beside it, not touching it.] A magnet pulls steel paper clips. The penny and the foil do not stick.
Test which things a magnet pulls, from how far, and through paper.
 Try it
 Magnet explorer
magnet near paper clip, penny, foil, eraser.
“Test many objects. Which ones stick? Write yes or no for each one.”
Predict first: Which things will stick? Write yes or no for each before you test.
Line up a paper clip, a penny, a piece of foil and an eraser.
Hold the magnet to each one. Does it stick or get pulled?
Write yes or no in a small table.
What you should see: The paper clip sticks. The penny, foil and eraser do not. Magnets pull on things with iron in them, not on every metal.
Why it matters: Seeing which things a magnet pulls helps you sort materials by what they do.

 Concept 4
Magnet design problem
 We can use magnet ideas to move something without touching it directly.
 Try it
 Paper-clip mover
paper clip, card, magnet under the card.
“Move the clip without touching it. Plan. Test. Improve.”
Predict first: Will a magnet under the card still move the clip on top? How far apart can they be?
Put the clip on the card. Hold the magnet under the card.
Slide the magnet slowly. Watch the clip follow.
Measure how far the clip travels. Is it 20 cm or more?
Change one thing (thicker card, or a different path) and test again.
What you should see: The clip follows because the magnet pulls through the card. A thinner card gives a stronger pull.
Why it matters: This is design: test, find what failed, change one thing, test again.

 Hands-on · Design or investigate
Try it at home or in class
 Design a paper-clip mover that moves a paper clip at least 20 cm without touching the clip directly. Criteria: the clip moves the full distance. Constraints: one magnet, one paper clip, paper or card, and no direct touching. Students test, record evidence, and improve the design.
 [SVG IMAGE] What the paper-clip mover kit looks like from the side.Text version of this diagram
Side view. A cardboard sheet rests like a bridge on two stacks of books. A paper clip sits on top of the cardboard at the start line. A magnet is held under the cardboard and moved along, and the clip follows it toward the finish line, at least 20 centimeters away. Nobody touches the clip.
 Try itWhat you need
toy car, cardboard ramp, books, tape, small ball, index cards, pencil, paper, 2 ring or bar magnets, paper clips, balloon, small paper bits, string.
Safety: keep magnets away from Chromebooks and other electronics. Use only large ceramic or ferrite magnets; small, very strong magnets can be swallowed and are dangerous. Keep every magnet out of mouths. Do the water-stream static demo over a tray, keep water away from Chromebooks, and dry hands before touching a keyboard.
 Try itWhat you do
test gentle vs harder pushes; roll a ball and mark positions; make a dot diagram; test magnets on objects; do the balloon static demo; design a paper-clip mover that moves without direct touch.
Push a toy car gently, then harder. Say which push changed its motion more (10 minutes).
Roll a ball down a ramp and mark where it is each second. Draw the dots (10 minutes).
Test a magnet on a few objects. Write yes or no (10 minutes).
Do the balloon demo: rub it on hair, hold it over paper bits (5 minutes).
Design the paper-clip mover. Test, change one thing, test again (20 minutes).
Notice: Close dots mean slow. Far-apart dots mean fast. A magnet and a charged balloon both act without touching.
Why it matters: Each step gives you evidence for balanced forces, patterns in motion, and pushes or pulls at a distance.

 Tutor notes: misconceptions, standards, pacing
 Pacing: six 30-minute sessions.
 Common misconceptions to listen for:
“A force is needed to keep an object moving.” Correct: unbalanced forces change motion; balanced forces do not change motion.
“Magnets attract all metals.” Correct: magnets attract things made of iron and some steels; most other metals, like aluminum foil and copper, do not stick.
“Forces only work when objects touch.” Correct: magnetic and static-electric forces can act without touching.
“A bigger push always makes an object go forever.” Correct: forces can change motion, but friction and other forces can slow objects.
 Limits and cautions:
Gravity is not taught here; it moves to 5.2.
Simple machines are not a grade 3 PE in this unit. They appear only as an application note if the teacher chooses: “A ramp spreads a lift over a longer path.”
Static-electricity demos can fail on humid days; the provided question prompts and magnet investigation still earn 3-PS2-3.
Friction is simplified as a force that can slow motion. Deeper force models come in later grades.
