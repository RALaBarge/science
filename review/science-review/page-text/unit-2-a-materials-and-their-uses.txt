 Materials and Their Uses
 How do we choose and change materials to make useful things?
 Everything we use is made of something, and the choice of material matters. You will sort materials by what they are like, pick the best one for a job, take objects apart and rebuild them from the same pieces, and find out which changes from heating and cooling can be undone and which cannot.

 Concept 1
Materials have properties we can observe
 We can sort materials by things like hardness, softness, flexibility, and whether they keep water out.
Sort everyday things into waterproof, bendy and hard bins.
 Analogy
 A closet with rain boots, mittens, and paper.
“Rubber keeps water out. Paper gets wet.”
 Try it

“Put all hard things here.”

 Concept 2
We choose materials for jobs
 The best material depends on what we need it to do.
 Try it
 Guess first: Which material will keep a paper note dry: paper, foil, cloth, or plastic wrap?
What you need: a small spray bottle of water, a tray, paper notes (about the size of a card), pieces of paper, foil, cloth, and plastic wrap.
Do: Put a note on the tray. Cover it with one material. Spray it 3 times from the same distance. Lift the cover and check the note: dry or wet? Do the same with each material, with a fresh note.
Look for: Which materials left the note dry? Which let water through?
What it shows: The best material depends on the job. For keeping dry, we pick a material that keeps water out.
Safety: Wipe up spills so no one slips.
“Which one keeps the note dry?”
 Data
 Table with dry/wet results.
“Foil: dry. Cloth: wet.”

 Concept 3
Pieces can be taken apart and rebuilt

 Concept 4
Heating and cooling can change materials
 Some changes caused by warming or cooling can be undone, and some cannot.
 [IMG: Photo of ice cubes melting in a glass bowl with a puddle of water, and a pat of butter softening in a pan.] Warming melts ice and butter. These changes can be undone by cooling.
 Analogy
 Some changes have an “undo” button, and some do not. Melting ice = a change with undo (freeze it again). Cooking an egg = no undo (it will not turn raw again).
Ask: if I cool it or warm it back, will it go back?
“Some changes can undo. Some cannot.”
 Try it
 Guess first: Will the ice turn back into ice after it melts? Will a cooked egg or toast turn back?
What you need: an ice cube in a small cup, a freezer or cold spot, butter or crayon wax, pictures of a cooked egg and toast.
Do: Put an ice cube in a cup and let it melt (an adult may use warm water). Look at the water. Put the cup in a freezer and check again later. Look at pictures of an egg before and after cooking, and bread before and after toasting.
Look for: Ice to water to ice again: it goes back. Egg or toast: it does not go back.
What it shows: Some heating or cooling changes can be undone, and some cannot.
Safety: An adult handles anything warm. No tasting science materials.
“Ice becomes water. Water can become ice again.”

 Hands-on · Design or investigate
Try it at home or in class
 Rainproof Note. Problem: a paper note must stay dry. Students sketch, choose two materials, test each with 3 sprays of water, record dry/wet, and choose the best material using evidence. Criteria: the note stays dry, and the cover bends around the note. If two materials both stay dry, the second criterion decides. Constraints: use kit materials and keep the test fair (same number of sprays, same distance).

 Try itWhat you need
assorted safe objects (wood, plastic, metal, fabric, paper, rubber), tray, paper notes, spray bottle, foil, cloth, plastic wrap, blocks/beads/paper chain, ice cube, small cup, butter or crayon wax, picture cards for cooked egg/toast, paper, crayons.
Safety: Adult handles warm things. No tasting. Wash hands after handling materials, especially food items. No coin cells or magnets are required.
 Try it
 What you do
 1. Sort (10 minutes). Sort objects by hard or soft, bendy or stiff, waterproof or not.
 2. Rainproof Note (20 minutes). Pick two materials. Spray each 3 times, same distance. Record dry or wet. Choose the better one.
 3. Take apart and rebuild (10 minutes). Use blocks or beads to make something new from the same pieces.
 4. Heating and cooling (15 minutes). Watch ice or butter change, then argue: can it change back? What is your evidence?

 Tutor notes: misconceptions, standards, pacing
 Pacing: 5 sessions of 30 minutes.
 Common misconceptions to listen for:
“We sort materials only by size.” Correct: we sort by properties such as hard, soft, bendy, waterproof.
“All changes can be undone.” Correct: some changes can be reversed; some cannot.
“Only water can melt and freeze.” Correct: other materials, like butter, can melt and harden with safe warming and cooling.
“Heating only makes things feel warm.” Correct: heating can melt or soften; cooling can harden or freeze.
“A material’s job is the same as its property.” Correct: a property describes the material; a job is how we use it.
 Limits and cautions:
Heating/cooling must stay safe: use ice, room temperature, sunlight, or adult-handled warm water only. Adult handles warm things.
If you cannot refreeze live, the provided dataset can still support an evidence-based argument, but live observation is stronger.
2-PS1-3 works best with blocks, beads, or paper chains; avoid glued objects.
Food materials are for observing only; no tasting.
If a teacher adds a light or magnet extension, use ready-made lamps only and keep magnets away from Chromebooks.
