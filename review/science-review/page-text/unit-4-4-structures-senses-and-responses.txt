 Structures, Senses, and Responses
 How do body parts and senses help living things survive?
 Every living thing is built with parts that help it survive. This unit covers the jobs of outside parts like roots, fur and beaks, the jobs of inside parts like hearts, lungs and plant tubes, and how senses gather information that the brain uses to guide a response.

 Concept 1
External structures have jobs
 Outside parts such as roots, leaves, fur, beaks, and spines help organisms get needs, grow, and stay safe.

 Concept 3
Senses send information and the body responds
 Sense organs collect information, the brain processes it, and the body responds.
 [SVG IMAGE] A sense takes in information, the brain works on it, and then the body responds.Text version of this diagram

 Try it
 Ruler-drop reaction
One student holds a ruler, and another student catches it when it drops. The dropper lets go without warning and gives no hint of when, so the catcher cannot guess the moment. Do the test 5 times for each cue, and write down every catch mark.
“Your eye sees the drop. Your brain says catch. Your hand moves.”
Worked reading: if you catch at 10 cm, that is faster than catching at 14 cm. The farther down, the slower the response.

 Data
 Reaction data
catch marks on the ruler for 5 tries with each cue, plus the backup dataset.
“Record every catch mark. Do 5 tries with your eyes on the ruler, then 5 tries with your eyes closed and a sound cue. Use the middle mark for each cue, not your best one. Backup dataset: sight 12 cm, 10 cm, 9 cm; sound cue 14 cm, 12 cm, 13 cm. The farther down the ruler you catch it, the slower you were.”
This is a rough comparison, because the dropper lets go with no warning and the sound cue also depends on when the dropper says “now.” In the backup data the middle marks are 10 cm (sight) and 13 cm (sound). That gap is not bigger than the gap between the tries, so the backup data does not show that one cue is faster. Swap roles and see if your own 5-try results hold.
 Hands-on · Design or investigate
Try it at home or in class
 Fair-test investigation: “Which sense gives a quicker response: sight or sound?” Students compare two ways to start the catch: watching the ruler with eyes open, and eyes closed with a sound cue (the dropper says “now” at the moment of release). The same two students keep ruler height and hand position the same, and the dropper lets go without warning, after a different secret wait each time and with no hint of when. They do 5 tries for sight and 5 tries for the sound cue, record every catch mark, and use the middle mark for each cue. They only say one cue is faster if the difference between the two middle marks is bigger than the gap between the tries at that cue. They write one claim with evidence. The sound cue also depends on how well the dropper times the word, so they say it is a rough comparison, then swap roles and see whether the result holds.

 Tutor notes: misconceptions, standards, pacing
 Pacing: five 30-minute sessions.
 Common misconceptions to listen for:
“Only outside body parts matter.” Correct: internal structures have jobs too.
“Plant parts are just decorations.” Correct: plant structures help survival, growth, and reproduction.
“Plant parts are not real structures.” Correct: roots, stems, leaves, and flowers are structures with jobs.
“Responses happen without information.” Correct: senses collect information, the brain processes it, and the body responds.
“Senses and responses are the same thing.” Correct: senses collect information; responses are actions after processing.
“The brain is not part of the body’s response system.” Correct: the brain processes information and helps the body respond.
 Limits and cautions:
Internal structures are simplified; cells and organ systems are not taught here. Those belong in later units.
Ruler-drop distance is evidence of reaction time, not an exact time measurement. The sound-cue version also depends on the dropper’s timing, so it is a rough comparison. Students do 5 tries per cue, use the middle mark, and say one cue is faster only if the gap between cues is bigger than the gap between tries. The unit says all of this.
Plant internal transport is simplified; the unit avoids xylem/phloem detail.
The unit does not cover inherited traits or ecosystems; those are in 3.3, 3.4, and 5.3.
