 Circuits and Energy Conversion
 How can we make a safe circuit that changes electrical energy into useful light?
 A working circuit is a complete loop, and building one safely shows how electrical energy changes into other kinds. You will test which materials let current flow, use switches to open and close a circuit, and see electrical energy turn into light, sound, motion or heat.

 Hands-on · Design or investigate
Try it at home or in class
 Design a “light-up card” with a switch. Criteria: the light turns on when the switch is closed. Constraints: one coin cell, one LED, foil or copper tape, paper. Students test, find why it fails if it is dark, and refine the circuit.
 [SVG IMAGE] What the light-up card looks like: tape, coin cell, LED and a paper clip switch in one loop.Text version of this diagram
Top view of an index card with a coin cell on the left, an LED in the middle and a paper clip switch on the right. Strips of copper tape connect them in one loop: from the plus side of the cell to the LED long leg, from the LED short leg to the first brass fastener, then through the paper clip to the second fastener and back along the bottom to the cell. When the clip touches both fasteners the loop is complete and the LED lights. When the clip is swung away the loop is open and the LED is off. The LED lights only when its long leg is toward the plus side of the cell.

 Try itWhat you need
coin cells, small LEDs, copper tape or aluminum foil, binder clips, paper clips, brass fasteners, index cards, tape, paper, pencil.
Safety: coin cells only; never wall current. Tape over coin cells when stored. Check LED orientation; the LED lights only one way. A small LED can run straight from one coin cell because the cell limits the current; never use a bigger battery or wall power. If foil or a coin cell feels warm, unhook it right away: a wire joining both ends of the cell is a short circuit and gets hot. An adult hands out and collects every coin cell; a swallowed coin cell is a medical emergency. Check battery freshness. If magnets are used for extensions, keep magnets away from Chromebooks.
 Try itWhat you do
Build a simple circuit. Test conductors and insulators. Make a paper-clip switch. Design and refine a light-up card.
Light the LED with a battery and foil (10 minutes).
Test five things in the gap: paper clip, foil, string, plastic, wood (10 minutes).
Make a paper-clip switch and use it to turn the light on and off (10 minutes).
Make the light-up card. If it stays dark, check: loop closed? LED the right way? Foil touching the battery? (20 minutes).
Notice: A circuit needs an unbroken loop. Metals let current through; plastic and wood do not.
Why it matters: Finding why a circuit fails is how real engineers fix them.

 Tutor notes: misconceptions, standards, pacing
 Pacing: five 30-minute sessions.
 Common misconceptions to listen for:
“Electricity is used up in the bulb.” Correct: energy transfers; current flows in a loop.
“One wire is enough.” Correct: a complete path is needed.
“The battery stores current like water.” Correct: batteries store chemical energy; current is moving charge.
“All circuits are dangerous.” Correct: small battery circuits are safe, but wall electricity is dangerous.
“LEDs work either direction.” Correct: LED lights only one way; flip it if it is dark.
“Plastic and wood conduct electricity like metal.” Correct: metals conduct; plastic and wood are insulators.
“An open switch still lets current flow.” Correct: an open switch breaks the path.
 Limits and cautions:
4-PS3-2 is partly earned here; sound, light, and heat transfer belong in 4.1.
Coin cells are safe for this grade, but they are a swallowing hazard for younger siblings; tape them when stored.
LEDs can stay dark if reversed or if the battery is weak; check orientation and battery freshness.
The unit does not teach voltage, series/parallel circuits, or current measurement.
