 Life Cycles and Traits
 How do living things grow, and why are offspring like but not exactly like their parents?
 Living things grow and change, and their young are like their parents but not identical. You will compare the life cycles of a frog, a bean plant and a mealworm beetle, see how traits are inherited and still vary, and find out how the environment can change the way a trait shows up.

 Concept 1
Life cycles are diverse but share stages
 A frog, a bean plant, and a mealworm beetle have different life cycles, but all include birth, growth, reproduction, and death.

 Concept 2
Offspring inherit traits but vary
 Young organisms often get traits from parents, but they are not exact copies.
 [SVG IMAGE] Each parent bean made 10 offspring seeds. Most look like the parent, but not all.Text version of this graph
Grouped bar graph of 10 offspring seeds per parent. Yellow parent: 8 yellow, 2 green. Green parent: 9 green, 1 yellow. Spotted parent: 6 spotted, 4 yellow.
 Try it
 Trait survey
beans of different colors/sizes.
“Sort beans. They are similar but not identical.”
Predict first: Will every bean in the bag look the same?
You need: a handful of 20 dried beans of one kind, a paper plate, a ruler.
Spread the 20 beans on the plate.
Pick two traits, such as color (light or dark) and length (longer or shorter than 1.5 cm).
Sort the beans into piles for each trait. Count each pile.
Write the counts in a tally table.
Notice: The beans are all the same kind, but they are not identical. Some are darker or longer.
Why it matters: Living things of one kind share traits and still differ a little. That difference is called variation.

 Concept 3
Environment can influence traits
 The same kind of plant can grow differently in sun and in shade; environment can change how a trait shows up.

 Try it
 Sun vs shade beans
two cups with beans, one in light, one in dark or shade.
“Same seeds. Same water. One in sun. One in shade.”
You did this before / what is new: You grew beans in 2-B to ask what plants need; now you use the evidence to explain environment-influenced traits. New here: keep the seeds and water the same and change only the light, then measure height in centimeters and record color, stem thickness, and leaf size, because the taller shade plant is not the healthiest. One plant is not the finding: grow several plants for each condition, repeat the test, and compare.

 Hands-on · Design or investigate
Try it at home or in class
 Fair test: Does light affect bean sprout growth? Change only light. Keep water, container, and seed type the same. Measure and graph sprout height, record color and stem thickness, or use the provided dataset if time or weather is a problem.
 [IMG: Illustration of a teacher and two students looking at two bean seedlings in clear cups on a windowsill, one short and green and one paler and taller.] Comparing the bean seedlings: only the light changed, so differences are evidence about light.
 Try itWhat you need
bean seeds, clear cups, paper towels or soil, water, magnifier, index cards, tape, pictures or drawings of frog, bean plant, and mealworm beetle life stages.
Safety: do not eat seeds; wash hands after handling soil.
 Try itWhat you do
build life-cycle models; sort beans or leaves by traits; analyze parent-offspring data; grow or examine beans in sun and shade.
Put the stage cards in order for frog, bean, and mealworm beetle (10 minutes).
Sort 20 beans by two traits and tally them (10 minutes).
Set up two cups with a bean each: one in a window, one in a dark cupboard. Same water, same cup (10 minutes today, then 2 minutes each day for two weeks).
Measure height in cm. Also note color and stem thickness.
Notice: The plant in the dark often grows taller but thin and pale. Look at more than height.
Why it matters: The shape and color of a plant depends on both its inheritance and its environment.

parent-offspring bean data: yellow parent produces 8 yellow and 2 green offspring seeds; green parent produces 9 green and 1 yellow; spotted parent produces 6 spotted and 4 yellow. Sun/shade data: same seed type, same water, sun plant 18 cm with a thick green stem and wide leaves; shade plant 22 cm with a thin pale stem and small leaves, after two weeks. What is new here: 2-B asked what plants need, with sun and water changed one at a time. This unit keeps water the same, changes only sun or shade, and asks how the environment changes a trait. Use two or more plants per condition, because one plant can differ from the next for reasons unrelated to light. Do not use the bean seed-coat colors as proof of what the plant passes on; use the parent-offspring counts as model data.
 Near you: Michigan dry bean fields grow many bean varieties; comparing seedlings of different varieties (plant height, leaf size) gives students real trait data.

 Tutor notes: misconceptions, standards, pacing
 Pacing: five 30-minute sessions.
 Common misconceptions to listen for:
“All life cycles look the same.” Correct: life cycles are diverse, but all include birth, growth, reproduction, and death.
“Offspring are exact copies of parents.” Correct: offspring inherit traits but also vary.
“Environment cannot change traits.” Correct: environment can influence how some traits show up.
“Plants eat soil.” Correct: plants use water, air, and light to grow; soil provides support and minerals.
 Limits and cautions:
This unit does not teach cells; full cell work belongs in later grades.
It does not teach genetics mechanisms or natural selection. Those come later.
Plant growth can take days; the provided dataset guarantees evidence if plants grow slowly.
The idea that environment influences traits is simplified to visible growth differences.
