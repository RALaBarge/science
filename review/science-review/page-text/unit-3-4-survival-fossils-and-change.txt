 Survival, Fossils, and Change
 How do living things survive, and what happens when their world changes?
 Surviving depends on traits, groups and the place a living thing lives. This unit looks at how animal groups help their members, what fossils tell us about past life and places, how small differences can give an advantage, and how people weigh the costs and benefits of solutions when habitats change.

 Concept 5
Solutions when environments change
 When habitats change, people can propose solutions, but each solution has costs and benefits.
 [IMG: Photo of a wetland with cattails, still water, a frog on a log and ducks.] A wetland is a habitat. Filling it in changes who can live there.

 Tutor notes: misconceptions, standards, pacing
 Pacing: six 30-minute sessions.
 Common misconceptions to listen for:
“Animal groups always help every member.” Correct: groups can help, but there are trade-offs such as competition for food.
“Fossils are real bones that are still alive.” Correct: fossils are preserved traces or mineralized remains.
“Every variation helps.” Correct: some variations help, some do not, depending on the environment.
“One habitat can fit all organisms.” Correct: some organisms survive well, some less well, and some not at all.
“Solutions have no costs.” Correct: every solution has trade-offs.
 Limits and cautions:
Natural selection is not taught in depth; this unit only introduces variation and advantage.
Fossil interpretation is simplified; real fossil evidence can be incomplete.
Habitat-change solutions are classroom models; real community decisions involve more science, law, and cost.
The deer-fur model is simplified to make variation visible; teachers should avoid saying all deer turn white in winter.
