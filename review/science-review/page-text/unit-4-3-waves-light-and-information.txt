 Waves, Light, and Information
 How do waves, light, and patterns carry energy and information?
 Waves are patterns that repeat, and they can carry both energy and information. You will learn the parts of a wave, how light reflecting into the eye lets us see, and how patterns of light or sound can send a message that we can compare for how well it works.

 Concept 1
Wave patterns have amplitude and wavelength
 Waves make repeating patterns, and those patterns can carry energy that moves small objects.
 [IMG: Photo of a yellow rubber duck riding gentle waves on a lake.] A wave moves forward, but the duck mostly moves up and down.
 [SVG IMAGE] Wavelength is crest to crest. Amplitude is the height above the resting line. The wave moves forward while the cork moves up and down.Text version of this diagram
A blue wave line over a dashed resting line. Wavelength is the distance from one crest to the next crest. Amplitude is the height of the wave above the resting line. A cork floats on the wave and mostly moves up and down while the wave moves forward to the right. A taller wave, with more amplitude, carries more energy.
Change a rope wave’s height and length and watch the cork bob.
 [SVG IMAGE] A small wave moved the floating object 1 cm. A large wave moved it 3 cm. Bigger waves carry more energy.Text version of this graph

 Concept 2
Light, reflection, and seeing
 We see objects when light reflects from them and enters our eyes.

Diagram of a lamp on the left, an apple in the middle and an eye on the right. Arrow 1 goes from the lamp to the apple. Arrow 2 goes from the apple into the eye. No arrow leaves the eye. Light from the lamp bounces off the apple and enters the eye, which is how we see it.

 Try it
 Mirror bounce
flashlight and mirror or shiny foil.
“Aim the mirror. The light changes direction.”
 [SVG IMAGE] A mirror bounces light so it leaves at the same angle it arrived. Tilt the mirror and the beam goes somewhere else.Text version of this diagram
A flashlight on the left sends a beam down to a mirror lying flat. The beam bounces off and goes up to the right. A dashed line stands straight up from the mirror; the beam comes in at the same angle on one side as it leaves on the other.
Predict first: If you aim the flashlight at the mirror from the left, where will the spot on the wall appear?
You need: a flashlight, a small mirror or foil on cardboard, a wall, a dim room.
Aim the flashlight at the mirror at a low angle.
Find the bright spot on the wall.
Tilt the mirror a little. Watch the spot move.
What you should see: The spot jumps to a new place as you tilt the mirror. Light bounces off in a new direction.
Why it matters: Mirrors and shiny things bounce light; that is part of how we see.

 Concept 3
Patterns can transfer information
 A pattern of light or sound can carry a message, and we can compare which pattern system works better.
 Try it
 Signal code
flashlight blinking short and long.
“Short flash, long flash. A pattern can mean something.”
Predict first: Can a partner tell short and long flashes apart across the room?
You need: a flashlight, a dim room, paper and pencil.
Make a code card: short flash = A, long flash = B, short-short = C.
Send one letter at a time. Partner writes what they see.
Switch and try a three-letter word.
What you should see: You may miss some. Slow down and make short and long very different.
Why it matters: This is how real signals, like Morse code, carry information with patterns.
 Real world
 Traffic light
red, yellow, green lights.
“Color patterns tell drivers what to do.”
 [SVG IMAGE] A color pattern is a signal. Everyone agrees on what each color means.Text version of this diagram
A traffic light with a red, yellow and green lamp. Next to each lamp is its meaning: red means stop, yellow means get ready to stop, green means go.
 Try it
 Compare two signals
one team using flashlight code and another using drum or tap code.
“Send the same message two ways. Which one is clearer?”
Predict first: Which will your partner get right more often, flashlight or drum?
Pick a short message, such as "GO".
Send it five times with the flashlight from 5 meters. Count the correct ones.
Send it five times with a drum or tap. Count again.
Use the same distance and the same message for both.
What you should see: Sample from the page: flashlight 4 of 5, drum 3 of 5.
Why it matters: Comparing two designs with the same test shows which one works better.

 Hands-on · Design or investigate
Try it at home or in class
 Design and compare two signal systems: a flashlight code and a drum/tap code. Teams send the same short message across the room. Criteria: the partner decodes the message correctly at 5 meters. Constraints: no shouting, one pattern key, same distance for both systems. Students record accuracy and choose the better solution with evidence.

 Try itWhat you do
Make rope waves and water waves. Observe waves moving objects. Model light reflection with mirror and eye-path drawings. Design and compare two signal systems for accuracy.
Shake a rope or slinky to make tall and short waves (10 minutes).
Float a cork in a bowl and tap the water (5 minutes).
Draw the light path from lamp to object to eye (10 minutes).
Make a flashlight code and a drum code. Send the same message five times with each from 5 meters. Count the correct ones (20 minutes).
Notice: Waves carry energy and move things. Patterns can carry information.
Why it matters: Choose the better design by comparing counts, not by guessing.

 Tutor notes: misconceptions, standards, pacing
 Pacing: five 30-minute sessions.
 Common misconceptions to listen for:
“Waves are only water.” Correct: waves can be in water, in a rope, or in air (sound). Light also moves in a wave pattern, but it does not push a cork the way a water wave does.
“Light comes out of our eyes.” Correct: light enters our eyes.
“Objects we see make their own light.” Correct: many objects reflect light from another source.
“Sound only travels through air.” Correct: sound can travel through solids, liquids, and gases.
“Sound can travel through empty space.” Correct: sound needs matter.
“Patterns are only decorations.” Correct: patterns can carry information.
“Light cannot carry messages.” Correct: light patterns can carry messages.
“Waves cannot move objects.” Correct: waves can move small objects by transferring energy.
 Limits and cautions:
Wave terms are simplified; 4-PS4-1 does not require wave-speed math.
Light reflection uses ray models; real scattering is simplified.
Sound-moving-rice can be subtle; the provided dataset guarantees evidence.
4-PS4-3 is broad; this unit earns it only if students generate and compare at least two signal solutions with accuracy data.
