 Plants, Animals, and Habitats
 How do plants, animals, and homes depend on each other?
 Plants and animals depend on each other and on the places they live. You will test what plants need by changing one thing at a time, see how animals carry seeds and pollen, and compare how different habitats support different kinds of living things.

Do plants need sunlight and water?

 Concept 2
Animals move seeds and pollen
 Animals carry seeds and move pollen; these interactions help plants make new plants, and animals often get food from plants.
 [IMG: Photo of a robin perched on a branch with a red berry in its beak.] Birds eat berries and carry the seeds to new places. The berry is food for the bird.
 [IMG: Illustration of a grandfather in a sun hat and two children looking at a bumblebee on a purple coneflower in a meadow, one child pointing at the bee.] Bees visit flowers for nectar and carry pollen along, so plants can make seeds.
 Analogy
 An animal can be like a delivery helper. The bee = the delivery helper. Pollen = the package, delivered flower to flower. A bird carries seeds in its tummy or on its feet and drops them far away.
Where it breaks: bees and birds are not trying to deliver. They are just looking for food.
“A bee carries pollen. A bird carries seeds.”
 Diagram
 Flower → bee → flower; berry → bird → new place.
“Pollen moves. Seeds move. The bee gets nectar. The bird gets a berry.”
 [SVG IMAGE] Top: pollen moves from flower to flower on a bee, and the bee gets nectar. Bottom: a bird eats a berry and the seeds land in a new place, and the bird gets food.Text version of this picture

 Try it
 Guess first: Will your pipe-cleaner bee move the yellow powder from one paper flower to another?
What you need: two or three paper flowers, a pipe cleaner “bee”, yellow cornmeal or flour (pollen), a fuzzy sock, felt, burrs or Velcro dots.
Do: Sprinkle a little yellow powder in one flower. Touch the bee to it, then touch the bee to the next flower. Or, wear a fuzzy sock on a short walk through tall grass and see what sticks.
Look for: Did powder move to the second flower? Did seeds or hooks stick to the sock?
What it shows: Animals carry pollen and seeds without knowing it, and that helps plants make new plants.
Safety: Do not taste the powder, seeds, or berries. Wash hands after.
“Make a model. Move the powder.”

 Hands-on · Design or investigate
Try it at home or in class
 Seed or Pollen Mover Model. Problem: a plant needs help moving pollen or seeds. Students build a paper seed with hooks or a pipe-cleaner “bee,” test it 3 times on felt/fuzz or paper flowers, and record whether pollen or seeds moved. Criteria: moves or sticks in at least 2 of 3 tries. Constraints: use kit materials.
 Try itWhat you need
bean seeds, cups, soil or paper towels, water, sunny spot, dark box or cupboard, fuzz sock or felt, burrs or Velcro dots, paper flowers, pipe cleaners, yellow cornmeal or flour, Michigan habitat picture cards, tally paper, crayons.
Safety: Wash hands after soil, seeds, and outdoor materials. No tasting seeds, berries, or powder. No coin cells or magnets are required.
 Try it
 What you do
 1. Bean test (a few minutes a day for 2 weeks). Grow beans in sun with water, dark with water, and sun with no water. Measure height in paper clips on day 7 and day 14.
 2. Seed or pollen mover (25 minutes). Build a hooked paper seed or a pipe-cleaner bee. Test it 3 times and tally how many times it moved or stuck.
 3. Habitat counts (20 minutes). Count the kinds of living things in a same-size patch of two habitats, for the same time.
 No time or no good weather? Use this data
This data is a backup. It lets you practice reading evidence, but the standard expects you to collect your own when you can.
Real observation log is preferred. If plants or outdoor observations are not possible, this dataset is a partial path: analysis of provided data for 2-LS2-1 and 2-LS4-1. With the backup you do not plan, conduct, or observe anything: you read the tables, compare conditions or habitats, and explain what the numbers show. The full path is your own bean-test log and your own habitat counts; the backup only covers the compare-and-explain part. What is new here: sun and water are changed one at a time, and plants are checked at day 7 and day 14. Use two or more plants per condition, and judge by leaves and color, not height alone. A plant in the dark can grow tall but stay pale and thin (see 3.3 and 5.3), so one height number from one plant can mislead.  Tutor notes: misconceptions, standards, pacing
 Pacing: 5 sessions of 30 minutes.
 Common misconceptions to listen for:
“Plants eat soil.” Correct: plants use sunlight, water, and air to grow; soil gives water and minerals.
“Animals do not need plants.” Correct: animals depend on plants for food, shelter, and many interactions.
“A habitat is only where an animal sleeps.” Correct: a habitat provides food, water, shelter, and space.
“Seeds only fall straight down.” Correct: animals, wind, and water can move seeds.
“Bees only make honey.” Correct: bees also move pollen, which helps flowers make seeds and fruit.
 Limits and cautions:
Bean plants need days to weeks; start two weeks early. If you use the provided dataset, that is a partial path: students analyze provided data, they do not plan or conduct the test.
Outdoor seed walks need safe spaces; use felt or indoor burrs if needed.
Use cornmeal or flour instead of real pollen if allergies are a concern.
Wash hands after soil and outdoor materials.
If an extension uses lights or magnets, use ready-made lamps only and keep magnets away from Chromebooks.
