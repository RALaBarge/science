 Sky Patterns
 What patterns do we see in the sky, and how can we predict them?
 The sky follows patterns we can watch and predict. This unit covers why day and night repeat, how the Moon seems to change shape, why stars are still there in the daytime, and how the amount of daylight in Michigan changes with the seasons.

 Concept 1
Day and night repeat
 Earth spins, so places face toward or away from the Sun.
 [SVG IMAGE] The Sun stays put and Earth spins. A place facing the Sun has day. When Earth turns that place away, it has night.Text version of this diagram

After Earth spins half way, the bottom picture shows the red dot on the dark side, so it is night. The Sun did not turn off, your place turned away from it.

 Real world
 In Michigan, noon is bright and midnight is dark.
“Noon: light. Midnight: dark.”

 Concept 2
The Moon and stars have patterns
 The Moon’s shape seems to change, and stars are visible at night but not in the bright daytime sky.
 [SVG IMAGE] The stars are still in the sky in the daytime. The bright sky hides them.Text version of this diagram
Two sky pictures side by side with the same star positions. Left, night: a dark sky with stars and a crescent Moon, so the stars can be seen. Right, day: a bright blue sky with the Sun and no visible stars, but dashed circles show where the stars still are. Stars do not turn off in the day. They are faraway suns, and the bright daytime sky hides them.
Darken the sky and the stars appear. They were there all day.

 Concept 3
Seasons change the amount of daylight
 In Michigan, summer days have more daylight and winter days have less daylight.
 [IMG: Illustration of a father with a child on his shoulders and a grandmother in a park on a long summer evening with the sun still high.] In Michigan summer there is daylight late into the evening.
 [IMG: Illustration of a girl pulling a sled across a snowy park as a low orange sun sets behind bare trees.] In winter the Sun sets early, so there is less daylight.
 [SVG IMAGE] Michigan daylight strips, about 9 squares of light in December, 12 in March and 15 in June. Summer days have more daylight.Text version of this graph

 Data
 Paper-strip daylight graph: long strip for summer, short strip for winter.
“Summer strip: long. Winter strip: short.”
 Try it
 Guess first: In which month is the Sun up longer in Michigan, June or December?
What you need: two pictures of the same Michigan place: one in June and one in December, or paper strips and a ruler.
Do: Look at the two pictures. Where is the Sun? Is it light or dark? Next, cut a long strip for June and a short strip for December (the page’s graph shows about 15 and 9 squares of daylight).
Look for: In June it is light late in the evening. In December it gets dark early.
What it shows: Summer days have more daylight than winter days.
“June: light late. December: dark early.”
 Real world
 In Michigan the Sun is up for more hours in summer and fewer hours in winter.
“Summer: the Sun is up a long time. Winter: not so long.”
 Real world
 Michigan summer evenings stay light late.
“Summer light stays late.”
 Hands-on · Design or investigate
Try it at home or in class
 Paper-Plate Sundial. Students build a simple sundial with a paper plate, straw, and tape. On a sunny day, they mark the shadow at morning, noon, and afternoon. Criteria: the shadow marks change in a predictable way. If the sky is cloudy, use provided shadow-position cards to complete the pattern. This supports observation of predictable sky patterns.
 [IMG: Photo of a paper plate with a straw standing in its center on a sunny sidewalk. The straw shadow falls across the plate, away from the Sun.] Paper-plate sundial: the shadow points away from the Sun, and it moves as the day goes on.
 Try itWhat you need
ball, lamp or strong flashlight, paper plates, straw, tape, crayons, Moon-phase cards, paper strips, provided data cards.
Safety: Adult handles anything warm, such as a lamp that gets hot. Use an LED lamp if possible. Use a ready-made flashlight or LED lamp; do not open the battery compartment.
 Try it
 What you do
 1. Lamp and ball (10 minutes). Turn the ball slowly in front of the lamp. Which side is lit (day)? Which is dark (night)?
 2. Moon cards (10 minutes). Put the Moon-phase cards in order: crescent, half, round.
 3. Daylight strips (10 minutes). Make a long strip for June and a short strip for December.
 4. Sundial (30 minutes, sunny day). Mark the straw’s shadow in the morning, at noon, and in the afternoon. Notice that the shadow moves.
 No time or no good weather? Use this data
This data is a backup. It lets you practice reading evidence, but the standard expects you to collect your own when you can.
- Real observation log: Begin a daylight strip log in fall. Each week, students mark about how long it is light. They may also draw Moon shapes when visible. - Provided dataset: Michigan daylight strips for backup: December about 9 squares, March about 12 squares, June about 15 squares (each square is about one hour of daylight); Moon-phase card sequence; day/night star visibility cards. Use the provided dataset if weather, school schedule, or cloudy days prevent real observations. With the backup, you do not observe the sky yourself: you graph the three months as strips and say that more daylight comes with summer.  Tutor notes: misconceptions, standards, pacing
 Pacing: 5 sessions of 30 minutes.
 Common misconceptions to listen for:
“The Sun goes around Earth.” Correct: Earth rotates; the Sun appears to move across the sky.
“The Sun turns off at night.” Correct: the Sun shines; our part of Earth turns away.
“The Moon makes its own light.” Correct: the Moon reflects sunlight.
“The Moon is a lamp, fire, or star.” Correct: the Moon does not make its own visible light.
“Stars disappear during the day.” Correct: stars are still there, but the bright sky hides them.
“Stars turn off in daytime.” Correct: they remain in the sky, but we cannot see them in daylight.
“Summer happens because Earth is closer to the Sun.” Correct: Earth is not closer in summer. Day length changes with the seasons; the reason comes in later grades.
“Day length is the same everywhere and every season.” Correct: day length changes with season and place.
 Limits and cautions:
Sky observations need clear days. The full path is the student's own log; if observations cannot happen, the provided data are a partial path (analysis of provided data), not observations.
The daylight strip log should start in fall if possible; if the unit is taught in spring, use the provided dataset.
Moon phases do not require nightly observation; cards or short videos are acceptable.
The tilt explanation is simplified; do not say Earth is closer to the Sun in summer.
This unit does not teach Moon-phase causes in depth, constellation names, or planetary orbits. Those belong in later grades.
