How Science Works

Science is not a pile of facts to memorize. It is a way of finding things out that people have been improving for thousands of years: ask a question, make a prediction, test it fairly, and let the evidence decide. Every other unit uses these steps, so learn them first.

Start here. Before any content unit, this page explains where science came from and how scientists work: asking a question, making a testable hypothesis, running a fair test, and writing a claim with evidence and reasoning. Every later unit has a Method link in its tutor notes that points back to this page, so come back to it whenever a student asks "how do we know that?"

Deep dive (optional) · Where science came from

People have always asked "why?"

Deep dive (optional): this section is extra. You can skip it and still follow the rest of the unit; come back when you are curious.

Science grew out of a simple habit: look carefully, explain with natural causes, and check the explanation against the world. No single person or culture invented it. Egypt, Babylon, Greece, China, India, the Islamic world, the Maya, and many others each added tools and ideas.

Analogy

Gossip vs. detective

Gossip

"I heard the cafeteria is serving pizza!" It is exciting, so it spreads. Nobody checks the menu, and being wrong does not slow it down.

A detective

Walks to the cafeteria. Reads the menu. Asks the lunch staff. If the sign says tacos, the pizza story is dropped, no matter how many people repeated it.

Science is that second move. A claim is kept only if it survives a check against the world, not because it is popular or exciting.

Real world

When evidence beat tradition: handwashing, 1847

Dr. Ignaz Semmelweis noticed far more mothers died in the Vienna hospital ward staffed by doctors than in the ward staffed by midwives. He guessed doctors were carrying "cadaverous particles" from autopsies on their hands, made them wash with chlorine, and deaths fell sharply. The germ theory that explained why came later (Pasteur, Lister). Approximate figures. Evidence can be right before the explanation is complete.

Real world

Who is at the center? A 1,400-year argument settled by evidence

For most of history, almost everyone assumed the Earth sat still in the middle and the Sun, Moon, planets and stars circled it. It matches what your eyes tell you: you do not feel the ground move, and the Sun rises and sets. About 150 CE the Greek-Egyptian astronomer Ptolemy wrote it up as a mathematical model so good at predicting where the planets would be that it was used for well over a thousand years.

Earth-centered (Ptolemy)

In this model, planets ride on small circles that ride on big circles around the Earth. It works for predictions, but it needs a pile of extra circles to explain why planets sometimes seem to move backward in the sky.

Sun-centered (Copernicus)

In 1543 Nicolaus Copernicus published a model with the Sun in the middle and the Earth as one planet among several. The backward loops happen naturally: when Earth, on its faster inner track, passes a slower outer planet like Mars, that planet seems to slide backward for a while, like a car you pass on the highway. A Greek thinker, Aristarchus, had suggested something similar around 270 BCE, but there was no evidence yet.

Two ideas, both able to predict planet positions. The difference had to be settled by new evidence, not by which idea sounded right or which one more people believed.

Try it

Galileo's test: what phase can Venus show?

Through the fall of 1610 Galileo watched Venus through his telescope. It went through phases like our Moon, from a thin crescent to nearly full. In Ptolemy's model Venus always stays between us and the Sun, so it can never be more than a crescent. Venus looking nearly full means it can be on the far side of the Sun, which only fits a model where Venus circles the Sun.

Try it

Four moons that change places

In January 1610 Galileo saw "stars" next to Jupiter that changed places from night to night. He realized they were four moons orbiting Jupiter. So not everything circled the Earth. (Positions here are illustrative; the orbit times are real.)

How the evidence piled up

ClaimThe Earth and the other planets orbit the Sun.
EvidenceJupiter's moons (1610). Venus's phases (1610). Planets travel in ovals called ellipses (Kepler, 1609), which fit Tycho Brahe's careful measurements.
ReasoningVenus's phases rule out Ptolemy's model. A third model (Tycho Brahe's, where the planets circle the Sun but the Sun circles Earth) also fit them, so the case was not closed overnight. Newton's gravity (1687) explained why planets orbit the Sun, and in 1838 astronomers measured stars shifting slightly as the Earth moves (parallax), which finished it.

The pattern: the model changed because the evidence changed, even though many people did not want it to. That is science correcting itself.

Concept 2 · The scientific method

A loop, not a ladder

The scientific method is the set of steps scientists use to answer questions with evidence: question, research, hypothesis, experiment, analyze, conclude, share. Real scientists loop back and forth, because a conclusion usually raises a new question.

Analogy

Perfecting a recipe

Tasting the soup is collecting data. "It needs salt" is a hypothesis. One pinch is changing one variable. Tasting again is analyzing; if the soup is still bland, the loop repeats.

Where it breaks: the cook can taste the soup directly. Scientists often measure something they cannot see, so they need instruments and careful records.

Real world

Not a straight line: sometimes you change the test

When a test gives no answer, the idea is not always the problem. Sometimes the test is. A better tool, a bigger sample, or a smarter way to measure can turn "we can't tell" into a clear result. Scientists change the test, and the idea gets a fair chance.

Fix the testMeasure more carefully, or use a better instrument.
Change the ideaThe evidence points somewhere new, so revise the hypothesis.
Ask a new questionEvery answer leads to more questions.

Galileo tried to time lantern flashes between distant hills, but light is far too fast for people to time. In 1676, Ole Rømer noticed that eclipses of Jupiter's moon Io ran late when Jupiter was farther from Earth, and used that delay to estimate how fast light travels: about 220,000 km/s, roughly three-quarters of the true value of 299,792 km/s. The question never changed. The test did.

Real world

Science also begins by accident

In 1928, Alexander Fleming returned from vacation to find a mold (Penicillium) had landed on one of his bacteria dishes, and the bacteria around it had died. He observed, asked "why?", and tested the mold. It became the first antibiotic, penicillin, which has saved hundreds of millions of lives. His luck only mattered because he was paying attention and asked a question.

Concept 3 · Questions and hypotheses

Make a prediction you can test

A hypothesis is a testable explanation or prediction. A strong one follows the pattern "If [we change this], then [this will happen], because [reason]." It must be possible to measure, and possible to prove wrong.

Analogy

Weak predictionStrong prediction
"The weather will be interesting.""If the front arrives tonight, then tomorrow's high will be below 10 °C, because cold air follows the front."
Can never be wrongCan be checked with a thermometer

A prediction that cannot fail tells you nothing. A hypothesis is a forecast with a test attached: it says what will happen and what evidence would prove it wrong.

Hypothesis, theory, law: what's the difference?

WordMeaning in scienceExample
HypothesisA testable prediction, not yet confirmed."Plants in more light will grow taller."
LawA description of a pattern that always holds.Newton's law of gravity describes what happens.
TheoryA well-tested explanation backed by lots of evidence. It is not "just a guess".Plate tectonics, germ theory, evolution.

A hypothesis does not "graduate" into a theory. A theory explains why; a law describes what.

Concept 4 · Fair testing

Change one thing at a time

In a fair test you change only the independent variable, measure the dependent variable, and keep everything else the same (controlled variables). Then repeat enough times to trust the result.

Analogy

Cookie test

Sugar is the only difference between the batches, so it is the only thing that can explain a difference in taste. One change at a time is what makes a test fair.

Where it breaks: a real oven has hot spots. A careful baker would run several batches and compare averages, not judge from one cookie.

Try it

Why we repeat: the average settles down

Teaching simulation. Each trial of a plant's growth has natural variation (the true average is 20 cm). One trial can mislead; the average of many is far more reliable.

Real world

Clinical trials

New medicines are tested on thousands of people split randomly into two groups. One gets the medicine, the control group gets a look-alike without it. Neither the patients nor often the doctors know who gets which, a "double-blind" design, so expectations can't bias the result. The variable changed: the medicine. Everything else is balanced by chance and large numbers.

Concept 5 · From data to claim

Measure, graph, conclude

Scientists measure in metric units (SI), show data in the right kind of graph, and explain conclusions with claim, evidence, reasoning. Two things happening together (correlation) do not prove one causes the other.

Data toolbox (open when you need it)
  • Units: always write the unit with a number (4.6 m, not just 4.6). Use metric: meters, grams, liters, seconds.
  • Repeat trials: do the same test several times. One try can be a fluke.
  • Average (mean): add all the results, then divide by how many there are.
  • Variability: repeats are never exactly the same, because of small differences in your throw, the air, or how you measure. The range (smallest to biggest) shows how much they differ.
  • Pick the graph: bar graph to compare groups, line graph for change over time, scatter plot to see if two measurements go together.
  • Correlation is not causation: two things changing together does not prove one causes the other. Ice cream sales and sunburns both rise in summer; the hot sunny weather causes both.
  • Uncertainty: every measurement is a little bit off. Say "about 4.6 m, give or take 0.8 m" instead of pretending to be exact.

Tiny example: you throw a paper plane 5 times and measure 4.2, 5.0, 4.6, 3.8 and 5.4 meters. Total = 23.0 m. Average = 23.0 ÷ 5 = 4.6 m. Range = 3.8 m to 5.4 m, which is 1.6 m wide. So you could report: "about 4.6 m, and throws varied by about 1.6 m."

Analogy

Claim, Evidence, Reasoning (CER)

ClaimBean plants given more light grew taller.
EvidenceAfter 14 days the 12-hour group averaged 18 cm and the 4-hour group averaged 9 cm, across 10 plants each.
ReasoningPlants use light energy to make food (photosynthesis), so more light gives more food for growth. Everything else was kept the same.

Like a lawyer in court: do not just say what you think. Show the evidence, then explain why that evidence supports the claim. A claim with no evidence is just an opinion.

Deep dive (optional) · Reading light from far away

How do we know what a star is made of?

Deep dive (optional): this section is extra. You can skip it and still follow the rest of the unit; come back when you are curious.

Nobody has ever visited a star, yet we know what the Sun is made of. The secret: light carries a fingerprint of whatever made it.

Analogy

A barcode made of light

A store scanner does not taste your cereal. It reads the pattern of black and white lines on the box and knows exactly what it is.

Split light with a prism and it spreads into a rainbow, called a spectrum. Look closely at the light from a glowing gas and you do not see a smooth rainbow. You see a few bright colored lines. Each element makes its own pattern of lines, like its own barcode. Hydrogen's barcode is never the same as sodium's.

Try it

Read an element's barcode

These are real wavelengths of light, drawn in the colors your eyes would see. (Your screen only approximates the colors.)

Try it

Mystery star: what is it made of?

A star's light passes through its own outer gases, which soak up exactly the colors those elements would give off. That leaves dark lines in the star's rainbow. Switch on elements below to see their bright-line barcodes lined up under the star. If they match up with the dark lines, that element is in the star.

Real world

Helium was found on the Sun before it was found on Earth

On August 18, 1868, during a total solar eclipse seen from India, astronomer Pierre Janssen noticed a bright yellow line in the Sun's spectrum. It sat close to the known sodium lines but did not match them. Two months later Norman Lockyer in England saw the same line. They concluded it came from an element nobody had found yet and named it helium, after Helios, the Greek word for the Sun. Helium was not found on Earth until 1895, 27 years later.

That is the power of the method: we learned what the Sun is made of, and discovered a new element, using nothing but its light.

Peek ahead: why does every element make different lines?

You will learn about atoms soon. Here is a teaser. Every atom has electrons around its center, and an electron can only sit on certain "steps," like a staircase. It cannot hover between steps. When an electron drops down a step, the atom gives off a little bit of light, and the size of the drop decides the color.

Every element has its own spacing of steps, so each one gives off its own set of colors. For example, the red line of hydrogen (at 656 nm) comes from an electron dropping from the third step to the second. That is also why starlight has dark lines: electrons in the star's outer gas climb those same steps by soaking up exactly those colors.

This is one of the ways chemistry and astronomy connect, and it is the beginning of the story of how scientists learned that atoms have an inside.

Deep dive (optional) · Deep time

The whole universe in one year

Deep dive (optional): this section is extra. You can skip it and still follow the rest of the unit; come back when you are curious.

The universe is about 13.8 billion years old. That number is too big to feel, so let's squash all of time into one calendar year and find where your birthday lands.

Step 2: Zoom out and watch the whole year

The scale: 13.8 billion years squeezed into 365 days. One cosmic day is about 38 million real years. One cosmic second is about 438 real years.

Data

When does life show up?

You might have heard that "all of life happened in the last hour of the year." The real picture is a little different, and just as surprising:

Life starts in September, but for most of the year it is tiny single cells. Animals arrive in mid-December. Dinosaurs rule from about December 25 to 30. Humans do not show up until roughly the last 12 minutes of December 31, and everything written down in human history, from the first writing to today, fits in the last 12 seconds.

Real world

Where did this idea come from?

Astronomer Carl Sagan popularized the Cosmic Calendar in his 1977 book The Dragons of Eden and in his 1980 TV series Cosmos. It came back in the 2014 series Cosmos: A Spacetime Odyssey, hosted by astrophysicist Neil deGrasse Tyson. Different versions place events at slightly different times because scientists keep refining the dates. Treat the times here as close estimates, not exact clock readings.

Analogy

How long is one cosmic second?

About 438 real years. That means the last cosmic second stretches back to roughly the year 1588: Galileo's lifetime. Every telescope, every airplane, every president and every phone fit inside a single tick of the clock.

By the same scale, your whole life so far (about 12 years) is less than one thirtieth of a second.

How many people have ever lived?

Demographers estimate about 117 billion people have ever been born. About 7 in every 100 of them are alive right now.

Check yourself

Can you explain it?

A classmate says: "My plants grew taller with fertilizer, so fertilizer works!" But they used a bigger pot and a sunnier window for the fertilizer plants. What is wrong with the test, and how would you fix it?

Show a model answer

The test is unfair because fertilizer wasn't the only thing that changed: the pot size and the amount of light also differed, so any of them could explain the taller plants. To fix it, keep pot, soil, water, light, and starting size identical in both groups, change only the fertilizer, include a control group with none, and grow several plants per group so the average is trustworthy.

Tutor notes: hands-on kit, misconceptions, pacing

Kit (about $8): bean seeds, 6 clear cups, potting soil, a metric ruler, a stopwatch, paper clips and string, a small ramp (book + cardboard) and a toy car, graph paper.

  • Concept 1: do the shadow-stick measurement outside at noon (the Earth-size demo works with any two cities and the same angle idea). Discuss why a claim like "the Earth is flat" can be tested.
  • Concept 2: start the bean-light investigation on day one so there is real data to analyze in Concept 5.
  • Concept 3: rewrite 5 weak hypotheses into "If…then…because" form.
  • Concept 4: ramp and car: change only ramp height, measure distance rolled, run 5 trials per height.
  • Concept 5: graph the ramp data; write a CER paragraph.
  • Concept 6: use a cheap diffraction-grating slide or a CD to look at a lamp and a window; compare to the elements on the page. Teaser only: atoms and electrons come later.
  • Concept 7: have students enter their birthday, then write one sentence about what was happening in the universe that day. Mark the cosmic calendar on a hallway strip of paper (1 meter of paper = 1 year is easy to fold).

Common misconceptions:

  • "There is one scientific method with fixed steps." Scientists loop, skip, and repeat; the steps describe common practice.
  • "A hypothesis is an educated guess you must prove right." A hypothesis can be wrong, and learning that is a result.
  • "A theory is just a guess." In science it is a strongly supported explanation.
  • "Experiments are the only kind of science." Observation, modeling, and comparison (e.g. astronomy, geology) are also science.

Pacing: about five 30-minute sessions, with the bean experiment running alongside them. Refer back: in later units, ask students which step of the method each Try it follows.
