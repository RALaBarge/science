Sunlight, Weather, and Staying Safe

What does sunlight do to places, and how do weather forecasts help us stay safe?

Sunlight and weather are part of every day, and we can learn from them. You will see how sunlight warms and lights surfaces, how shade keeps things cooler, how weather repeats in patterns when we keep a record, and how forecasts help us get ready and stay safe.

In this lesson

  1. Sunlight warms and lights surfaces
  2. Shade can keep things cooler
  3. Weather has patterns over time
  4. Forecasts help us get ready and stay safe

Concept 1

Sunlight warms and lights surfaces

Sunlight makes surfaces brighter and often warmer; dark surfaces often warm more.

Analogy

The Sun is like a lamp, but very, very far away.

“Sunlight travels to Earth.”

Try it

A thermometer shows how warm something is.

What you need:

  • black paper
  • white paper
  • a sunny spot and a lamp (an adult sets up the lamp)

Do: Lay the black paper and the white paper in the sun or under a lamp. Use a thermometer, if you have one, or your hands. Which feels warmer?

Safety: An adult handles the lamp. Touch the paper gently, and wait a moment if it feels hot.

“Which feels warmer?”

Data

Play Sun and Weather Sort above. It has you sort what sunlight does. Then make your own smiley chart from your hand test.

  • Black paper: warm, big smile.
  • White paper: cooler, small smile.

Example, not measured numbers.

“Black paper: warm. White paper: cooler.”

Real world

Dark pavement can feel hot, and grass feels cooler. On a hot Michigan summer day, a dark road can feel hot. Grass stays cooler. Never walk on hot pavement in bare feet.

“Dark road gets hot.”

Concept 2

Shade can keep things cooler

A shade blocks sunlight, so it can reduce warming.

Shade is a place where sunlight is blocked. Melt means ice turns into water.

Diagram

Ice cube under shade and ice cube with no shade.

“Shade blocks sunlight.”

Try it

Guess first: Two ice cubes are in the same sunny spot. One gets a shade. Which one melts slower?

What you need:

  • 2 ice cubes of the same size
  • 2 trays
  • a paper card and craft sticks (or a paper cup turned over) for the shade
  • a sunny window or a lamp

Do: Put one ice cube on each tray in the sun. Build a small shade over only one. Wait 10 minutes, then look. Draw how big each cube is now. Do it again if you can.

Look for: The shaded cube is bigger and has fewer drops of water around it.

What it shows: Shade blocks sunlight, so less warming happens. That is why a shady spot feels cooler.

Safety: An adult handles any warm lamp. Wipe up water so no one slips.

“Which ice cube melts slower?”

Data

Example melt after the same time. Shaded ice cube: 5 drops. Uncovered ice cube: 12 drops. Example numbers, not your own.

“Shaded ice: less melt. Sun ice: more melt.”

Real world

A beach umbrella blocks sunlight and makes shade where a person sits.

“A beach umbrella helps.”

Concept 3

Weather has patterns over time

We can record weather over time and see patterns.

Try it

A pattern is something that repeats.

Guess first: What will the weather be like today: sun, cloud, rain, or snow?

What you need:

  • a class calendar
  • weather stickers or crayons (sun, cloud, rain, snow)

Do: Each morning, look out the window together. Say what you see. Put the matching sticker or drawing on today’s square. Do this for many days.

Look for: After a few weeks, count each kind. Which kind came most? Did the weather change when the seasons changed?

What it shows: One day does not show a pattern. Many days written down do.

“Sun, cloud, rain, snow. Mark today.”

Data

A tally is a count with marks. In this 20-day Michigan fall sample:

  • sunny: 8 days
  • cloudy: 6 days
  • rainy: 4 days
  • snowy: 2 days

Total: 20 days.

Example sample, not real observations.

“In this 20-day sample, 8 days were sunny.”

Diagram

Row of four symbols: sunny, cloudy, rainy, snowy.

“We can sort sunny, cloudy, rainy, snowy.”

Real world

In Michigan winters, snow can cover yards, sidewalks, and roads. We wear warm coats, hats, gloves, and boots. We play away from the road, and we stay back when snowplows are working.

“Snow falls in winter. We dress warm and stay safe near roads.”

Concept 4

Forecasts help us get ready and stay safe

Forecasts tell us what weather may come so we can get ready and stay safe.

A forecast is a report about the weather that may come.

Analogy

A forecast is like a helper note about tomorrow. Forecast = the note. Weather coming = what the note tells you. Your coat or boots = what you do because of it.

A note cannot be perfect, because weather can change, so we check the forecast again.

“A forecast helps you choose a coat.”

Try it

Picture-sort “What do we do?” for thunderstorm, tornado, and blizzard. A thunderstorm has lightning and thunder. A tornado is a spinning windstorm from a storm cloud. A blizzard is heavy snow with strong wind.

What you need:

  • picture cards for thunderstorm, tornado, and blizzard
  • a card for “go inside”
  • a card for “go to a basement or an inside room with no windows”
  • a card for “stay off roads”

“Thunderstorm: go inside. Tornado: go to a basement or an inside room with no windows. Blizzard: stay off roads.”

Data

Ask and answer questions about a forecast.

  • What will tomorrow be like?
  • What should we wear?
  • What will we need on the next day?

Today: sunny, 20 °C (68 °F). Tomorrow: rain, 13 °C (55 °F). Next day: snow, -1 °C (30 °F). Example forecast, not a real one.

To change °F to about °C, subtract 30 and cut in half. Freezing is 32 °F, or 0 °C.

Ask a grown-up to print a real forecast for your town, and use it for the questions.

“Forecast says rain. We need raincoats.”

Real world

A Michigan winter storm watch or warning helps families plan.

A watch means a winter storm may come. A warning means a winter storm is coming or is here, so act now.

“A winter storm watch helps us get ready. A winter storm warning means act now.”

Hands-on · Design or investigate

Try it at home or in class

Sun-Safe Beach Helper. Problem: a toy helper at a Michigan beach needs a cool place. Students build a small shade for an ice cube or toy. They compare melting or warmth with an uncovered control. Criteria: the shaded spot stays cooler or melts less than the uncovered control. If sunlight is unavailable, use a lamp.

Try it

What you need

  • black paper
  • white paper
  • a lamp or sunny window
  • 2 ice cubes
  • 2 small trays or cups
  • paper or cardstock for shade
  • craft sticks
  • tape
  • weather calendar stickers or crayons
  • weather picture cards

Safety: An adult handles any warm lamp. Use a sunny window or a lamp only. Clean up water spills right away.

Try it

What you do

1. Black and white paper (10 minutes). Lay both papers in the sun or under a lamp. Guess first: which will feel warmer? Wait a few minutes, then touch each.

2. Shade for an ice cube (15 minutes). Build a shade over one ice cube, leave the other uncovered, and compare the melting. Run it two times.

3. Weather calendar (2 minutes a day). Mark the weather each day for a few weeks. Count the days at the end.

4. Safety sort. Use the picture sort above for thunderstorm, tornado, and blizzard.

Look for:

  • black paper warmer than white
  • shaded ice melting slower
  • which weather came most days

Tutor notes: misconceptions, pacing

Pacing: 5 sessions of 30 minutes.

Common misconceptions to listen for:

  • “Sunlight only makes light, not warmth.” Correct: sunlight can also warm surfaces.
  • “Sunlight makes surfaces colder.” Correct: sunlight can warm surfaces.
  • “Dark surfaces stay cooler than light surfaces in sunlight.” Correct: dark surfaces often warm more.
  • “Shade makes cold.” Correct: shade blocks sunlight and reduces warming; it does not make cold.
  • “Weather has no patterns.” Correct: weather can show patterns over days and weeks.
  • “A forecast makes the weather happen.” Correct: a forecast tells what may happen so we can prepare.
  • “Severe weather means panic.” Correct: we follow a calm safety plan.

Limits and cautions:

  • Weather observations need to repeat over several weeks. If the unit is compressed, the provided 20-day sample is only a partial path (analysis of provided data) and is not enough on its own.
  • The shade part is strongest when students build and test their own shade with a control. A teacher demo alone is only partial.
  • The forecast part needs a real or realistic forecast, such as a printed weather forecast or classroom weather report.
  • Severe weather must be taught calmly: prepare, do not scare.
  • This unit does not cover materials in a light beam or signal devices; those belong in 1-A Sound and Light.
  • Design choice: using shade over an ice cube as a K-safe warming-reduction test
