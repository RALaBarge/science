Earth’s Spheres, Water, and Communities

How do Earth’s spheres interact, where is Earth’s water, and how do communities protect it?

Earth’s air, water, rock and living things all affect one another. You will see the four spheres working together in a rainstorm, find out where Earth’s water is and how little of it is liquid fresh water, and see how communities use science to protect water, land and air.

Concept 1

Spheres interact

A rainstorm is the atmosphere and hydrosphere eroding the geosphere while the biosphere’s roots hold soil.

Earth has four parts that touch each other: the air (atmosphere), water (hydrosphere), rock and soil (geosphere), and living things (biosphere). Each one is called a sphere, which here means one of the four big parts of Earth.

Diagram

Spheres

Find the four Earth spheres in the picture.

  • Atmosphere (air): the rain cloud.
  • Hydrosphere (water): the water running down the bare slope.
  • Geosphere (rock and soil): the soil and rock carried downhill.
  • Biosphere (living things): the grass roots holding the soil.

“Rock, water, air, and life all connect.”

Try it

Stream tray

Sediment is small pieces of rock or soil that water carries.

  1. Pour a layer of sand or soil on a tilted tray.
  2. Pour 1 cup (about 240 milliliters) of water slowly across the top, with no plants.
  3. Repeat with grass along one bank, then with stones. Count the spoonfuls of sediment that move each time. A spoonful is about 1 tablespoon (about 15 milliliters).
  4. Repeat each test at least three times.

“Make a tiny river. Watch it cut a channel.”

Real world

Michigan hill after rain

Soil is the loose dirt on top of the rock. When rain runs down a hill, it can carry soil away with it (this is called erosion). A hill in Michigan gets a spring rain.

“Rain moves soil. Roots help.”

Concept 2

Most Earth water is salty, and much fresh water is frozen

Earth has limited liquid fresh water; most water is salt, and much fresh water is ice.

Groundwater is water that soaks down into the soil and rock.

Try it

Graph the water

100 small squares.

“Color 97 squares salty ocean. Color 2 squares frozen fresh water. Color 1 square for fresh water under the ground. Lakes and rivers are less than one square: draw a tiny dot.”

Color plan for 100 squares: 97 squares blue (salty ocean), 2 squares white with blue edge (frozen fresh water), 1 square light blue (fresh water underground). Draw one tiny dot for all the lakes and rivers: less than one square.

Data

Provided percentages

About 97% salt water, and a little under 3% fresh water (the 97, 2, 1 squares are rounded). Of the fresh water, most is ice and most of the rest is underground; lakes and rivers are only a tiny part.

“Most Earth water is salty. Most fresh water is ice or underground. Lakes and rivers hold very little.”

Concept 3

Communities use science to protect resources

Communities use information and design to protect water, land, and air; science helps choose solutions.

Real world

Case cards

three cards: city rain-garden program, dune-grass restoration, farm fertilizer rules.

“Each card shows a problem, a solution, and how science helped.”

City rain garden

  • Problem: rain runs off roofs and parking lots and picks up dirt.
  • Solution: a garden that soaks the water in.
  • How science helped: tests showed less runoff, and the water looked cleaner.

Dune-grass planting (Michigan example: Sleeping Bear Dunes)

  • Problem: wind and waves carry dune sand away.
  • Solution: plant dune grass to hold the sand.
  • How science helped: scientists learned that grass roots hold sand.

Farm fertilizer rules

  • Problem: extra fertilizer washes into streams and lakes.
  • Solution: rules on when and how much to spread it.
  • How science helped: water tests found where it came from.

Read one card at a time and say the problem, the solution and how science helped.

Try it

Combine information

Evidence means clues, like counts or test results, that show whether something worked. Make a chart with four columns: Problem, Solution, Evidence, Who helped.

“Put the clues together. How did science help?”

  1. Pick one case card and write its Problem, Solution, Evidence and Who helped in a four-column chart.
  2. Example row: Problem - parking lot runoff; Solution - rain garden; Evidence - 10 buckets of runoff left the parking lot, and 6 left the rain garden; Who helped - city workers and scientists.
  3. Do one more case in the next row.
  4. Underline the clue that shows science helped.

Notice: Each solution has evidence behind it, and different people worked on it.

Why it matters: This is how communities decide what to do: they combine clues from science.

Hands-on · Design or investigate

Try it at home or in class

Design and test a model solution to protect a slope or shoreline. Compare two solutions, such as grass, stones, or a foil barrier. Criteria: less sediment moved. Constraints: same amount of water each test, limited materials, about 30 minutes for the tray test (10 to set up, 20 to test). The graph and case cards take about 25 more.

Tutor notes: misconceptions, limits, pacing

Pacing: five 30-minute sessions.

Common misconceptions to listen for:

  • “Earth has plenty of liquid fresh water everywhere.” Correct: most water is salty; much fresh water is frozen.
  • “Most fresh water is in rivers and lakes.” Correct: much fresh water is frozen or underground.
  • “The geosphere, hydrosphere, atmosphere, and biosphere stay separate.” Correct: they interact.
  • “Only nonliving things change Earth’s surface.” Correct: living things, like roots and people, also change the surface.
  • “Community solutions cannot reduce harm.” Correct: solutions can reduce harm, but they cannot eliminate all risk.
  • “One solution, like recycling, fixes everything.” Correct: communities need many solutions, and each has trade-offs.
  • “A rain garden stops all flooding forever.” Correct: it reduces runoff; it does not stop all water problems.

Limits and cautions:

  • Water percentages are rounded to 97, 2, and 1 squares; lakes and rivers are under 1% of fresh water, so draw them as a dot.
  • The sphere model is simplified; real Earth interactions happen over many scales.
  • This unit does not teach the full water cycle; Unit 4 does that.
  • This unit does not teach plate tectonics; Unit 5 does that.
  • Community protection examples require obtaining and combining information; the three case cards guarantee access even if students cannot research independently.
