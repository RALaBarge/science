Matter, Mixtures, and Changes

What is stuff made of, and how can we tell if it changed?

Matter is anything that takes up space and has mass, which means how much stuff it is made of. All matter is made of particles, tiny pieces too small to see, and that idea explains a lot. This unit connects particles to solids, liquids, and gases, uses properties to identify materials, separates mixtures such as salt and sand, and shows that when things change or dissolve the total amount of matter stays the same.

In this lesson

  2. Solids, liquids, and gases act differently

  4. Mixtures can be separated

Concept 1

Matter is made of tiny particles

Matter is anything that takes up space and has mass, which means how much stuff it is made of. All matter is made of particles, tiny pieces too small to see.

Analogy

LEGO bricks from far away

A model is a simple picture or object that stands in for something too small to see. This wall is a model for matter.

“From far away it looks smooth. Up close it is made of pieces.”

Diagram

Particle boxes

Matter comes in three forms you can touch. A solid keeps its own shape. A liquid flows and takes the shape of its cup. A gas spreads out to fill the space around it, like the air in a room.

“Particles in a solid are close. Particles in a liquid can slide. Particles in a gas spread out.”

Try it

Air takes space

“Blow air into a bag. Seal it. Press it. The air takes space.”

Predict first: Will the sealed bag feel empty or full? Can you squash it flat?

You need:

  • A zip bag.
  1. Open the bag and sweep it through the air. Zip it nearly closed.
  2. Blow air in and seal it quickly.
  3. Press on it with your hands. Try to squash it flat.

What you should see: The bag stays puffy. You can squeeze it a little but not flat, because air takes up space.

Why it matters: Air is a gas, and gases are matter too even though we cannot see them.

Data

Air has mass

“The inflated balloon tips down a little. If it does not, try a pumped-up ball and a flat ball on a kitchen scale. Air is matter.”

Concept 2

Solids, liquids, and gases act differently

The state of matter depends on how particles move and stay together.

Diagram

Three cups

Water can be ice, liquid water, or water vapor, which is a gas you cannot see.

“Ice keeps its shape. Water takes the cup’s shape. Water vapor, an invisible gas, spreads out. The white cloud over a kettle is tiny droplets.”

Try it

Safe warming

“Warm ice melts. It is still water.”

You need:

  • An ice cube.
  • A clear cup.
  • A tray or plate.
  • A small cup (about 1/4 cup, or 60 mL) of warm water. Warm to the touch, not hot. An adult pours it.
  1. Put the ice cube in the clear cup and set it on the tray.
  2. Watch for 5 minutes. Write down what you see.
  3. An adult adds a small splash of warm water.
  4. Watch again. Write down what you see now.

What you should see: The ice gets smaller and turns into water.

Why it matters: Melting changes the form of water, but it is still water.

Real world

Michigan snow

“Snow is solid water. When it warms, it becomes liquid water.”

Concept 3

Properties help us identify materials

We can identify materials by their properties: how hard or soft they are, whether they are magnetic, whether they dissolve in water, and how they feel (texture).

Try it

Property sort

“Sort by hard or soft, magnetic or not magnetic, and sinks or floats.”

Predict first: Which of the four things will be magnetic? Which will float?

You need:

  • A steel paper clip (aluminum ones are not magnetic).
  • An eraser.
  • A wood chip.
  • A plastic bead.
  • A magnet.
  • A bowl of water.
  1. Hold the magnet to each object. Is it magnetic? Write yes or no.
  2. Drop each object in water. Does it sink or float?
  3. Press each object. Hard or soft?

What you should see: The steel paper clip is magnetic and sinks. The wood chip usually floats. The eraser is soft. Test each one yourself. Results can differ: some plastic beads float, and some erasers float.

Why it matters: Properties are clues that tell materials apart.

Recycling

Concept 4

Mixtures can be separated

A mixture keeps the properties of its parts, so we can often separate it.

Analogy

Trail mix

A bowl of trail mix: raisins, peanuts, and cereal.

“You can pick trail mix apart. The pieces do not become one new thing.”

Try it

Sand and salt

“Add water. Salt dissolves. Sand does not. Filter the sand.”

You need:

  • A clear cup and a second cup.
  • A spoon.
  • About 1 spoonful each of sand and salt. Sand from a Great Lakes beach or a sandbox works well.
  • Water, about 1/2 cup (120 mL).
  • A coffee filter.
  1. Put the sand and salt in the cup and add the water.
  2. Stir for 30 seconds. Look: the salt disappears, and the sand does not.
  3. Set the coffee filter over the second cup. Pour the mix slowly through it.
  4. Look at the sand on the filter and the water in the cup.

What you should see: Sand on the filter, clear water in the cup.

Why it matters: Salt is still in the water even though you cannot see it.

Concept 5

Changes can be physical or chemical, but no matter is lost

A physical change changes the form or shape of something, but it is still the same stuff (ice melting into water). A chemical change makes new stuff (a bag that fizzes and makes a new gas).Heating, cooling, mixing, and dissolving may change appearance, but total matter stays the same. Some changes make new substances.

Diagram

Physical vs. chemical

“Melting is still water. Fizzing can make a new gas.”

Try it

Sealed bag reaction

“Mix inside the bag. The bag puffs with gas. If sealed, nothing escapes.”

Predict first: What will happen when the vinegar and baking soda meet? Will the bag weigh more, less, or the same?

You need:

  • A zip bag.
  • 2 teaspoons (about 10 mL) baking soda.
  • A small cup with 1/4 cup (about 60 mL) vinegar.
  • A kitchen scale.
  • Safety glasses.
  1. Put the baking soda in the bag. Stand the cup of vinegar upright inside without spilling it. Zip the bag.
  2. Weigh the whole bag and write the number.
  3. Tip the cup over to mix inside the bag. Watch.
  4. Weigh the sealed bag again.

What you should see: The bag puffs up and the number on the scale stays the same (or nearly).

Why it matters: Matter is not lost in a change. The gas that formed is still in the bag.

Data

“The gas has mass. The total stays the same.”

Rust

“Iron changes into rust. It is a new substance.”

Real world

Bread dough and yogurt

“Yeast is a tiny living thing. It eats sugar in dough and makes carbon dioxide gas, so the dough puffs up. Other tiny living things change milk into thick, sour yogurt. Both make new substances, so both are chemical changes.”

Hands-on · Design or investigate

Try it at home or in class

Design a method to separate a mixture of sand, salt, and small iron filings. Use a magnet for iron, water to dissolve salt, filtering for sand, then let the salty water dry on a dark plate so the salt comes back. Record steps and evidence.

Criteria

  • Separate all three materials.

Constraints

  • One cup.
  • One filter.
  • One magnet.
  • Limited water (for example, 1/2 cup, or 120 mL).
Try it

What you need

You need:

  • Zip bags.
  • Cups and spoons.
  • Salt and sand.
  • Coffee filters.
  • Baking soda and vinegar.
  • Two balloons.
  • A straw or hanger for a balance.
  • A small magnet (and iron filings for the hands-on).
  • A kitchen scale and safety glasses.
  • An ice cube and a tray.
  • Paper towels.

Safety:

  • Wash hands after sand and salt work.
  • An adult handles warm water.
  • Keep magnets away from Chromebooks.
  • Keep iron filings in a closed bag and hands away from eyes.
  • Use 2 teaspoons (about 10 mL) baking soda and 1/4 cup (about 60 mL) vinegar so the sealed bag puffs without popping.
  • Seal it over a tray and stand back while it fizzes. Do not hold the bag.
  • An open bag lets gas escape, so its mass drops. That is a leak, not proof that gas weighs nothing.
Try it

What you do

In this kit you will:

  • Mix and separate sand and salt.
  • Watch salt dissolve.
  • Test that air takes up space.
  • Do the sealed baking soda and vinegar bag and compare the weight before and after.

You did this before, and what is new:

  • You sorted materials in 2-A. In 5.1 you use property tests to identify unknowns and record a data table.
  • You used fair-test habits in Unit 0. Here you use repeated measurements and graphs to show matter is conserved.
  1. Blow up a zip bag and press it. Hang two balloons on a stick to see whether air has mass (10 minutes).
  2. Test four objects for magnetic, sink or float, hard or soft (10 minutes).
  3. Stir sand and salt in water, filter out the sand, then let the water dry on a dark plate (20 minutes plus drying).
  4. Weigh a sealed bag before and after the vinegar and baking soda react (10 minutes).

Notice: The same matter is there after a change. Salt returns after the water dries. The sealed bag weighs the same.

Why it matters: These tests show matter is made of particles and is not lost when it changes.

Words to know

matter
anything that takes up space and has mass.
mass
how much stuff something is made of. A scale measures it in grams (g).
particle
a tiny piece of matter.
solid
matter with a fixed shape.
liquid
matter that flows and takes the shape of its container.
gas
matter that spreads out to fill space.
mixture
two or more substances mixed but not chemically joined.
property

dissolve
when a substance breaks into tiny pieces in a liquid.

model
a simple picture or object that stands in for something too small or too big to see.
evaporate

the form changes, but it is still the same stuff.
chemical change
new stuff forms.
Tutor notes: misconceptions, limits, pacing

Pacing: five 30-minute sessions.

Common misconceptions to listen for:

  • “Air is nothing.” Correct: air is matter; it has mass and takes space.
  • “Only things you can see are matter.” Correct: matter includes things you cannot see, like air.
  • “Dissolved stuff disappears.” Correct: it is still there as tiny particles.
  • “Dissolved stuff becomes air or water.” Correct: it remains as the same substance, mixed in tiny pieces.
  • “Dissolving makes a new substance.” Correct: dissolving is a mixture; the substances stay themselves.
  • “Particles get bigger when heated.” Correct: particles move more; the substance may expand.
  • “Melting makes a new substance.” Correct: melting is a physical change; it is still the same substance.
  • “Gas has no weight.” Correct: gas has mass.

Limits and cautions:

  • Without a good balance, conservation of mass is harder to show. A homemade hanger balance helps but is less precise. The best way is weighing and graphing your own measurements; the sample data is only for practice graphing given numbers.
  • Chemical reactions are simplified: students observe signs of change but do not identify all products.
  • This unit does not cover temperature scales or detailed heat transfer; those belong in later energy units.
  • If magnets are used, keep them away from Chromebooks and cards.

What students do in this unit: You build a model of particles, measure mass before and after changes, identify materials by their properties, and find out when mixing or changing makes new stuff.
