Sound and Light

How do vibrations make sound, and how does light help us see and send messages?

You did this before / what is new. this is new in grade 1 (you may have compared pushes in K-A). Students collect evidence that sound can shake objects, explain seeing with illumination, and build/test a coded signal.

Concept 1

Vibrations make sound; sound makes things vibrate

When materials shake they make sound, and sound can shake materials.

Try it

Pluck a rubber band stretched over a cup. Tap a drum or container covered with plastic wrap while rice sits on it.

“Pluck the band. Watch the rice dance.”

Diagram

A ruler shaking over the edge of a table, with a child’s ear nearby.

“The ruler shakes. Your ear hears.”

Real world

A child hums and touches their throat.

“Feel the buzz when you hum.”

Data

Observation log with columns: “Did it shake?” and “Did it make sound?”

“Rubber band: shake yes. Sound yes.”

Concept 2

We see only what is lit

Objects can be seen only when light shines on them, or when they make their own light, like a lamp or the Sun.

Try it

Look inside a mystery box with a peephole. First the light port is closed. Then open a flashlight port.

“No light: I cannot see it. Light on: I see it.”

Diagram

Flashlight, object, and eye. Arrows show light going to the object and into the eye.

“Light bounces into your eye.”

Data

Class tally: “Seen in dark?” and “Seen with light?”

“With light: yes. Without light: no.”

Real world

A dark bedroom becomes easier to see when a lamp is turned on.

“A lamp helps you find your toy.”

Concept 3

Materials in a light beam

Light passes through, is blocked, or bounces depending on the material.

Try it

Shine a flashlight through clear plastic, wax paper, and cardboard.

“Clear: light goes through. Cardboard: light stops.”

Data

Results table with material names and what happened.

“Plastic: through. Wax paper: blurry. Cardboard: blocked.”

Real world

A window lets light into a room; a curtain makes the room darker.

“Window: bright. Curtain: darker.”

Diagram

A flashlight beam hitting foil and bouncing away.

“Foil makes light bounce.”

Concept 4

Sending messages with light or sound

A pattern of flashes or beats can carry a message across a room.

Analogy

A flashlight blink is like a knock on a door.

“A pattern can say something.”

Try it

Teams make a signal code. Example: flash-flash-pause means “hello.” Tap-tap-stop means “stop.” A partner decodes it from across the room.

“My partner watches or listens.”

Data

Signal test chart: message sent, message decoded.

“My partner got 3 of 4 messages.”

Real world

A lighthouse flashes a pattern to help boats know where they are.

“A lighthouse flashes to help boats.”

Hands-on · Design or investigate

Try it at home or in class

Signal Device Challenge. Problem: two people need to send a message across the room without talking. Students choose light or sound, design a signal code, build a device or signal system, and test it with a partner. Criteria: the partner can decode at least one message. Constraints: use only kit materials and no spoken words. This earns 1-PS4-4 when students build and test.

Try it

What you need

rubber bands, paper cup or small container, plastic wrap, a few grains of rice, shoebox or small box for mystery box, flashlight or lamp, clear plastic, wax paper, cardboard, foil, paper, crayons.

Safety: Use ready-made flashlights or LED lamps only; do not open battery compartments or handle loose button batteries. Never shine a light into eyes. Small rice grains are not food in science class.

Try it

What you do

Rotate through four stations: vibrating sound makers, mystery box, light-beam material test, and signal-device design.

No time or no good weather? Use this data

This data is a backup. It lets you practice reading evidence, but the standard expects you to collect your own when you can.

- Real observation log: Students draw or mark what they observe: shaking yes/no, sound yes/no, seen yes/no, material lets light through yes/some/no, signal decoded yes/no. - Provided dataset: Picture cards and a simple table for backup: rubber band shaking and making sound; drum skin with rice jumping; child touching humming throat; mystery box dark vs. lit; clear plastic lets light through; wax paper lets some light through; cardboard blocks light. Use the provided dataset if materials fail or time is short.

Near you: Michigan has many lighthouses on the Great Lakes. Their flashing lights send patterns that help boats know where dangerous shores are. Child text: “A Michigan lighthouse flashes a pattern.”

Tutor notes: misconceptions, standards, pacing

Pacing: 5 sessions of 30 minutes.

Common misconceptions to listen for:

  • “Sound is a thing flying through the air.” Correct: sound is a pattern of vibrations moving through material.
  • “Sound can happen without anything shaking.” Correct: sound comes from vibrations.
  • “We can see in total darkness.” Correct: we need light to see.
  • “Our eyes shoot light to see.” Correct: we see when light enters our eyes.
  • “All materials let light pass.” Correct: some materials let light pass, some block it, and some bounce it.
  • “Light disappears forever when blocked.” Correct: blocked light does not keep going through; it may be stopped, absorbed, or reflected.
  • “A signal can work without a pattern.” Correct: a pattern helps the receiver decode the message.
  • “Signals must use words.” Correct: patterns of light or sound can carry messages.

Limits and cautions:

  • Sound making rice jump may need a loud voice, drum, or speaker; use provided data if the effect is weak.
  • The mystery box must be dark enough; test before class.
  • Flashlights need safe storage and battery checks.
  • This unit does not teach wave diagrams, sound speed, or light as electromagnetic radiation. Those belong in later grades.
  • Simplification: “light bounces into your eye” is a grade-1 version of reflection and vision.

Michigan Science Standards this unit earns:

  • 1-PS4-1: Plan and conduct investigations to provide evidence that vibrating materials can make sound and that sound can make materials vibrate. Student evidence: rubber-band, drum-skin, and rice observations.
  • 1-PS4-2: Make observations to construct an evidence-based account that objects can be seen only when illuminated. Student evidence: mystery-box observation with light off/on.
  • 1-PS4-3: Plan and conduct an investigation to determine the effect of placing different materials in the path of a light beam. Student evidence: flashlight test of clear plastic, wax paper, and cardboard with a results table.
  • 1-PS4-4: Design and build a device that uses light or sound to solve the problem of communicating over a distance. Student evidence: build and test a flashlight or drum signal that a partner decodes.

Connections: - Builds on: K2.0 Science Noticing and Asking *(optional)*, K-A Pushes and Pulls for fair-test habits, K-B Sunlight, Weather, and Staying Safe for sunlight observations. - Builds toward: Unit 1: Light & Matter, Unit 3: Forces, Motion & Energy, and later grades 6–8 work with waves, energy transfer, and signals.
