Earth’s Surface: Rocks, Maps, Erosion, Resources

How do rocks, water, maps, and resources help us explain and protect Earth’s surface?

Earth’s surface is shaped by materials that break down, move and settle. You will separate weathering from erosion, read rock layers and fossils as evidence of change, find patterns on maps, see how fuels and energy sources are natural resources that affect land, air and water, and consider designs that reduce harm.

Concept 1

Earth materials break and move

Rocks and soil have properties, weathering breaks rocks into pieces, and erosion moves those pieces.

Try it

Soil sort

“Look closely. Find bits of rock, sand, and plant matter.”

Predict first: What do you think is in a handful of soil?

You need:

  • a small scoop of soil from a Michigan yard or a Lake Michigan beach (ask a grown-up first)
  • a magnifier
  • white paper
  1. Spread the soil on the paper.
  2. Use the magnifier to find: bits of rock, sand, and plant bits.
  3. Sort them into three piles. Draw what you see.

Notice: Soil is a mix of tiny rock bits and once-living plant matter.

Why it matters: Soil comes from rock that broke into small pieces and plants that decayed.

Try it

Shake and water tray

“Shake sugar cubes. Edges break. Pour water. Watch sand move.”

Predict first: What will happen to sugar cubes after a lot of shaking?

You need:

  • sugar cubes or chalk
  • a lid-tight jar
  • sand in a tray
  • a cup of water (about 240 milliliters; four cups are about one liter)
  1. Shake 3 sugar cubes in a jar for 30 seconds. Look at the pieces.
  2. Pour a cup of water (about 240 milliliters; four cups are about one liter) slowly across the sand tray tilted a little.
  3. Watch where the sand goes.

Notice: Shaking rounds the corners and makes small pieces (weathering). Water carries sand downhill (erosion).

Why it matters: Rocks break down and move like this too, only much more slowly.

Concept 2

Rock layers and fossils show landscape change

Layers and fossils are evidence that Earth’s surface and living things changed over time.

Sandstone is rock made of sand. Shale is rock made of mud.

Diagram

Rock layers

A fossil is what is left of a plant or animal that lived long ago, preserved in rock.

“Older layers are usually deeper.”

Try it

Make layers

“Add layers. Each layer is a new event.”

Predict first: If you pour sand, then pebbles, then soil, which layer will be on the bottom?

You need:

  • a clear cup
  • sand
  • small pebbles
  • soil
  • a teaspoon
  1. Add one teaspoon (about 5 milliliters) of sand. Level it.
  2. Add pebbles on top, then soil.
  3. Look from the side. Which went in first? Which is oldest?

What you should see: The first layer is on the bottom, the last on top. Each layer is a separate event.

Why it matters: This is how scientists read old landscapes in a cliff.

Real world

Petoskey stone

“A Petoskey stone is a fossil. It shows ancient seas in Michigan.”

Data

Patterns question

A quarry is a pit where workers dig out rock.

“Shell fossils were found in a Michigan quarry. What do they tell us about this place long ago? What other clues would you want?”

Think about it: Sea shells are made by animals that live in water. If you find shells in a Michigan quarry, the place was once under water. More clues to look for:

  • ripple marks in the rock
  • fish bones
  • how deep the shell layer is

Concept 3

Maps show patterns of Earth’s features

Maps let us see patterns, such as mountain ranges, broad plains, and connected Great Lakes.

Diagram

US relief map

A relief map uses color and shading to show how high or low the land is. Maps show height with color bands, like the rings on a topographic map.

“Look at the colors. What shapes do the mountains make? What shapes do the plains make?”

Concept 4

Energy and fuels come from natural resources

Fuels and energy sources such as natural gas, wind, coal, and sunlight are natural resources, and using them affects land, air, and water.

Real world

Michigan energy cards

A fuel is something we burn to make energy. A natural resource is something from nature that people use.

“Some energy comes from gas, wind, sun, or coal.”

Concept 5

Designs can reduce harm from natural Earth processes

Barriers, drains, plants, and rules can reduce damage from erosion, floods, and storms, but solutions have trade-offs.

Sediment is the sand, soil, and tiny rock bits that water moves.

Analogy

Helmet

“A helmet does not stop falls. It reduces harm.”

What matches what: the helmet is the design (a wall, grass, a breakwall). A fall is the hazard (a flood, an eroding shore). The design does not stop the hazard. It makes the harm smaller.

Try it

Barrier test

soil slope, water, cardboard or foil barriers, grass or pebbles.

“Test barriers. Which one keeps soil in place?”

You did this before / what is new: You tested erosion guards in 2-C and a flood barrier in 3.2; now the new part is comparing at least two designs, like grass and a wall, with the same amount of water each time and a real observation log. New measurement: spoonfuls (a teaspoon is about 5 milliliters) of moved sediment. One messy trial is not the finding: run each design at least three times the same way and compare before you claim.

Real world

Great Lakes flooding

Hands-on · Design or investigate

Try it at home or in class

Design a model shoreline or slope barrier that reduces erosion. Students generate and compare at least two solutions, such as grass and a wall. They test with the same amount of water each time and compare sediment loss. Claim frame: “I claim ___ reduces harm from ___ because our data show ___.”

Try it

What you need

  • cups
  • sand, soil, and pebbles
  • a shallow tray
  • a straw
  • a magnifier
  • sugar cubes or chalk
  • water
  • cardboard
  • foil
  • tape
  • craft sticks
  • paper
  • a pencil
  • provided map cards
  • provided energy-resource cards

Safety: Wash hands after soil and sand trays. Wipe water spills. Keep soil away from Chromebooks.

Try it

What you do

  1. Sort soil and shake sugar cubes (10 minutes).
  2. Pour water over a slope with no barrier. Count spoonfuls of sediment that moved (10 minutes).
  3. Repeat with grass, then with a wall. Same water each time (20 minutes).
  4. Write your claim: “I claim ___ reduces harm because our data show ___.”

Notice: The barrier that moves the fewest spoonfuls helped most. Repeat each design at least three times.

Why it matters: Engineers compare designs with the same test before they build.

Tutor notes: misconceptions, limits, pacing

Pacing: six 30-minute sessions.

Common misconceptions to listen for:

  • “Rocks never change.” Correct: rocks change slowly by weathering, erosion, and other processes.
  • “Soil is just dirt.” Correct: soil contains minerals, water, air, and living things.
  • “Erosion and weathering are the same.” Correct: weathering breaks; erosion moves.
  • “Fossils are fresh bones.” Correct: fossils are preserved traces or mineralized remains.
  • “Landscapes have always been the same.” Correct: layers and fossils show change over time.
  • “Maps are just pictures.” Correct: maps are data that show patterns.
  • “Using resources has no effect.” Correct: resource use affects land, air, and water.
  • “Most Earth water is liquid fresh water.” Correct: most Earth water is salty; much fresh water is frozen.

Limits and cautions:

  • This unit does not teach plate tectonics or the full rock cycle; Unit 5 handles that later.
  • Map work is simplified; students do not need coordinate systems.
  • Resource formation and mining details are simplified; the focus is on location, use, and effects.
  • Erosion measurements are approximate; the provided dataset guarantees evidence if measurements are messy.
  • Climate change is not taught here; Unit 13 and later middle-school units handle it.
