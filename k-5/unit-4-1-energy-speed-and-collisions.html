Energy, Speed, and Collisions

How can we use speed, collisions, heat, light, and sound as evidence that energy moves?

Think about a sled going down a hill in Michigan in winter. Speed is how fast something moves. Motion energy is the energy something has because it moves.

Energy can be moved from place to place, and we can find evidence of it. You will connect speed to energy, watch what collisions do, follow heat as it moves from warmer to cooler things, and see how sound and light can carry energy too.

Concept 2

Collisions change energy

A collision is when objects hit each other. Heat is energy that moves from warmer things to cooler things.

When objects collide, motion energy can move, so things can speed up, slow down, or change shape.

Try it

Marble target

a marble rolling into a small block target.

“Roll the marble. Watch the target move. Energy moved.”

A prediction is your best guess before you test.

Predict first: If the marble rolls faster, will the block move farther or less far?

You need:

  • a marble
  • a small block or box
  • a ruler
  • a ramp or book
  1. Set a ramp. Place the block at the bottom.
  2. Roll the marble from a low start. Measure how far the block slides.
  3. Roll from a higher start. Measure again.
  4. Do each three times.

What you should see: The higher start makes the marble faster, so the block moves farther. Your numbers will differ.

Why it matters: Motion energy moves from the marble to the block.

Concept 3

Heat moves from warmer to cooler

Heat is energy that moves from warmer things to cooler things.

Heat energy transfers from warmer objects to cooler objects until temperatures become more equal.

Diagram

Heat moves, and a coat slows it

Heat moves along a metal spoon from warm cocoa toward the cooler handle. A coat works the same way in reverse: it slows the heat leaving your body.

Heat always moves from warmer to cooler. A coat does not add cold or heat: it slows the heat leaving your body.

A coat does not make heat; it slows the heat leaving.

Analogy

Hot cocoa and spoon

a spoon in a cup of warm cocoa.

“The spoon gets warm. Heat moved from cocoa to spoon.”

Try it

Cup warming

hands wrapped around a warm cup. Adult handles warm water.

“Your hands warm up because heat moves into them.”

Predict first: Will your hands feel warm? Where does the warmth come from?

You need:

  • a mug
  • warm (not hot) water; an adult pours it
  1. Feel the empty mug first.
  2. Have an adult fill it with warm water.
  3. Wrap both hands around it for one minute.

Notice: Your hands feel warm and the water cools a little. Heat moved from the warm water, through the mug, into your hands.

Why it matters: Heat always moves from the warmer thing to the cooler thing.

Real world

Michigan winter coat

In a Michigan winter, a warm coat, hat, and mittens help. The coat does not make heat. It slows the heat leaving your body, so you stay warm longer outside.

“A coat slows heat leaving your body. It does not make heat.”

Concept 4

Sound and light can move energy

A vibration is a quick back-and-forth shake. Sound is made of vibrations. Light is energy we can see.

Sound vibrations and light can also transfer energy to other things.

Try it

Sound moves rice

rice on plastic wrap over a cup or near a speaker; the rice jumps when sound is made.

“Make a sound. Watch rice jump. Sound moved energy.”

A prediction is your best guess before you test.

Predict first: Will rice jump when you make a loud sound near the wrap?

You need:

  • a bowl
  • plastic wrap
  • a rubber band
  • a pinch of rice
  • a pot and spoon
  1. Stretch plastic wrap tight over the bowl. Put the rubber band on.
  2. Sprinkle rice on top.
  3. Hold a pot near the bowl and bang it with a spoon.

Notice: The rice dances. Sound is moving air, and it shakes the plastic and the rice.

Why it matters: Sound carries energy from one place to another.

Try it

Light warms a hand

a hand held near a lamp, not touching it. Adult handles the warm lamp.

“Hold your hand near the lamp. It feels warm. Light moved energy.”

A prediction is your best guess before you test.

Predict first: Will your hand feel warm near a lamp, without touching it?

You need:

  • a desk lamp with a regular bulb
  • an adult to switch it on
  1. Hold your palm 20 cm (about 8 inches) below the lamp for 10 seconds.
  2. Move it away and compare.
  3. Never touch the bulb; it gets hot.

Notice: Your hand warms up in the light. Light moved energy to your hand.

Why it matters: This is how the Sun warms the Earth.

Hands-on · Design or investigate

Try it at home or in class

A fair test changes only one variable, the one thing you choose to change, and keeps everything else the same.

Fair-test task: “Which collision makes the target move farthest?” Students choose one variable to change, such as marble speed or marble mass. They keep the target the same, predict outcomes, test three times, record results in a table, and write one evidence sentence.

What you need

  • toy car or marble
  • cardboard ramp
  • books
  • small block or target
  • paper cups
  • spoon
  • warm water
  • thermometer (if available)
  • flashlight or lamp

Safety: An adult handles warm water and warm lamps. Do not stare into bright lights. Keep water away from Chromebooks.

Try it

What you do

Test ramp height and block movement. Run marble collisions and predict outcomes. Observe heat transfer with a warm cup. Observe sound moving rice and light warming a hand. Record real observations.

  1. Let a toy car roll down a low ramp, then a high one. Measure how far it pushes a block each time (15 minutes).
  2. Roll a marble into a block. Predict first, then test three times (15 minutes).
  3. Warm your hands on a warm cup. Bang a pot near rice. Hold a hand under a lamp (15 minutes).
  4. Write one evidence sentence, such as: "The higher ramp made the car faster, and the block moved farther."

Notice: Faster things carry more energy. Energy can move from one thing to another.

Why it matters: Energy is easier to track when you measure what it moves.

Tutor notes: misconceptions, limits, pacing

Pacing: five 30-minute sessions.

Common misconceptions to listen for:

  • “Faster objects have less energy.” Correct: faster objects usually have more energy.
  • “Energy disappears in a collision.” Correct: energy can transfer and change motion.
  • “Cold flows into things.” Correct: heat transfers from warmer to cooler.
  • “A coat makes heat.” Correct: a coat slows heat leaving your body.
  • “Sound cannot move things.” Correct: sound vibrations can move small objects.
  • “Still things have no energy.” Correct: energy can be present or transferring even when the evidence is subtle.

Limits and cautions:

  • Teacher note: this unit covers part of the energy-transfer standard. Electric current is taught in Unit 4.2.
  • This unit uses speed evidence only. It does not use energy formulas.
  • Sound-moving-rice can be subtle; the provided dataset guarantees evidence if equipment fails.
  • Temperature measurement is optional; the key idea is direction of heat transfer, not exact scales.
