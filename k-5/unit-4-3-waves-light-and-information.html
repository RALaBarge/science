Waves, Light, and Information

How do waves, light, and patterns carry energy and information?

Waves are patterns that repeat, like a bump that keeps coming back along a rope or a ripple on a lake. Waves can carry energy (the power to move things) and information (a message that tells us something). You will learn the parts of a wave, how light bouncing off things into your eyes lets us see, and how patterns of light or sound can send a message. You will also compare two ways of sending a message to see which one works better.

Concept 1

Wave patterns have amplitude and wavelength

Have you ever shaken a rope or dropped a pebble in a pond? You made a wave. Waves on the Great Lakes, such as Lake Michigan, are real waves you can see from the beach. Watch a rubber duck on a lake: the waves make it bob, but the duck mostly stays in about the same place.

Waves make repeating patterns, and those patterns can carry energy that moves small objects.

Diagram

Wave parts

Look at the wave picture. Amplitude is how tall the wave is. Wavelength is the length of one full wave, from crest to crest. Tall waves have big amplitude. Close repeats have short wavelength.

Try it

Rope or rubber-band waves

“Make waves in a rope. Some waves are tall. Some waves are close together.”

What you need:

  • A rope about 2 meters (about 6 feet) long, or a long rubber band

What you do:

  1. Hold one end of the rope.
  2. Shake it gently 5 times and watch the waves.
  3. Shake it hard 5 times and watch the waves.
  4. Shake it fast, then slowly, and watch how close together the waves are.

What you should see: Gentle shakes make small, low waves. Hard shakes make tall waves, which have big amplitude (wave height). Fast shakes make waves that are close together, which have short wavelength (a short distance from one wave to the next).

Why it matters: Changing how you shake changes the wave’s amplitude and wavelength.

Try it

Waves move objects

“A wave can move a cork. Energy travels with the wave.”

What you need:

  • A bowl of water
  • A small cork or bit of paper
  • A spoon or your finger
  • A towel

What you do:

  1. Float the cork in the bowl.
  2. Tap the water once at one edge with your finger or the spoon.
  3. Watch the cork as the ripple reaches it.

What you should see: The cork bobs up and down and stays close to the same spot, while the ripple moves outward past it.

Why it matters: Energy travels with the wave, so a wave can move an object even though the wave itself keeps going.

Safety: Wipe water spills. Keep water away from Chromebooks.

Concept 2

Light, reflection, and seeing

Light bounces off things. This bouncing is called reflecting. When light that bounced off an object gets into your eyes, you can see the object.

Diagram

Eye and object

Look at the picture. Light from the lamp hits the apple. Some of that light bounces off the apple and goes into your eye. The apple does not make its own light, and no light comes out of your eye.

“Light hits the apple. Some light bounces to your eye.”

Try it

Mirror bounce

A mirror is a flat, shiny surface that bounces light back.

“Aim the mirror. The light changes direction.”

Predict first: If you aim the flashlight at the mirror from the left, where will the spot on the wall appear?

What you need:

  • A flashlight
  • A small mirror, or foil on cardboard
  • A wall
  • A dim room

What you do:

  1. Aim the flashlight at the mirror at a low angle.
  2. Find the bright spot on the wall.
  3. Tilt the mirror a little. Watch the spot move.

What you should see: The spot jumps to a new place as you tilt the mirror. Light bounces off in a new direction.

Why it matters: Mirrors and shiny things bounce light. That is part of how we see.

Try it

Draw the light path

Use a blank sheet of paper.

What you do:

  1. Draw a lamp on the left.
  2. Draw an apple in the middle.
  3. Draw an eye on the right.
  4. Draw an arrow from the lamp to the apple.
  5. Draw an arrow from the apple to the eye.
  6. Do not draw any arrow leaving the eye.

“Draw the path. The arrow into your eye is how you see.”

Real world

Moon

Look up at the Moon on a clear night. It looks bright, but it does not make its own light. It reflects sunlight, so we can see it. You can see the Moon from your yard or a Michigan beach on a clear night.

“The Moon does not make its own light. It reflects sunlight.”

Concept 3

Patterns can carry information

A signal is a message sent by light, sound (the energy you hear), or another code.

A pattern of light or sound can carry a message, and we can compare which signal works better.

Try it

Signal code

“Short flash, long flash. A pattern can mean something.”

Predict first: Can a partner tell short and long flashes apart across the room?

What you need:

  • A flashlight
  • A dim room
  • Paper and pencil

What you do:

  1. Make a code card:
    • Short flash = A
    • Long flash = B
    • Two short flashes = C
  2. Send one letter at a time. Your partner writes what they see.
  3. Switch and try a three-letter word.

What you should see: You may miss some. Slow down and make short and long very different.

Why it matters: This is how real signals, like Morse code, carry information with patterns.

Real world

Traffic light

“Color patterns tell drivers what to do.”

Michigan traffic lights use this same red, yellow, and green pattern.

Try it

Compare two signals

“Send the same message two ways. Which one is clearer?”

Predict first: Which will your partner get right more often, flashlight or drum?

What you do:

  1. Pick a short message, such as “GO”.
  2. Send it 5 times with the flashlight from 5 meters (about 16 feet, about as long as a minivan). Count the correct ones.
  3. Send it 5 times with a drum or tap. Count again.
  4. Use the same distance and the same message for both.

What you should see: Sample from the page: flashlight 4 of 5, drum 3 of 5.

Why it matters: Comparing two designs with the same test shows which one works better.

Hands-on

Try it at home or in class

Design and compare two signals: a flashlight code and a drum or tap code. Teams send the same short message across the room. Criteria: the partner decodes the message correctly at 5 meters (about 16 feet). Constraints: no shouting, one pattern key, same distance for both systems. Students record accuracy and choose the better solution with evidence.

Try it

What you need

  • A rope or rubber band
  • A shallow tray or bowl, and water
  • A small cork or bits of paper
  • A flashlight
  • A mirror or shiny foil
  • Paper, pencil, and tape
  • A cup or small drum, and a spoon

Safety: Do not shine lights into eyes. Wipe water spills. Keep water away from Chromebooks.

Try it

What you do

Make rope waves and water waves. Watch waves move objects. Draw how light bounces off an object into an eye. Design and compare two signals for accuracy.

  1. Shake a rope or slinky to make tall and short waves (10 minutes).
  2. Float a cork in a bowl and tap the water (5 minutes).
  3. Draw the light path from lamp to object to eye (10 minutes).
  4. Make a flashlight code and a drum code. Send the same message 5 times with each from 5 meters (about 16 feet). Count the correct ones (20 minutes).

Notice: Waves carry energy and move things. Patterns can carry information.

Why it matters: Choose the better design by comparing counts, not by guessing.

Electric signals on a wire. A wire with steady electricity flowing in it does not send out a wave. When you switch the electricity on and off, the magnetic field around the wire changes, and those changes can spread out as a wave. Telegraphs sent Morse code by switching electricity on and off in a wire.

Tutor notes: misconceptions, pacing

Pacing: five 30-minute sessions.

Common misconceptions to listen for:

  • “Waves are only water.” Correct: waves can be in water, in a rope, or in air (sound). Light also moves in a wave pattern, but it does not push a cork the way a water wave does.
  • “Light comes out of our eyes.” Correct: light enters our eyes.
  • “Objects we see make their own light.” Correct: many objects reflect light from another source.
  • “Sound only travels through air.” Correct: sound can travel through solids, liquids, and gases.
  • “Sound can travel through empty space.” Correct: sound needs matter.
  • “Patterns are only decorations.” Correct: patterns can carry information.
  • “Light cannot carry messages.” Correct: light patterns can carry messages.
  • “Waves cannot move objects.” Correct: waves can move small objects by transferring energy.

Limits and cautions:

  • Wave terms are simplified; wave-speed math is not needed.
  • Light reflection uses ray models; real scattering is simplified.
  • The signal comparison is broad; students should make and compare at least two signals and record accuracy data.
